# Chapter 8: White Sugar

The Hour of the Rat. The seventh day.

Seven people stepped out of the darkness and filled the main hall.

[Deathsworn · Twenty-Two]

[Skill: Sugar Refining (Grandmaster)]

[Deathsworn · Twenty-Three]

[Skill: Pastry (Grandmaster)]

[Deathsworn · Twenty-Four]

[Skill: Pottery (Grandmaster)]

[Deathsworn · Twenty-Five]

[Skill: Singing (Grandmaster)]

[Deathsworn · Twenty-Six]

[Skill: Blade Arts (Grandmaster)]

[Deathsworn · Twenty-Seven]

[Skill: Climbing (Grandmaster)]

[Deathsworn · Twenty-Eight]

[Skill: Beekeeping (Grandmaster)]

Shen You's eyes stayed on the first line for a long time.

Sugar Refining.

Week three was fully booked, so week four got a new surname: Chen One through Chen Seven. The sugar refiner was Chen One, forty-four or forty-five, gaunt, and he looked older than his years. His back was a little stooped, and under the nails of both hands was a brown that would never wash out.

"The Master wants sugar?" he asked.

"White sugar," Shen You said. "White as snow."

Chen One thought about it, then nodded. "Where there's black sugar, there's white sugar."

***

At daybreak, Zheng Three gave her report on the accounts.

"Zhou Two came back from the East Market side last night. Another nineteen strings. We have a little over nineteen strings in the house now."

"How much do we spend a day?"

"Twenty-eight people. Food alone, over five hundred coins a day."

Shen You nodded, then asked, "And on day thirty?"

Zheng Three paused, then lowered her head and started working the counting rods. As she went, her hands slowed.

"Day thirty: 465 people," Shen You said for her. "Food alone, over nine strings a day."

"Day one hundred: 5,050 people. A hundred strings a day."

"A year from now: more than sixty thousand people. Thirteen hundred strings a day."

The main hall went quiet all at once.

Zheng Three's counting rods hung in midair. For the first time, her round face, which never showed much of anything, looked a little stunned. Every one of the deathsworn knew there'd be more of them every day. None of them had ever worked it out like this.

Zhou Two was squatting on the threshold. The pebble between his fingers had stopped too.

"I've made the rounds of two houses in the East Market as well," he said. "Fan Da sent word yesterday. All the houses around the West Market are talking about some young fellow with droopy eyelids who wins every time he plays. Two houses already won't let new faces through the door."

"Keep it up, and every house in Chang'an will know your face," Shen You said. "And even if every last one let you in, could you win a hundred strings a day on your own?"

Zhou Two didn't say a word.

"Gambling can get you out of a pinch. It can't feed a household," Shen You said. "We need something we can keep selling. The more we sell, the better. And it has to be legal to sell to anyone."

He turned to Chen One. "How much capital for white sugar?"

"Black sugar," Chen One said. "The fruit sellers' row in the West Market has sand sugar shipped in from Shu. Dark brown, comes in lumps. Around a hundred and twenty coins a jin. Three jin of black makes a little over one jin of white."

"Buy thirty jin to start," Shen You told Zheng Three. "Earthenware pots, fine cloth, mutton bones—whatever he needs, buy it."

***

Chen One didn't ask for much: thirty jin of black sugar, a dozen-odd cone-bottomed earthenware pots with small holes in their tips, a bolt of fine hemp cloth, a basket of mutton bones, and two shoulder-pole loads of yellow mud.

Feng Three fetched the mutton bones from the butchers' row. There was no meat on them, so the butcher didn't charge. Feng One tossed the bones into his clay forge and burned them black through and through, then smashed them and ground them to a fine powder. In the back courtyard, Zheng Two built a filter barrel out of bamboo tubes, fine sand, and charcoal: a layer of sand, a layer of charcoal, a layer of cloth. Chen Three, the potter, took one look at the earthenware pots from the West Market, decided the holes had been bored crooked, and drilled every one of them over again with an awl.

The black sugar went into the pot and dissolved in water, and the whole courtyard filled with a scorched-sweet smell. A'xing squatted at the kitchen door, sniffing for all she was worth.

When the syrup came to a boil, Chen One scattered in the bone-char powder and stirred slowly with a long wooden ladle. Syrup that had been so dark it shone lost its color bit by bit and turned a cloudy brownish yellow.

"This step draws the color out," Chen One said.

The syrup was poured into Zheng Two's filter barrel. What ran out of the bottom was already clear and bright, the color of amber, like the finest honey.

Back into the pot it went, boiled down until it pulled into threads, then poured into the cone-bottomed pots. The holes at the bottom had been plugged with straw first, and each pot was set over the mouth of a vat.

"Now we wait for it to cool, wait for it to set." Chen One wiped away his sweat. "First thing tomorrow, the mud goes on."

***

That night, the moon was very full.

The fifteenth day of the eighth month.

Zhou Three had stewed a huge pot of mutton, and the smell of Sichuan pepper and ginger drifted all through the back courtyard. The jar of rice mash Feng Two had set the day before had just turned to sweet rice wine, and everyone got a bowl. Twenty-eight people, plus the three girls, crowded around the low tables outside the kitchen door and ate until sweat stood out on their foreheads.

After dinner, Feng Seven clapped her hands and pulled everyone out onto the open ground in the back courtyard, row by row, calling out the beat. Three steps left, three steps right, turn, and again. At first it was a mess. Someone trod on someone else's foot, someone turned the wrong way, and A'xing, watching from the veranda, laughed out loud. But after half a double-hour, the steps of twenty-odd people had gradually landed on a single beat.

Zhou Four watched from the side for a while, arms folded, then walked over and said a few words to Feng Seven.

When they went again, the formation had changed. Three ranks in front, three ranks behind, a gap left open down the middle, and the two wings curving forward to close around.

Shen You sat in a chair on the veranda with a bowl of sweet rice wine in his hands, watched those neat, orderly shadows under the moonlight, and said nothing.

Of the ten-day deadline, eight days were left.

A'na sat on the rim of the well with her pipa and played a very slow Hu melody. The moonlight fell on her curls and left a faint layer of silver.

"What is the Master thinking about?" Qingluo asked from behind him.

"Mooncakes."

"What are mooncakes?"

"A kind of cake. Round, with a filling inside, sweet. Where I come from, the custom is to eat them on the fifteenth of the eighth month."

Qingluo thought about it and shook her head. "Chang'an doesn't have cakes like that."

Chen Two, who was nearby collecting bowls, looked up. He was the young one, the pastry cook, and he had a bit of a stutter. "M-Master, the cake you mean—it has a filling? Sweet?"

"Mm."

"T-tomorrow, once there's sugar, I'll make some for the Master."

Shen You laughed, tipped his head back, and finished his bowl of sweet rice wine.

***

At the Hour of the Rat, eight more names went onto the roster. Shen You couldn't be bothered to look them over one by one anymore. He had Zhou One ask each of them what they could do and note it on the roster, then rolled over and went back to sleep.

The next morning, before it was fully light, Chen One was already hard at work in the back courtyard.

Overnight, the syrup in the earthenware pots had set into a layer of brownish-yellow sugar grit. He pulled the straw plugs from the bottoms of the pots, then ladled thin yellow mud slurry over the grit, one spoonful at a time.

The slurry slowly seeped downward. From the small holes in the bottoms of the pots, dark brown molasses began to drip, one drop after another, into the vats below.

Little by little, the sun climbed.

Everyone in the household was gathered in the back courtyard, watching those pots.

When it had seeped until midday, Chen One gently peeled away the topmost layer of dried mud.

Beneath the mud, it was white.

Not rice-white. Not grayish-white. White as snow. In the sunlight it glittered, every grain catching the light.

The back courtyard was silent for a while.

Chen One scraped off a small pinch with a sliver of bamboo, put it in a coarse earthenware dish, and held it out to Shen You.

Shen You picked up a little and put it in his mouth.

Sweet. Clean, pure sweetness, without the faintest trace of scorched bitterness, without any off taste at all. It melted on the tip of his tongue and stayed sweet all the way down his throat.

He narrowed his eyes and handed the dish to A'xing behind him. "Try it."

A'xing carefully took a pinch and put it in her mouth. Then her whole body went stiff.

"...Heaven above." She clapped a hand over her mouth, her eyes round as coins. "This... this is sugar?"

A'na tried a little too. She froze for a moment, then rattled off a long string of words. Zheng One translated. "She says it's like the stone honey from her homeland, but whiter than stone honey, and sweeter."

Qingluo was the last to taste it. She tasted it slowly, and when she was done, she lowered her hand and was quiet for a while.

"When the Prince of Qi's household entertained honored guests, it used stone honey sent as tribute from the Western Regions," she said. "Yellow shading into brown, in lumps that had to be scraped with a knife. The man who kept the household storerooms said that in the West Market, it went for two or three strings a jin, and you needed connections to get any."

She looked at the dish of snow-white sugar.

"This is whiter than the tribute."

Thirty jin of black sugar had made twelve jin of white.

Zheng Three squatted beside the vats, looking at those dozen-odd earthenware pots, and for a long time didn't touch her counting rods.

***

"Go and invite two people," Shen You said. "Kang Satuo and Dou the Eleventh."

"Together?" Zhou One asked.

"Together," Shen You said. "Same time. The Hour of the Monkey."

Zhou One paused. Then he understood, and the corner of his mouth twitched.

***

At the Hour of the Monkey, Dou the Eleventh arrived first.

He hadn't brought his falcon today, only Fan Da. The moment he stepped through the gate, his stride slowed.

The front courtyard, the veranda, beside the moon gate to the back courtyard—there were people everywhere. Some were splitting firewood, some were hauling vats, some were squatting on the ground sharpening knives, and from the back courtyard came the *clang, clang* of Feng One at his forge.

The Eleventh's gaze swept once around the courtyard. His lips moved, as if he were counting.

Halfway through, he gave up.

"Brother Shen." He walked up to the veranda and cupped his hands in salute, wearing the same lazy smile as ever. "Your household's grown another good stretch since the day before yesterday."

"Care to guess how many?"

"Fan Da can't guess, and neither can I." The Eleventh glanced at the arrow hole in the gatepost. "I did hear how that hole got there, though."

The words were barely out of his mouth when someone else came through the gate.

Green eyes, a curly beard, a Hu robe, and a string of glass beads hanging at his waist.

Kang Satuo.

The two men came face to face in the middle of the courtyard, and both of them stopped.

"Kang the Hu." The Eleventh spoke first, his smile deepening a shade.

"Young Master Dou." Kang Satuo cupped his hands. His green eyes made a circuit of the Eleventh's face, then moved on to Shen You's.

They'd done business in the West Market more than once. Half the glass cups in the Dou household had been bought from Kang's shop.

The two of them turned their heads at the same moment and looked at Shen You.

Both were clever men. One look at each other, and each understood: two men who'd each asked for "the first look" had been invited to the same courtyard, at the same hour.

The Eleventh was the first to laugh out loud. "Brother Shen, what's all this..."

"You both said that if I had something good, you wanted the first look." Shen You got to his feet. "I could hardly favor one of you over the other. Please, sit."

***

In the main hall, two folding stools had been set out, with a low table between them.

Qingluo brewed two bowls of plain tea. On the table sat a coarse earthenware dish holding a small heap of white sugar, like a handful of freshly fallen snow.

Kang Satuo's eyes landed on the dish and didn't move again.

"This is..."

"Sugar," Shen You said. "Have a taste, both of you."

Kang Satuo picked up a pinch, but didn't rush to put it in his mouth. First he held it up before his eyes and looked at it, then brought it under his nose and sniffed it, and only then set it on the tip of his tongue.

Slowly, his eyebrows climbed.

The Eleventh tasted quickly. When he was done, he said nothing, picked up the bowl of plain tea and took a sip, then took another pinch of sugar and put it in his mouth. This time he held it there a long while.

The main hall was very quiet. The only sound was the kettle burbling on the small brazier out on the veranda.

"Stone honey." Kang Satuo spoke at last, his voice lower than usual. "The best stone honey in the West Market comes by camel all the way from India and Persia. Over the Congling Mountains, across the deserts, and by the time it reaches Chang'an, it's two strings a jin. And it's still yellow."

He looked at the dish of sugar.

"This is white."

"Brother Shen." The Eleventh set down his tea bowl. "In a few days, the palace will hold the investiture of the empress. Every household in Chang'an is preparing gifts and arranging banquets. Whichever family can bring out a dish of this at its banquet—" he tapped the dish of sugar with a fingertip, "—that family will have face."

The two men exchanged a look.

"Young Master Shen," Kang Satuo said, "I want this. I know every Hu merchant in the West Market and every camel caravan heading west. I'll put down fifty strings up front."

"Eighty strings," the Eleventh said. "Within the city of Chang'an, the doors of the great and noble—I'll do the knocking."

Kang Satuo's green eyes narrowed. "A hundred strings."

"A hundred and twenty."

"A hundred and fif—"

"Stop bidding," Shen You said.

The two men fell silent at once.

"Keep bidding each other up, and I'm delighted," Shen You said with a smile. "But by the end, one of you will have made the trip for nothing, and the next time you run into each other, it'll be awkward."

He held up two fingers.

"The West Market, the Hu merchants, the caravans heading west—those go to the Kang house."

"The great households of Chang'an and the palace purveyors go to you, Brother Dou."

"Each of you pays a hundred strings as a deposit. From then on, I supply the sugar monthly. Of what it sells for, seven parts to me, three to each of you."

Kang Satuo didn't rush to agree. First he asked, "How much can you turn out in a month?"

"However much you two want, I'll make."

The main hall was silent for a moment.

Kang Satuo laughed first. He shook his head, laughing so hard his curly beard bobbed. "Twenty years I've been in the West Market, and this is the first time anyone's played me like this. And once he's finished playing me, I still have to thank him for it."

The Eleventh laughed too. He laughed so hard he rocked backward and nearly toppled off his folding stool. "Brother Shen, you called the two of us here together just for this?"

"You both wanted the first look." Shen You picked up his tea bowl. "I couldn't very well let one of you make the trip for nothing."

"Heading west..." It suddenly dawned on the Eleventh. He turned to look at Kang Satuo. "You're going to sell this stuff back to the Western Regions?"

Kang Satuo didn't answer. He only smiled.

For hundreds of years, men from the Western Regions had been selling stone honey to Chang'an. Now Chang'an's sugar was going to travel the other way, west.

***

Kang Satuo pulled two gold cakes from the front of his robe on the spot and pressed them down on the table, saying the balance would be waiting at Kang's shop tomorrow. The Eleventh didn't have that much cash on hand, so he sent Fan Da back for sixty strings' worth of silk and said he'd make up the remainder within three days.

By the time Shen You had seen the two of them off, the sun was already sinking westward.

Zheng Three squatted in the main hall, facing the two gold cakes on the table and the cartload of silk Fan Da had delivered outside the door, and worked her counting rods for a long time.

When she was finished, she looked up.

"Two hundred strings in deposits," she said. "Even if every house let Zhou Two in, he'd have to gamble for ten days to make that."

More important still, there would be more every month from here on. However many more people there were, they could simply boil more sugar.

Shen You nodded. As of today, money was more or less taken care of.

Chen Two came out of the kitchen carrying a flat bamboo tray. "M-Master," he stammered, "m-mooncakes."

On the tray sat a dozen-odd round cakes, baked golden, with patterns pressed into their tops.

Shen You picked one up and broke it open. Inside was jujube paste and sesame, mixed with white sugar, and the sweet fragrance rushed out all at once.

A mooncake a day late. The first mooncake of the Great Tang.

He took a bite and smiled.

***

That night, Zhou One came into the main hall looking somewhat grim.

"Master, when Kang Satuo was leaving, he caught hold of me at the gate and told me something."

"Told you what?"

"After the market opened at midday today, two men were in the West Market asking about the Master," Zhou One said. "Which temple the Master took his vows at, what his master's Dharma name was, when he came to the capital."

Shen You put down the mooncake in his hand.

"What kind of men?"

"Kang Satuo said they were in plain clothes." Zhou One paused. "But on their feet were official boots."
