# Setting Notes for Chapters 20–26

*A reader's guide to the background behind the chapters north of the Wei. So far this file covers Chapter 20; later chapters will be added as they are translated. Nothing here is needed to follow the story, but it explains why things happen the way they do. Spoilers through Chapter 20. For terms, see the Glossary; for a one-page overview of the whole book so far, see "Setting Notes for New Readers"; for the chapters just before these, see "Setting Notes for Chapters 17–19."*

## Where the story stands

At the end of Chapter 19 it is still Day 23, the first of the ninth month. Shen You has 276 people. A hundred and twenty of them are with him north of the Wei, camped in a dead-end gully north of the burned village of Willow Gully. Ahead of them is the tail of the Turkic host on its way home, with the treaty silk. Riders of the khan's guard have been seen on a ridge looking back south, so Chu One has switched the household to sleeping by day and moving by night.

## The calendar, and the count

- **Day 24 is the second of the ninth month.** The new day begins at the Hour of the Rat, so the summons at the start of Chapter 20, late on the night of the first, already belongs to Day 24: twenty-four people.
- **Running total: 300.** That is 24 × 25 ÷ 2.
- **Who came on Day 24.** The surnames run in order, seven to a surname. Peng Three arrived last on Day 23, so Day 24 brings the last four Pengs (Peng Four to Peng Seven: horseshoeing, curing hides, rope making and barbering), then seven Langs and seven Lus, then the Weis. **Lu Three** climbs cliffs. The other skills aren't named in the chapter.
- **Why there is no Wei Three.** "Wei Three's already taken," says Shen You. Wei San, the Crown Prince's old squad leader who tried to steal the black lacquer box (Chapters 6, 9 and 12), was sent home with a jar of white sugar, and in Chinese his name is the same as "Wei Three." So the household's numbering skips it.
- **No moon.** The second of a lunar month is still dark, as on the first.

## Chapter 20: The Hidden Valley

**A summons with nobody watching.** No one, not even the household's own people, sees the new deathsworn appear. Ge Four and Shui One pitch the white felt tent from Kaiming Ward at the north end of the gully: one *zhang* six *chi* across, four layers of felt. Shen You sits inside alone. Zhou Four and three men stand ten paces off with their backs to it, and Ashina and Feng Five are up on the rim, facing north. Only when the tent is full does Zhou Four call Zheng One over to open the flap. Ashina isn't a deathsworn, and the summons is kept from him. When he notices on the march that the column has grown, Zheng One tells him the extra people are "the Kang family's second batch," who followed the camel bells in the dark.

**The roster, without Zhou One.** At home, Zhou One questions each newcomer and writes everything down. He has stayed in Chang'an to cover for the Master's "chill," so Shen You reads the names and skills off the golden characters and Zheng One, the interpreter, writes them on a scrap of sheepskin with a stick of charcoal. It takes less time than a cup of tea. But after the names, Zheng One's notes are his own mix of Sogdian letters, Turkic notches and little pictures (a hoof for the horseshoer, a beehive for the climber), and nobody else can read them. Shen You, the former programmer, calls it a single point of failure. The second half of the thought is the one he keeps: a record only your own people can read isn't all bad.

**Hide robes and boots.** The household packed a hundred and thirty sets before it left Chang'an: one for each of the people due to be summoned on the second to the sixth of the month (24 + 25 + 26 + 27 + 28). From this night on, the pile gets shorter every night.

**The night march.** At the Hour of the Ox the column climbs out of the gully and heads north across the tableland: 120 people, 20 camels and ninety-odd horses, with the 24 new people walking behind, holding on to the camels' lead ropes. There is no light at all. Yun Five, the star reader, steers by the empty patch of sky at the pole (Chapter 19). At first light Feng Five leads the column down off the tableland and east into a dry gully, with a great ridge black against the sky to the east. He and two men drag bundles of wild jujube branches behind them to sweep away the tracks where the column turned off the road.

**The Hidden Valley.** The valley mouth is a gap between two cliffs, no more than three *zhang* wide. Forty-odd paces in, it bends north; a hundred-odd paces farther it bends again, and opens onto thirty or forty *mu* of level ground with a stream, oaks, aspens and smoke trees. At the far end the slope is forest: a man can climb it, a horse can't. There is only one way in. Chu One picks it for the same reason the household camped in the dead-end gully in Chapter 19: travelers don't go into a place with no way through. A man on the north cliff can see twenty *li*, so Lu Three, the new cliff climber, goes up it to keep watch.

**Too clean.** Late in the Hour of the Dragon, Lu Three signals from the cliff: five riders coming down the gully, riding slowly and looking at the ground. They are looking at the stretch Feng Five swept. On a road covered in hoofprints, one stretch with none at all stands out "like a face with one patch where the beard won't grow." Shen You's only comment is "Next time, sweep it dirtier." The household can't hide anyway: ninety-odd horses leave too much dung.

**The trap.** Chu One's plan is to let them in and not let them out.

- At the mouth: a pack rope as thick as a thumb buried in the dust, each end wrapped round a boulder, with Feng Seven and six of her people lying behind the boulders, three at each end. Seven camels trample the mouth to cover the horses' tracks.
- At the first bend: the seven camels kneeling with their packs off, and Ge Four's second fire, fed with wet wood so it smokes. A merchant train resting with no smoke would look wrong. Ashina, Zheng One and three drivers sit around it. Ashina, with his robe open, looks like a Turk hired to drive a Hu merchant's camels.
- Wei One and Chu Four are behind a stack of packs just inside the mouth, Chen Five flat against the south cliff, Zheng Six and two archers in the oak scrub on the north slope, and Feng Six on the south slope with his bronze-toothed crossbow.
- Everyone else, with the ninety-odd horses and thirteen camels, is behind the second bend, where Cao One hums the herd quiet.

The riders come in to collect a toll, and as Ashina explained in Chapter 19, men out raiding on their own take what they can on the way home. Ashina haggles; the leader asks for one camel. While they bargain, the leader reads the ground: more churned hoofprints and more steaming dung than seven camels could make. He agrees to the price, whistles, and all five wheel for the mouth. Feng Seven shouts, and the rope snaps up to the height of a horse's knees.

**How it ends.** One dead, four taken alive. Wei One throws one rider; Chu Four's staff takes another at the knee; Chen Five, as always when he wants a man alive, uses the back of his blade and the hilt, never the edge. Zheng Six shoots a horse, not its rider. The horse falls and crushes the rider, who is the one man killed. The leader's arrow grazes one of the household's archers, and Feng Six's bolt drops his horse just below the rock where Shen You is sitting. Zhou Four steps in front of Shen You and never has to move again: Ashina is ten paces behind the leader with a drawn bow, and a second bow creaks on the north slope. The leader drops his bow. By Zhou One's conditions, Shen You stays out of the fight and gives no orders. He watches it all with a cold parched-grain cake halfway to his mouth, and the water in Ge Four's pot hasn't boiled.

**Tuli's brand, for real.** Turkic horses carry their owner's brand on the haunch. In Chapter 16, Feng Five saw Tuli's (a hook with two short bars under it) on horses north of the Wei and drew it on a brick, and Zhou One cut a bronze seal from the drawing. Ashina carries the seal with the golden arrow. Laid beside the brand on the dead horse, it is a perfect match: the hook curls inward by half a *fen*, the left bar is shorter than the right. All five horses carry it. "The lesser khan's horses," says Ashina. "Real." A *fen* is a tenth of a *cun*, about an eighth of an inch.

**What the prisoners say.** The five are Tuli's men. They left the lesser khan's camp at first light, telling the sentries they were hunting for strayed horses. Jieli's main camp is on the river plain twenty-five *li* north, and it has stopped moving. The treasure carts stand in a ring at its heart, guarded by the *fuli*. **At dawn on the fifth the khan will divide the silk**, and then every tribe goes home to its own pastures. Tuli's people put up the most horses and lost the most men, and "a hundred men don't get one cartload," so they rode south again for the villages that weren't burned yet. The leader, tied to a tree all day and hearing nothing but Guanzhong accents around him, works out who the household must be: "What you're after. It's before the fifth." The household can neither let the prisoners go nor take them along. Chu One sends four men with them into the woods. The chapter says only that the woods soon went quiet.

**Only one night.** At dusk Chu One draws the camp in the dirt. Ashina adds that the khan's tent stands north of the treasure carts and the horses are tied downwind, because the khan's people always pitch camp the same way. Three or four hundred *fuli* ride around the camp. Two things limit the plan:

1. **The henbane.** Zhou Six has seven *jin* of ground henbane seed, wrapped in oiled paper. Henbane is a real plant of the nightshade family, long known to Chinese medicine; the book treats the smoke of its burning seed as a knockout drug. Lit with the wind behind it, it will drop one ring of sentries, and then it's gone. More would have to be bought in Chang'an, where the apothecaries were stripped bare under martial law.
2. **The lead mare.** Cao One's gray mare is the lead mare of a herd somewhere in Jieli's herds (Chapters 14 and 16). Her first call will bring the herd to her. The Turks will hear it too, and at the second call they'll know which horse is calling and where. "Then it's one arrow."

So: one go with the drug, one call from the mare. The silk is divided at dawn on the fifth, so it has to be the night of the fourth.

**Duoxi Wulue.** The chapter ends inside the khan's camp, with the man who guards the treasure. Duoxi Wulue is an *irkin* (see the Glossary) of Jieli's own clan, past fifty. Every day before dark he counts the carts, eighty-two of silk and six of gold and silver vessels, with a palm on each cart's shaft. He doesn't trust the treaty, but he trusts the silk: people who pay that much are afraid, and won't cross the Wei to take it back. Word comes that five of the lesser khan's men rode south at first light and haven't returned. He doesn't think of the Tang at all. He thinks of Tuli, who camps ten-odd *li* off at the east end of the plain, whose men have been riding around behind the baggage, and who has complained the whole way about his share. So from that night he orders:

- another line of trip ropes outside the camp, and another around the treasure carts;
- twice as many roving sentries, and at first light a party sent south to find the five ("If not the men, then the horses will do"), but nobody sent out in the dark, because reshuffling the southern sentries at night would alarm the lesser khan's men first;
- the lesser khan's men to hang up their bows at the camp gate when they come in;
- nobody's men, Tuli's included, within thirty paces of the treasure carts until the silk is divided.

He thinks of everything. He just has the wrong enemy in mind.

**Time of day.** The summons is at the third watch, the Hour of the Rat. The column moves out at the Hour of the Ox (1–3 a.m.) and enters the valley in the middle of the Hour of the Rabbit (around 6 a.m.). The riders are sighted late in the Hour of the Dragon (towards 9 a.m.). The prisoners are questioned in the Hour of the Goat (1–3 p.m.), and Chu One draws the camp early in the Hour of the Rooster (soon after 5 p.m.), the same hour Duoxi Wulue counts his carts.
