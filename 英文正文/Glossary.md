# Glossary (Chapters 1–16)

*For readers who want the background. Nothing here is needed to follow the story. Light spoilers through Chapter 16.*

## The system

- **Deathsworn** (singular and plural): the absolutely loyal people the Host summons, each with one grandmaster-level skill.
- **Summons**: on Day N, N deathsworn appear. The running total after N days is N(N+1)/2. Unused summons expire at the end of the day.

| Day | 1 | 3 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 22 | 30 | 40 | 100 | 365 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total | 1 | 6 | 55 | 120 | 210 | 253 | 465 | 820 | 5,050 | 66,795 |

- **Days and dates**: Day 1 is the ninth day of the eighth month, so Day N falls on day N + 8 of that month. Because the new day starts at the Hour of the Rat, a summons late at night already belongs to the next day: the one on the night of the twenty-eighth is Day 21. Chapter 16 ends on Day 22, the thirtieth.
- **Naming**: deathsworn take a surname from the *Hundred Family Surnames* plus a number, seven to a surname. To Tang ears these sound like ordinary birth-order names. To a modern Chinese ear, Zhou One through Zhou Seven are Monday through Sunday.
- **Sanniang**: "Third Daughter." A common way to address a woman who is third in her family.
- **Square Dancing**: Feng Seven's skill, rolled in Chapter 7. It translates the Chinese *guangchang wu*, "public-square dancing": the group exercise-dancing that crowds of mostly middle-aged and retired women do in plazas and parks across modern China, every morning and evening, moving as one to a leader's count and a loudspeaker. It has nothing to do with the American square dance. The "square" is a public square. The joke in Chapter 7 is a modern one. Shen You's second thought is not: a woman who can make a crowd move to one beat has a skill with another name. Drilling troops.

## Time

- **Wude** (618–626): the reign era of the Tang's first emperor. The ninth year of Wude is 626 CE. By the Western calendar, the ninth day of the eighth month falls in early September. The next era, **Zhenguan**, begins in 627.
- **Double-hour**: a two-hour unit. **The Hour of the Rat** is 11 p.m. to 1 a.m., and in the old reckoning it is where the new day begins. It is also the **third watch**. **The Hour of the Horse** is 11 a.m. to 1 p.m. The text sometimes says "the start", "the middle" or "the end" of a double-hour.
- **Watches**: the night is divided into five, each the length of a double-hour. The **second watch** is roughly 9 to 11 p.m., when the book's night work usually happens. The **fourth watch** is roughly 1 to 3 a.m. The **fifth watch** is roughly 3 to 5 a.m., when court convenes.
- **The twelve double-hours**: each is named for an animal of the zodiac.

| Rat | Ox | Tiger | Rabbit | Dragon | Snake |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11 p.m.–1 a.m. | 1–3 a.m. | 3–5 a.m. | 5–7 a.m. | 7–9 a.m. | 9–11 a.m. |

| Horse | Goat | Monkey | Rooster | Dog | Pig |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11 a.m.–1 p.m. | 1–3 p.m. | 3–5 p.m. | 5–7 p.m. | 7–9 p.m. | 9–11 p.m. |

## Money and measures

- **Coin**: one bronze Kaiyuan Tongbao coin, first cast in 621. The name means "inaugurating a new era" and has nothing to do with the later Kaiyuan reign.
- **String**: 1,000 coins on a cord. For scale: a sesame flatbread costs 8 to 10 coins, and a fire steel costs 1.
- **Bolt of silk**: used as money, and paid out by the tens of thousands when the court needs to buy peace.
- **Tael** (*liang*): the unit of weight for gold and silver.
- **Shi, dou, sheng**: measures of grain by volume. Ten *sheng* make a *dou*, and ten *dou* make a *shi*. A person eats about two *sheng* a day. The book never converts them into Western units.
- **Jin**: a unit of weight, used for things like sugar. **Chi** and **zhang** are units of length; ten *chi* make a *zhang*. **Li** is a distance, about a third of a mile.

## Chang'an

- **Ward** (*fang*): a walled residential block whose gates are locked from dusk to dawn. Chang'an had 108 of them. Being on the avenues after the gates close breaks curfew. The **ward warden** is the official who holds the gate keys.
- **East Market and West Market**: the only two commercial districts in the city. The West Market opens at the Hour of the Horse.
- **Imperial City and Palace City**: the government offices, and the palace itself, in the north of the city. **Anfu Gate** is the Imperial City's west gate; **Hanguang Gate** is the westernmost of its south gates.
- **Kaiyuan Gate**: the northern gate in the city's west wall, the road out toward the Wei bridges.
- **Eastern Palace**: the crown prince's residence. After the Incident, Li Shimin was named crown prince and took the throne there. His father, now the **Retired Emperor** (Li Yuan, Emperor Gaozu), stayed in **Taiji Palace**.
- **County**: Chang'an was split into two counties along Vermilion Bird Avenue. Kaiming Ward belongs to Wannian County. **Constables** are the county's detectives and enforcers. **Patrolmen** guard the streets and gates.
- **Human and Horse Market** (*kouma hang*): the row in the West Market where people and horses were sold. *Kou*, "mouths," was the official word for heads of people. Under Tang law, slaves were a base class and counted as their master's property. They could be bought, sold, given away or inherited. Every sale had to be recorded in a deed drawn up at the Market Office, and a master could free a slave with a letter of manumission. The maids bought there, like the household slaves of great families, held this legal status.
- **Great Granary**: the state granary in the capital, which fed the army in wartime.

## Around Chang'an

- **Guanzhong**: "within the passes," the Wei River plain around Chang'an and the Tang heartland.
- **Wei River**: runs west to east just north of the capital. The **Middle Wei Bridge** crosses it due north of the city; the **Bian Bridge** crosses it to the northwest. Between the city and the river lies the **Forbidden Park**, the imperial hunting grounds, and the ruins of the **Han dynasty's Chang'an**.
- **Jingyang**: a county north of the Wei, on the Jing River. **Jingzhou** is a frontier prefecture farther northwest, where the Turks first struck. **Wugong** is a county west of the capital; **Weinan** is to the east.
- **Liangzhou**: a prefecture far to the west, on the road to the Western Regions.
- **Gaochang** and **Kucha**: oasis kingdoms on the Silk Road, whose merchants trade in Chang'an.

## History

- **Xuanwu Gate Incident** (summer 626): Li Shimin ambushed and killed his elder brother, the Crown Prince, and his younger brother, the Prince of Qi, then forced his father to step aside. He took the throne on the ninth day of the eighth month.
- **The general amnesty**: proclaimed with the accession, on the ninth day of the eighth month. Its standard formula pardons "all crimes, grave or minor, whether already discovered or not yet discovered."
- **The investiture of the empress**: Li Shimin's wife, of the Zhangsun clan, was made empress on the twenty-first day of the eighth month.
- **The Turks**: the Eastern Turkic Khaganate, steppe nomads north of China. Not modern Turkey. **Jieli Khan** is their supreme ruler.
- **The Turkic invasion of 626**: within weeks of the accession, Jieli and Tuli brought more than a hundred thousand horsemen south. They raided Jingzhou, and their vanguard passed Wugong. **Yuchi Jingde** beat them at **Jingyang** and captured one of their great generals, but the khan's main host still reached the north bank of the Wei. The emperor rode out to the river with only six riders to face him across it. A treaty followed at the **Bian Bridge**, sealed by sacrificing a white horse, and paid for out of the Tang treasury.
- **The culling of monks and nuns**: a decree of spring 626 sending most monks and nuns back to lay life and tearing down many temples. It was rescinded after the Incident.
- **Militia** (*fubing*): registered farmer-soldiers who served in rotation, the backbone of the early Tang army.
- **Wei Zheng**: the minister famous for scolding the emperor to his face.
- **Zhangsun family**: the clan of Li Shimin's wife and her brother Zhangsun Wuji, one of the Incident's key plotters. **Zhangsun Shunde**, Duke of Xue, is the empress's clan uncle, one of the original followers from the rising at Taiyuan, and was at the Xuanwu Gate.
- **Dou family**: Li Shimin's mother's clan.
- **Early Tang wars**: Taiyuan (617), where the Li family rose against the Sui; Huoyi (617), where Song Laosheng, a Sui general, was beaten; Qianshuiyuan (618), Li Shimin's one great defeat.
- **Wagang**: a great rebel army of the last Sui years. Several famous Tang generals, Cheng Zhijie among them, started out there.
- **Yuchi Jingde** is also known as Yuchi Gong. The English text always calls him Yuchi Jingde.
- **Cheng Zhijie** and **Yaojin** are the same man. Cheng Zhijie is the name he goes by at court, and the one the narration uses. Yaojin ("Bite Gold") is his old name, and centuries of storytellers, novels and operas made "Cheng Yaojin" the name every modern Chinese reader knows him by. That is the joke in Chapter 15: Shen You blurts out the name everybody knows from the stories, while some of the general's own young riders have never heard it. In Chapter 1, Shen You's mental list of famous men gives him as Cheng Yaojin, the way a modern reader would.

## Offices and ranks

- **The guard armies**: the standing armies stationed in the capital, each with a name such as the **Left Encampment Guard** (Cui Shouli's and Yuwen Yan's) or the **Left Valiant Guard**. From the top: **Grand General**, **General**, **Senior Colonel** (*zhonglangjiang*, lower fourth rank), **Colonel** (*langjiang*, upper fifth rank). A colonel serves under a senior colonel.
- **Duke** (*guogong*): a high title of nobility, given with a place name: Zhangsun Shunde is the **Duke of Xue**.
- **Chancellor**: one of the chief ministers of state. "Chancellor Pei" was the Retired Emperor's right-hand man; Pei Shisan is his grandnephew.
- **County Commandant** (*xianwei*): the county official in charge of arrests, prisons and criminal cases. Commandant Lu of Wannian County hears the charge against Shen You.
- **Chief constable**: head of the county's constables. Qian Biao holds the post in Wannian County.
- **Militia garrison** (*zhechongfu*): a local headquarters of the militia. Its commander is the **Garrison Commandant**; his second is the **Deputy Garrison Commandant**, like Deputy Commandant Liang.
- **Granary Officer of the Field Army Headquarters**: the officer who sees to an army's grain on campaign, like Granary Officer Dugu.
- **Censor**: an official whose job is to impeach other officials. One memorial from a censor can ruin a household.
- **Classics examination** (*mingjing*): one of the civil-service examinations. Commandant Lu came up through it; Liu Qi failed it.
- **Crown Prince's Librarian** (*taizi xima*): an official of the Eastern Palace in charge of the crown prince's books and records, lower fifth rank. The title reads literally as "horse washer," but it comes from an older word for the outriders who went ahead of the prince's horse.
- **Palace Food Service**: the palace office that prepares the imperial table.

## Law and paperwork

- **Writ**: an official order from a county, prefecture or guard, needed to search a home or make an arrest.
- **Private ordination**: becoming a monk without government permission. The general amnesty pardons it, which matters to a man whose papers say "monk returned to lay life."
- **Entering a home at night**: Tang law gave forty strokes of the light cane to anyone who entered another's home at night without cause, and no charge to a householder who killed him on the spot.
- **Letter of manumission**: the document by which a master freed a slave, signed with a mark and a thumbprint.
- **Martial law**: declared in the capital when the Turks cross the border.
- **Hiring** (*hegu*): in wartime the government hired carts, oxen and laborers from ordinary people, and wrote each owner's name in the county's ledger.

## The Turks

- **Khan**: the Turkic ruler's title. Historians often write *qaghan*, and the Turks of this era are sometimes called the Göktürks; the book simply says khan and the Turks. **The khan's camp** is his royal encampment.
- **Tuli Khan**, "the lesser khan": Jieli's nephew, who ruled the eastern tribes under him. Their men camp apart.
- **Shad** and **tegin**: Turkic titles. A shad commands a wing of the army or a group of tribes; a tegin is a prince of the ruling house.
- ***Fuli***: "wolf." The khan's own guard.
- **Ashide**: a leading Turkic clan. A great general of that name is captured at Jingyang.
- **Zhishi Sili**: one of Jieli's trusted men, sent into Chang'an to see how strong the Tang really is.

## People and peoples

- **Hu**: the Tang's catch-all term for Central Asian and other western foreigners. **Sogdians** were the great merchant people of the Silk Road, around Samarkand.
- **Fulin**: the Tang name for the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire. Its 24-letter alphabet is Greek.

## Forms of address

- **Master / the Master**: how deathsworn and maids address Shen You. Said in the third person, it is respect rather than distance.
- **young master**: lowercase, a stranger's polite term for a well-dressed young man.
- **Young Master Shen**: used once the speaker knows his surname.
- **Little Master**: a polite address to a young monk. **Old Master** is how the ward speaks of an old monk.
- **Elder**: a respectful address to an old man.
- **Manager** (*guanshi*): runs a business for its owner. A **steward** runs a household. They are different jobs.
- **Dou the Eleventh**: Tang men were often named by surname plus birth order, counted across all male cousins of a generation. "Dou the Eleventh" is the eleventh son of the clan's generation. A bare surname-plus-number such as **Fan Da** or **Wang Da** is a plain name.
- **Erlang**: "Second Son." The Meng family's younger boy is called this, and so, privately, is Li Shimin by his wife: he was his father's second son.
- **His Majesty / Your Majesty**: how the emperor is spoken of, and spoken to.

## Things

- **Futou**: the soft black cloth headwrap with two trailing tails that Tang men wore.
- **Travel pass** (*guosuo*): the document needed to travel between districts. **Household register**: every subject was entered in the county registers. Someone with no entry had no standing.
- **Chupu**: a dice-and-pieces gambling game. Five pieces are thrown. All five black is *lu*, the best throw. *Zhi* is the second best.
- **Nine-Two, Nine-Three, Nine-Five**: positions in the *Book of Changes*. Nine-Five is the sovereign's position. The Sui planners left that ridge to temples so no subject would live "above" the emperor.
- **Crimson and green robes**: official ranks. Crimson was for middle and upper ranks, green for lower. Purple was for the highest.
- **Halberds arrayed before a gate**: a privilege of officials of the third rank and above.
- **Snow Sugar**: the name the emperor gives Shen You's white sugar after the empress says it is white as snow. The lighter, presented at the same banquet as a treasure from Fulin, gets called **Fulin Fire**.
- **Shaochun**: a strong spirit, brewed in Shen You's house.
- ***Hengdao***: the straight Tang single-edged sword.
- ***Mingguang* armor**: Tang plate armor, named for the round, polished **mirror plates** on the chest.
- **Lance**: the long cavalry spear of Tang and Turkic horsemen alike. **Horn bows** and **whistling arrows** are the Turkic rider's weapons.
