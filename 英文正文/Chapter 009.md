# Chapter 9: Casting the Net

Early on the morning of the ninth day, Shen You called everyone out to the front courtyard.

Thirty-six people filled the courtyard, with more crowded under the eaves of the veranda. Another nine, who had arrived at the Hour of the Rat, had already been questioned one by one by Zhou One about their skills and entered in the roster. Right now they were eating breakfast in the back courtyard.

Shen You didn't stand. He sat in the chair with the backrest that Zhou Five had made, holding a bowl of plain tea Qingluo had just brewed.

"From here on, there are only going to be more of us," he said. "If you all just orbit around me, this courtyard won't hold you, and you'll be bored out of your minds."

"Zhou One, you're in charge of the roster. Zheng Three, you're in charge of the money."

"Zhou Four, pick fifteen who can fight and keep them here at the house. The house is yours. So is my life."

Zhou Four nodded and said nothing.

"Everyone else goes out."

The courtyard went quiet for a moment.

Shen You took a sip of tea and went down the line, one by one, not fast, one job per sentence.

"Chen One, go rent a big compound in Baoning Ward and open a sugar works. Starting today, Dou the Eleventh and Kang Satuo get their sugar supplied monthly. However much they want, we turn out. If you're short of hands, pick from the roster."

"Feng Two, go with him. Put the winery right next door to the sugar works. First brew some good wine, then distill me something strong. The kind that drops a man with one swallow. Feng One will forge your pot, and Chen Three will pick out your steamers."

"Chen Two, go open a pastry shop in the East Market. Borrow the storefront from Dou the Eleventh. He mentioned yesterday that his family has an empty shop there. Pastries made with white sugar—mooncakes, sugar cakes, sweet milk curds. The finer, the better. That's going to be our household's public face."

"Feng Four, you're a storyteller. Go tell stories in the taverns of both markets, and at the popular sermons in the temples. Wherever there are the most people, that's where you go. Tell whatever you like. Just keep your ears open for me."

"Feng Five, Zhou Seven, Chen Six, no sleeping at night for you three. Qian Biao, the Pei family, and whoever it was that left the knife—watch them, one house at a time, and don't let them out of your sight."

"Zheng One, ride with Kang Satuo's camel caravans. How the roads west run, how the Hu merchants do business, what's stirring among the Turks—learn it all and bring it back."

"Zhou Five, Zheng Two, Chen Three." He set down his tea bowl and looked once around the house. "Make this place look like people actually live here."

"What did the Master have in mind?" Zhou Five asked.

Shen You thought about it.

"I want to be comfortable lying down, comfortable soaking, comfortable sleeping," he said. "Money's no object."

***

Over the next three days, the people of Kaiming Ward watched with their own eyes as the haunted house in the northeast corner changed from one day to the next.

On the first day, Zheng Two led a crew that tore down the old screen wall in the front courtyard, already half collapsed, and set fire to the weeds in the back courtyard, burning them down to a great black stretch of earth. He the broker was invited back once more, and in one go the house bought up the two mu of vegetable plots beyond its eastern wall, along with an empty, run-down compound next door. When He the broker left, he was bowing lower than when he'd come.

On the second day, cartload after cartload of goods went in. Gray bricks, lime, elm, nanmu wood, and whole bolts of figured silk and gauze. The Hu boy from Kang's shop in the West Market drove a donkey cart over twice, its load covered with oilcloth so no one could see what it was.

Early on the third day, a fragrance began drifting out of the house. Not cooking. It was something hard to put a name to, a little like a temple, and yet not like a temple.

Sister Liu hung over the top of the wall peering in for a good long while, then went back and told everyone, "They've planted bamboo in that courtyard. And they moved in an old pomegranate tree, with the fruit already red on it."

The whole ward was talking.

"A monk who's gone back to lay life—where does he get that kind of money?"

"Haven't you heard? There's a new pastry shop in the East Market called Shen's, selling sugar cakes as white as snow. The Dou family's carriage goes to haul goods from it every day. The line runs from the shop door all the way to the end of the street, and carriages from quite a few great households pull up for it every day..."

"Over in the West Market there's a storyteller too, and he's something else. The moment he opens his mouth, the whole tavern forgets to drink..."

"Did you all hear it at night? Somebody in that courtyard was singing."

The Hour of the Rat didn't stop during those three days, either. Nine on the ninth day, ten on the tenth, eleven on the eleventh.

The roster grew longer by the day. Someone who could raise horses, someone who could weave brocade, someone who could paint, someone who could read accounts, someone who could fight with a sword, someone who could set bones. And there was one whose skill read "Landscape Gardening." On the very day he arrived, he squatted down on that burned-black ground in the back courtyard and spent the whole afternoon drawing in the dirt with a stick.

Sixty-six people.

Only twenty-some stayed at the house. The rest seeped into the city of Chang'an like water.

***

On the morning of the eleventh day, Shen You was woken by a ray of light.

It came in through the window and passed first through a layer of moon-white gauze bed curtains. The gauze was so thin it seemed to weigh nothing at all. Zheng Four said it was called *qingrong*, and that a single bolt cost several strings. The sunlight that came through it broke into a soft, gentle white that fell across his face without the least glare.

Beneath him was a broad elm bed, enclosed on three sides by low screens carved with scrolling vines, every stroke of them cut by Zhou Five's knife. The mattress was padded with silk floss, thick and fluffy; when he lay down, his whole body sank half an inch, like lying on a cloud. The coverlet was figured silk Zheng Four had got by trading away the plain silk Dou the Eleventh had sent over. Smooth and cool, it lay against his skin like water.

There had originally been a porcelain pillow beside the bed, one Chen Three had picked out at the potters' row. It was celadon-glazed, cool and slick to the touch, and the most fashionable style in the homes of Chang'an's great families. Shen You had slept on it for one night and nearly cricked his neck. The next day he'd had Zheng Four sew him a soft pillow stuffed with buckwheat hulls and silk floss.

He lay in bed and stretched lazily. Outside, someone was sweeping the courtyard, the bamboo broom going *shhh, shhh*.

"Is the Master awake?"

Qingluo's voice, from the other side of the gauze curtains.

She lifted the curtain and hung it on its copper hook. In her hands was a lacquer tray holding a bowl of warm water, a little dish of green salt, a hog-bristle toothbrush Zhou Five had made, and a white cloth folded into a neat square.

She had changed clothes today. Zheng Four had made two new outfits for each of the three girls. Qingluo's was a skirt of pale jade-green figured silk tied high at the chest, with a moon-white half-sleeved jacket over it, and a pale blue-green shawl draped across her shoulders whose two ends floated lightly as she walked. Her hair had been redone too, swept up into a high chignon and held with a newly made silver hairpin topped with a tiny plum blossom.

She stood in the morning light, her features faint and delicate, like a fine-brush painting that had only just dried.

Shen You looked at her once. Then he looked again.

"Beautiful."

The tips of Qingluo's ears turned faintly red. She lowered her head to wring out the cloth and didn't answer.

***

When he'd washed up, he got out of bed barefoot.

Under his feet was a Persian rug.

A gift from Kang Satuo—so he called it. Shen You had told Zheng Three to take it out of the sugar money, but the Hu merchant flatly refused to put a price on it, saying only, "The young master's room deserves this." The rug had a deep red ground woven with ring after ring of pearl-roundel patterns, and inside each roundel lay a pair of deer gazing at each other. The wool was thick and dense; when his foot came down, it sank in softly, and even the spaces between his toes felt warm.

The sleeping hall was three bays wide, and Zhou Five had redone every one of them.

The east room was the bedroom. The west room was the study: by the window stood a tall table and two tall chairs, and on the table were Qingluo's brushes, ink, paper, and inkstone. Along the wall stood a row of bookshelves, still empty, with no time yet to fill them. In the middle room, facing the door, stood a six-panel folding screen. On its silk, Qingluo had copied out the *Preface to the Orchid Pavilion* stroke by stroke in small regular script, the ink clear and lustrous, line after line of it like flowing water.

In front of the screen sat a bronze *boshan* censer, its lid shaped like peak upon layered peak. The incense Zheng Seven had blended drifted slowly out through the gaps between the mountains, wisp after wisp: agarwood, cloves, and a touch of something sweet he couldn't name.

Shen You breathed in deeply, and his whole body went slack.

***

The bath room was next to the kitchen.

He pushed open the door, and a wave of steam hit him in the face.

The old tub hooped together from door planks was gone. Built into the floor was a square pool, eight chi on each side and waist-deep, its walls faced with celadon-glazed tiles Chen Three had picked out at the kilns. The glaze was bright and glossy, slick under the hand. Steam rose from the water, which was clear all the way to the bottom.

Behind the wall stood a stove, and on the stove sat a copper cauldron Feng One had forged. When the water in it boiled, it ran down a length of clay pipe into the pool. In the bottom of the pool was a separate outlet; water that had gone cool drained out through it and along a covered channel Zheng Two had dug, all the way to the vegetable plots in the back courtyard.

Even better was the floor. Beneath the gray bricks of the bath room, Zheng Two had laid several flues. The smoke and heat from the stove passed under the floor, so the whole floor was warm. Bare feet on it felt toasty, the warmth spreading from the soles all the way up to the calves.

"Master, try the water." A'xing came running in with a wooden bucket and set it down by the edge of the pool. In it were the bath beans Zheng Seven had just made and a stack of folded white cloths. Today she wore a goose-yellow short jacket with a long pomegranate-red skirt, and her two little topknots had new red cords. Her round face was rosy, like a sugar cake fresh from the oven. "Sanniang says it's been heating for a whole double-hour. Not too hot, not too cold, just right!"

Shen You dipped a foot in.

The water was warm, just a touch above body temperature. Just right.

He sank all the way in, right up to his chin, and let out a long, long breath.

The hot water closed around him from every side, holding him up, soaking into him. Every bone in his body seemed to be melting, little by little. A few dried osmanthus blossoms Zheng Seven had tossed in floated on the surface, and as the steam rose through them, their fragrance filled the room.

He closed his eyes.

In his past life, he'd worked overtime until three in the morning on the thirtieth floor of an office tower, gone home to a rental at thirty-two hundred a month, and taken a hot shower whose water pressure came and went. That had counted as the most comfortable moment of his day.

Now he was soaking in a hot pool in the Great Tang, warm underfoot, fragrance in his nose, and outside there were people brewing his tea, cooking his meals, and playing the pipa for him.

He soaked for a good half double-hour, until the tips of his fingers wrinkled.

***

Breakfast was laid out under the pomegranate tree in the back courtyard.

The old pomegranate had been moved here from an abandoned temple in the south of the city by the landscape gardener among the deathsworn. Its trunk was as thick as the mouth of a bowl, and its branches hung heavy with fruit, red to the point of shining; a few had already split open, showing the jewel-like seeds packed inside. A bamboo mat had been spread beneath the tree with a low table on it, and on the table: a bowl of Zhou Three's mutton soup noodles, the broth milky white and sprinkled with scallions and Sichuan pepper; a dish of Chen Two's steamed sugared milk curd, snow-white and silky, trembling; two sesame flatbreads fresh from the oven, glazed with syrup and baked a glossy gold; and a pot of Feng Two's sweet rice wine, sending up a cool breath.

Under the eaves, a rocking chair Zhou Five had just made was already in place. It had curved elm runners and a seat and back of woven rattan; once you sat down and leaned back a little, the chair began rocking slowly back and forth on its own. It was the first time Zhou Five had ever made such a thing. When he'd finished, he'd sat in it himself first and rocked for ages, his old face cracking into a grin.

When Shen You had finished breakfast, he lay back in the rocking chair. Back and forth. Back and forth.

A'na sat on a stone stool under the pomegranate tree with her pipa in her arms. She'd changed into a pomegranate-red Hu outfit, its turned-down lapels edged with gold thread, and her little chestnut braids hung over her shoulders, swaying as she plucked the strings.

Beside her sat Chen Four, the singer, a girl in her early twenties, fresh-faced and pretty. But when she opened her mouth to sing, her voice was like a mountain spring, clear and bright, flowing straight into your heart.

Shen You listened for a while with his eyes closed, then suddenly spoke up. "Do you know any new songs?"

"What would the Master like to hear?"

Shen You hummed a couple of lines. It was a song that had been on repeat on his phone for years in his past life.

Chen Four tilted her head and listened to the end, thought for a moment, then opened her mouth and carried it on. The words were the same words, but she'd kneaded the tune into the cadences of the Tang, singing it out word by word, lilting and long, as if it had flowed all the way down from fourteen hundred years ago to today.

A'na listened twice, and then her pipa came in after it.

Shen You rocked and listened, and as he listened, the corners of his mouth curled up.

***

By the time the sun was well up, Zhou One and Zheng Three came to report.

Shen You didn't get up. The rocking chair kept rocking. Qingluo stood behind him, kneading his shoulders, neither too hard nor too soft.

"The sugar works is open," Zheng Three said first. "The first batch came to forty jin. Dou the Eleventh took twenty-five jin and Kang Satuo took fifteen. The Dou side says seven powerful households in the city have already sent cards placing orders, all of it for the banquets they're throwing during the days of the empress's investiture. Kang Satuo says two Hu merchants just in from the west tasted it once and wanted to buy up a whole month's output on the spot."

"The pastry shop in the East Market," Zhou One went on, "has been open two days. Every day the line forms before the market drums have even sounded, and it sells out less than a double-hour after the market opens. Yesterday a steward from a duke's household came to the shop in person to ask whether we could make a special order to send to the house."

"And the wine?"

"Feng Two's strong spirit produced its first jar yesterday." The corner of Zhou One's mouth moved, which was rare. "Feng One refused to believe it was that strong. He took a big swig and slept the afternoon away beside the stable."

Shen You laughed out loud.

"What about Feng Four?"

"Feng Four has spent two days telling stories at a Hu tavern in the West Market, the kind with Hu girls serving," Zhou One said. "The first day, a dozen or so people listened. Yesterday, even the doorway was packed. He says the gossip he picks up grows by the day. There are two things the Master needs to know."

"Go on."

"The first. The day before yesterday, two of the Pei family's hangers-on went to the Bureau of Sacrifices in the Department of State Affairs and went through the monastic registers of every temple in the capital. The ones from last year to this year."

Shen You's rocking chair hitched once, then went on rocking.

The Pei family was looking into his monastic registration.

"The second thing isn't something Feng Four heard." Zhou One lowered his voice. "It's from Zheng One. He's been riding with Kang Satuo's camel caravan, and yesterday, outside Jinguang Gate, they met a band of Hu merchants coming back from Hexi. Their leader said that on the road they'd seen Turkic outriders heading south, one band after another."

The courtyard fell quiet for a moment. Even A'na's pipa dropped lower.

Shen You said nothing. He was remembering a line from the history books: *In the eighth month of the ninth year of Wude, Jieli Khan led more than a hundred thousand horsemen south and brought his army to the Wei River.*

Soon.

"There's one more thing," Zhou One said. "Feng Five and the others have found the man who left the knife."

***

Feng Five was called in. His long, narrow face had the gray cast of a night without sleep, but his eyes were very bright.

"Kaihua Ward," he said. "Second ward south of the Imperial City. A house with bows and blades hanging in the gatehouse, and everyone going in and out is a soldier. It's a military officer's house."

"Whose?"

"Cui Shouli. Colonel of the Left Encampment Guard."

Behind Shen You, Qingluo's hands paused on his shoulders.

"The man who left the knife is living in a side courtyard of the Cui house," Feng Five went on. "Qian Biao has been there twice. Both times at night, by the back door."

"Last night, at the third watch, Zhou Seven lay on a beam in the main hall of the Cui house." Feng Five paused. "He heard them talking."

"Saying what?"

"Cui Shouli said he's already made contact with thirty-odd of the old Eastern Palace men who scattered, and there are more in the Zhongnan Mountains who'll take a few days yet to get here. When the ten-day deadline comes, he moves," Feng Five said. "He told the man who left the knife to pay our house another visit tonight. Get a good look at what the house has been turned into, how many people there are, and where the box is kept."

Shen You rocked, gazing up at the pomegranate tree overhead. One fully ripe pomegranate had split open, and a few seeds dropped out and landed on the bamboo mat.

"Tonight?"

"Tonight."

Shen You thought it over, then smiled.

"Then let's invite him in."

He looked toward the veranda, where Zhou Four stood. Then, to Zhou One: "Make another box just like that lacquer box. Same clay seal, exactly."

Zhou One nodded. "One double-hour."

"Zhou Four, get the net cast. Don't damage my screen."

"And," he added, "tonight's songs go on as usual."

***

By the time Wei San came over the east wall, it was already the second watch.

He had been a squad leader in the Eastern Palace Guard's Left Command. He'd cut men down outside Xuanwu Gate with Cui Shouli and hidden in the Zhongnan Mountains for two months. Going over a wall into someone's house at night was about as hard for him as walking.

But tonight, when he landed, he froze for a moment.

He'd been to this house six days ago. Back then it had been a drafty haunted house, the courtyard choked with weeds, the roof creaking at every step.

Now there were level gray bricks under his feet. Lanterns hung under the eaves with glass shades, and the light that came through them was warm, a soft pale yellow. There was a fragrance in the air, sweet, a little like a temple and yet not. Bamboo had been planted in the back courtyard, and when the wind blew, its leaves rustled.

From deeper in the back courtyard came the sound of a pipa, and a woman singing. He couldn't tell what tune it was, lilting and lingering, drifting over line by line in a way that made a man's heart go soft. There was laughter too, and the clink of cups knocking together.

Wei San lay hidden in the dark for a quarter of an hour.

There was no one patrolling the courtyard. The pipa and the singing never stopped, and neither did the laughter. Everyone was in the back courtyard.

He crept along the foot of the wall toward the main hall, then circled around behind it to the sleeping hall. The window of the west room was lit, and the shadow of a row of shelves fell across the window paper.

He wet a fingertip with spit and poked a tiny hole in the paper.

The room was empty. A tall table, tall chairs, an empty bookshelf. On the table lay a sheet of paper written halfway down, the brush resting beside the inkstone, the ink still wet. Weighing down the paper beside it was a black lacquer box. It looked as though the owner had been halfway through writing when someone called him to the back courtyard to drink.

The clay seal on the lid of the box—he had seen a rubbing of it at Colonel Cui's. It was exactly the same.

Wei San's heart skipped a beat.

The Colonel had told him to come tonight only to look. But the box was right in front of him, three paces away, with no one in the room, and everyone in the back courtyard listening to songs and drinking.

Get it, and there'd be no more waiting four days. No more staking the lives of thirty-odd brothers.

He hesitated for only a single breath.

The window swung open without a sound, and he slipped in like a shadow, landing without the faintest noise. He reached out and took hold of the lacquer box.

From the bottom of the box ran a silk thread so fine it was nearly invisible.

*Twang.*

Something overhead made a soft sound.

A great net dropped down over him from the beams. Its cords were weighted with lead, and the instant it fell, it wrapped him up and pressed him to the floor. Wei San reached back and drew his blade, but it was only half out of the scabbard when a panel of the ceiling flipped open with a *whoosh*, and a small, slight figure dropped straight down from above and stamped on the wrist of his sword hand.

The blade left his grip.

The door opened. Zhou Four walked in, bent down, clamped one hand on the back of his neck, and pushed down.

Wei San's face was pressed against the cold gray bricks, and he couldn't move again.

Start to finish: three breaths.

In the back courtyard, the song had just reached its most lilting line, and the pipa went *ting-ting, dong-dong*, like pearls dropping onto a jade plate.

Not once, from beginning to end, had it missed a note.

***

By the time Wei San was dragged into the middle room of the sleeping hall, Shen You was already sitting there.

He'd changed into a clean robe and was leaning back in a tall chair. At his elbow was a plate of purple-red grapes, and Qingluo stood to one side, peeling them. The incense in the *boshan* censer was still drifting slowly upward, and the screen with the *Preface to the Orchid Pavilion* copied on it glowed faintly in the lamplight.

Wei San knelt on the Persian rug, his face covered in bloody welts from the net cords, but he held his neck stiff and didn't say a word.

"Colonel Cui's men," Shen You said, eating a grape, "certainly have stiff spines."

Wei San didn't so much as look at him.

Shen You wasn't in any hurry either. He tilted his head toward the back of the screen.

From behind the screen came a voice.

It wasn't loud. A man of forty or so, with a trace of a Hebei accent, speaking slowly, every word sounding as though it had been pressed flat in his mouth once before it came out.

"Wei San."

Wei San's whole body jolted, and his head snapped up.

"You did not get the thing, and you have been taken," the voice said slowly. "You may as well tell him the truth."

"C... Colonel?"

The color drained from Wei San's face all at once. He stared hard at the screen, his lips trembling, until there was not a drop of blood left in his cheeks.

From behind the screen stepped a skinny little fellow, seventeen or eighteen, who flashed him a grin.

It was Zhou Seven.

Wei San was stunned.

He stayed stunned for a long time, and then, slowly, he lowered his head.

He understood now. This household could imitate even the Colonel's voice down to the last inflection. Whatever the Colonel had said to Qian Biao at night, whatever he'd ordered Wei San to do, these people very likely knew all of it already.

He was guarding his tongue to protect something that had long since been taken out of his hands.

"...Cui Shouli," he said hoarsely. "Colonel of the Left Encampment Guard. He used to be deputy commander of the Eastern Palace Guard's Left Command."

"What's in the box?"

"I don't know," Wei San said. "The Colonel only said it was a roster His Highness the Crown Prince left behind."

"What roster?"

"Before the Xuanwu Gate Incident... the men who were secretly in touch with the Eastern Palace. Who passed it messages. Who made it promises." Wei San closed his eyes. "A good many of them are at the new emperor's court now, holding office."

The room went still.

Qingluo's hands stopped on the grape she was peeling. She looked at Shen You and said softly, "Cui Shouli... Back in the Prince of Qi's household, this maid sealed letters to him twice on the Prince of Qi's behalf."

Shen You said nothing. Zhou One took a black lacquer box from his sleeve and set it at Shen You's elbow.

Wei San stared at the box, stunned. The same box. The same clay seal.

"The one in the study—Zhou One made it this afternoon." Shen You tilted his head toward Zhou One. "What you risked your neck to grab was an empty box."

Every last trace of color was gone from Wei San's face.

Shen You picked up the real lacquer box and weighed it in his hand.

A roster. Some of the people on it had already died along with the Crown Prince. Others had gone over to the new emperor, swapped into a new set of official robes, and now stood at court in Xiande Hall of the Eastern Palace.

No wonder Cui Shouli was this desperate. If this box were ever delivered to Xiande Hall of the Eastern Palace, who knew how many of the court's civil and military officials would lie awake tonight.

He set the box back on the table and suddenly smiled.

"Master?" Zhou One asked.

"Let him go."

Zhou Four's brows drew together. "Let him go, and Cui Shouli knows what we've got."

"That's the point," Shen You said. "He'll go back and say the Shen household has dozens of people, every one of them a real hand, that they took him in three breaths, and that they could imitate the Colonel's voice down to the last inflection."

"Once Cui Shouli hears that, either he backs down, and that's the end of it. Or he calls up every man he can get and comes himself on the day the deadline runs out."

He looked at Wei San and smiled, perfectly easygoing.

"Rather than guard against a knife in the dark, better to invite him out into the light."

"In four days, there'll be a hundred and twenty people in my house," he said. "He doesn't know that."

***

Wei San was let go.

When he left, there was a clay jar in his hands, its mouth sealed with red paper, heavy.

"White sugar, for Colonel Cui to taste," Shen You said. "And while you're at it, take him a message: four days left. If the Colonel wants the box, he can come get it himself. Dinner's on me."

***

In Kaihua Ward, the lamps in the main hall of the Cui house burned until dawn.

Cui Shouli was a man of forty or so, square-faced, his temples touched with frost. He sat under the lamp and listened to Wei San tell it from beginning to end without interrupting once.

When Wei San got to the "three breaths," his brow twitched.

When he got to "the voice from behind the screen," Cui Shouli's fingers went still on the table.

When he got to the fake box that was exactly the same, Cui Shouli closed his eyes for a moment.

When it was over, he looked at the clay jar on the table for a long time.

He lifted off the red paper, took a pinch with his fingertips, and put it in his mouth.

Sweet.

Clean, pure sweetness, sweeter than the tribute from the Western Regions he had eaten at Eastern Palace banquets.

Cui Shouli slowly swallowed that bit of sweetness. His face showed no expression at all.

"Colonel..." Wei San was kneeling on the floor. "That man has at least forty or fifty people in his house, and every one of them is a hard hand."

"I know."

Cui Shouli rose and walked to the window. Outside, the edge of the sky was turning the pale white of a fish's belly.

"Go," he said. "Call back the men in the Zhongnan Mountains. All of them."

"And," he paused, "tell the brothers the day stays the same day. But the method changes."

"Yesterday the Left Encampment Guard received a military dispatch. The Turkic vanguard has already passed Jingzhou. In a few days, the capital will be under martial law."

He turned around, his face caught where the morning light met the lamplight, half bright, half dark.

"When the capital falls into chaos, that is our chance."
