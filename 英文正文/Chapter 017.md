# Chapter 17: Beyond the Pass

When the Hour of the Rat came, the eighth month was over.

Not one lamp in the east compound had gone out. Lamps hung under the eaves of all seven rows of thatched sheds. The sacks of millet had been piled back onto the planks over the five pits, and the open ground was stacked with something else now: pack frames, waterskins, rolls of felt, bundle after bundle of arrows wrapped in hemp cloth. Feng Seven squatted in the middle of a heap of hemp rope, counting knots one coil at a time; each time she finished a coil, she drew a line on the rope's end with a piece of charcoal. Of the hundred and twenty, half were already slumped asleep under the sheds, hide robes for blankets, bows for pillows. The other half were still awake—whetting blades, patching boots—and nobody spoke. Behind the wooden palisade, eighty-seven Hu horses had their heads down over the night fodder. Twenty camels knelt in two rows chewing their cud, jaws grinding side to side, and the sour smell of it hit the nose from ten paces off.

Ge One went down the line with a lamp, running his hands over one camel after another. He felt whether the felt pads under the pack frames were thick enough, and which hole the girth had been cinched to. At the seventh camel, the beast swung its neck around and sprayed him full in the face with froth. He wiped it off with his sleeve and kept feeling.

Feng One's clay forge was still glowing red, but the hammering had stopped. Su Three had taken the spot by the forge instead, sewing a lining into a hide robe by the firelight. Zheng Four sat under a lamp putting in the last few stitches, a curtained hat across her knees. Zhou Five had bent the hoop from split bamboo, and over it went a layer of fine woven rattan. All around the brim hung a curtain of gauze—*qingrong* cut from Shen You's own bed curtains, so thin the light came through it—falling all the way to the shoulders.

Shen You was curled up in the rocking chair with a silk-floss quilt over his legs, shelling chestnuts. Zhou Four had carried the rocking chair over from the back courtyard and set it on the steps of the east compound. Every time it rocked, it creaked.

"Master," Zhou One said from the foot of the steps. "It's the third watch."

"Mm." Shen You tossed a chestnut into his mouth. "Go ahead."

And there were more people at the foot of the steps.

One was squatting beside a camel. The camel stretched out its neck to sniff the top of his head, and he didn't flinch—just stared back at it, the two of them eye to eye. One stood in front of Feng One's forge and, without thinking, held out both hands to warm them. One sat on a sack of millet, legs crossed, settled there as solidly as if he'd always been sitting there, and had been all night.

Twenty-three. Men and women, tall and short, all in plain undyed cloth. Two of them were barefoot.

Zhou One took up his brush and asked them one by one. The six remaining Ges, seven Xis, seven Fans, three Pengs. When he got to the Xis, someone by the palisade looked up. It was Ashina, sitting cross-legged on the ground, cleaning his bow.

"Xi," he said. "East on the grassland, there is a tribe also called Xi."

"It's from the *Hundred Family Surnames*," Zhou One said.

Ashina nodded, lowered his head, and went back to cleaning his bow.

Oil pressing, pot mending, basket weaving, well digging, dog raising, mat weaving... The brush flew. It reached Ge Four and stopped.

"Cooking for an army in the field," Ge Four said. He was about thirty, his face smoked dark, his eyebrows singed down to half their length.

"Cooking how?"

"Dig a pit. Off the side of the pit, dig a trench at a slant. Roof the trench with branches, cover it with earth. The fire burns in the pit, the smoke runs down the trench and comes out in the grass three zhang away, and the moment it comes out, it's gone," Ge Four said. "From three li off, you won't see a single wisp."

Zhou One raised his eyes toward Chu One by the palisade. Chu One was looking at Ge Four too. He looked a while, then gave a single nod.

Fan Two was a woman of about thirty with broad palms, and all ten of her fingers were scored with lines worn in by thread. When Zhou One asked what she could do, she didn't answer. First she looked at Shen You in the rocking chair.

"Master, stand up. Walk a couple of steps."

Shen You blinked, threw off the quilt, stood, and walked a couple of steps.

"The left leg is half a finger longer than the right," Fan Two said. "The saddle was made for two legs the same length. Sit the Master in it, and his backside goes hunting to the left all on its own."

Zhou Four, three paces behind Shen You, spoke up. "So that's why. Tips left."

The ring of people above and below the steps were holding in laughter so hard their shoulders shook. Shen You cracked first. He pointed at Zhou Four. "How many times did you actually count that day?"

"Left eleven, right nine."

"Saddlery," Fan Two said, only now answering Zhou One's question. "Give me the Master's saddle. It'll be altered before dawn."

The last was Peng Three—the one sitting on the millet sack. Early forties, lean and dried-out, with hands that were long and slender.

"Ear cleaning."

Shen You had only just sat back down in the rocking chair. A mouthful of chestnut caught in his throat, and he coughed so hard the chair pitched back and forth.

"A foot man, a barber," he said between coughs, ticking them off on his fingers, "and now an ear cleaner. One more back-scrubber and I'm opening a bathhouse in the West Market."

Without a flicker on his face, Zhou One wrote in small characters in the margin of the paper: *Bathhouse.*

Peng Three looked at Shen You, looked a while. "There's something in the Master's left ear."

Shen You's hand stopped beside his ear. "Now?"

Peng Three's eyes slid toward the pack frames. "When the Master comes back."

"Two hundred and seventy-six, counting the two on the road to Yizhou," Zhou One said, and added a line in small characters in the corner of the paper: *Two more rows of sheds in the east compound.*

Shen You tipped his chin at the twenty-three below the steps. "Once you're here, you're family. There's hot soup noodles in the kitchen, Sanniang's cooking. Go eat first. Once you're full, whoever needs sleep, sleep; whoever needs to work, work."

The twenty-three newcomers gave a folded-hands salute all together and followed a woman from the kitchen out. At the moon gate, the one who'd been staring the camel down turned his head for one more look at it. The camel was looking at him too.

Zhou One laid down his brush, drew a roll of hemp paper from his sleeve, and handed it up with both hands. It was the travel pass, copied out stroke by stroke in a neat, proper hand, the vermilion seal at the end bright as fresh paint. The people of the camel train were listed one after another—name, age, appearance—with the counts of camels and horses after them. Shen You read down line by line. Partway through, he came to one line:

*Kang Fuyan, nephew of Kang Satuo, aged twenty-nine, beardless, pale of face.*

Shen You looked up.

"You'd already written me in."

"What else am I for?" Zhou One gave his goatee a tug, and this time the tug went all the way down. "I'll ask only one thing. The Master is going—for what?"

The east compound went still all at once. Zheng Four's needle stopped in midair. Ge One's lamp stopped swaying. Dozens of people pretended not to be looking, every ear pricked.

Shen You tossed a chestnut shell on the ground.

"Two things." He held up one finger. "A thousand horses. A hundred and twenty people can't drive them back. Every night I get twenty-some more people, and they turn up within ten paces of me. If I'm in Kaiming Ward, they turn up in Kaiming Ward. If I'm at the foot of the great ridge, they turn up right there in front of the horses."

Zhou One didn't answer. His lips moved, adding it up one number at a time.

"The second of the month, twenty-four. The third, twenty-five. The fourth, twenty-six. The fifth, twenty-seven. The sixth, twenty-eight," he said. "A hundred and thirty."

"Enough to drive horses?"

"Enough," Zhou One said. "The grain is packed for two hundred and fifty mouths, and Ge One's pack frames carry a hundred and thirty extra sets of hide robes and boots as well. And the second thing?"

Shen You raised a second finger, then lowered it again and broke into a grin.

"I want to see."

Zhou One looked at him for a long while. Then he took one step forward.

"I have three conditions."

"Let's hear them."

"One: you go only as far as the foot of the great ridge. You do not enter the camp, you do not join the fighting, you do not give orders. Two: by day, the hat stays on. Three: Zhou Four is never more than ten paces from you."

"Deal," Shen You said. "I'll add one: when I'm tired, I rest, and nobody rushes me."

Zhou One's beard gave a twitch. "...I have noted it."

"And at home?"

"The Master has caught a chill, cannot be out in the wind, and is receiving no one," Zhou One said. "If someone comes from the county, I'll receive them. If the Eleventh comes, I'll tell him the Master is soaking in the hot pool and won't be out for ten days."

"Ten days in there, I'll be a prune."

"The Eleventh will believe it."

At the foot of the steps, someone finally laughed out loud. It was Chu Four—a flash of white teeth, and he clapped a hand over his mouth.

"One more matter," Zhou One said. "Deputy Commandant Liang's chit. Up on the slope, the Master promised the general on the black horse it would be delivered to the camp the next day. It wasn't, and as of today it's two days late. I've made a copy for our records. As soon as the ward gate opens, the gatekeeper will take the original to the camp by the Wei, show it to the General's people, and bring it back once they've seen it. The gatekeeper's bow—not too high, not too low. No one could fault it."

"And if they fault us for the two days?"

"It won't land on the Master," Zhou One said. "The Master has caught a chill."

Shen You roared with laughter, slapped the arm of the rocking chair, and stood up. "Bed!"

He said bed. He didn't sleep.

At the fourth watch, he threw a robe over his shoulders and came out again. Most of the lamps in the east compound had gone out, and people were dozing slumped under the sheds—some pillowed on pack frames, some on millet sacks, a pair sitting back to back. Ge Four and a few of the newcomers had been taken to the kitchen for a bowl of hot soup noodles, and now they were packed in together, sleeping the soundest of anyone.

Someone was still sitting by the palisade.

Ashina sat cross-legged on the ground with the black-lacquered horn bow across his knees and a lump of beeswax in his hand, working it down the bowstring stroke by stroke. When he reached the end, he started again from the top.

"Can't sleep?"

Ashina looked up, saw it was Shen You, and started to rise. Shen You waved him down and sat on a millet sack beside him.

"Since the year I am fourteen," Ashina said, "the night before a long journey, I never sleep."

"This isn't a long journey," Shen You said. "This time you're heading back."

The wax in Ashina's hand paused.

"...Yes."

He lowered his head and went on waxing the string. The bone thumb ring sat on his thumb and didn't turn once.

Shen You sat a while, listening to the camels chew their cud and the horses behind the palisade shifting their weight from hoof to hoof. Then he got up and went back. This time, the moment he lay down and closed his eyes, he was asleep.

The first day of the ninth month. The middle of the Hour of the Rabbit.

The sky was just turning gray-blue when the camels in the east compound rose to their feet, one after another.

The eighty-seven Hu horses behind the palisade stayed put. The day before, the moment the horses came through the gate, Zhou One had settled it: the new purchases were green and had never done a long road with these people, so they would stay behind the palisade and be fed up until the horses from the north came back, and then they'd all change hands together. The mounts for the road were seventy picked from the horses the household already had. The twenty-four wounded horses in the stable stayed too, and Feng Three would go on changing their dressings as before. Only the gray mare went along, her lead gripped in Cao One's hand.

Fan Two brought the saddle. The front of the saddletree had been built up a layer, the left stirrup taken up half a finger, and the seat stuffed with sheepskin Su Three had tanned, fleece side in. She held it up beside the chestnut and waited for Shen You to mount.

Shen You looked at the horse, looked at the saddle, then looked down at his own two legs. Yesterday afternoon he'd bounced around half a circuit on horseback, and the insides of his thighs were still on fire.

"Camel first."

Ge One had come prepared. The lead camel, a two-humped Bactrian, was kneeling on the ground, and between its humps a seat had been rigged, with crossbars front and back; the back one was wrapped in felt for leaning on. Shen You climbed up by way of Ge One's knee, got himself settled, and before he had a chance to say one word of praise, the camel moved.

The hind legs came up first.

The camel's back pitched sharply forward, and Shen You was flung forward with it, nose smacking into the front hump. Before he'd recovered, the forelegs came up, the back tipped the other way, and he was thrown backward, his spine slamming into the felt-wrapped crossbar. Finally the camel stood square and gave itself a shake from head to tail.

Shen You clung to the front hump with both arms, and it was a long moment before he got his breath back.

"...It even runs a self-test on boot."

Nobody understood. Ge One stroked the camel's neck and explained on its behalf: "It was thinking."

Qingluo came over from the veranda, rose on tiptoe, tied the hat's strings under his chin in a slipknot, and smoothed the gauze curtain flat with her fingers. Her hands were cool. Her fingertips brushed his chin once and withdrew.

"Today this maid has not seen the Master," she said. "The Master is in the hot pool."

"Don't let the water go cold."

"This maid will heat it every day."

A'xing came trotting up with a cloth bag and stood on her toes to hang it on the pack frame. "Sanniang says flatbread gets hard on the road, so these have mutton fat in them—you can still bite them cold. Ten! The Master eats three a day, and the one left over is for Zhou Four."

Zhou Four was holding the camel's lead rope and didn't turn his head. "I've got my own."

"Sanniang said." A'xing glared at him.

Zhou Four didn't say another word.

Ge Four had an iron pot on his back, stuffed with an iron spatula, a fire steel, and a bundle of dried mugwort, and he followed behind the camel. During the night Zhou One had added a line to the travel pass: *one cook*. Fan Two was staying home. This saddle, she said, had been altered overnight and would do for now; when the Master came back, she'd make him a new one from the saddletree up, fitted to his two legs.

***

With the ward gate thrown wide open, Warden Zhao felt ten years older and ten years younger at once.

Ten days. Martial law had been declared on the night of the twenty-first of the eighth month, and the ward gate had opened only one leaf, with everyone going in or out questioned. Every day he'd squatted in the gateway with the keys in his robe, until he could barely straighten his back. Before dawn today, someone had knocked on his door to say word had come down from the palace: the city gates would run as usual, the ward gates as usual. The West Market had heard first—yesterday afternoon men from the Jinwu Guard had put the word out in the market, and the Hu merchants had harnessed their camels overnight. The proper word for the wards had arrived behind it. He worked the key into the lock and pushed both leaves all the way open. The gate pivots squealed and squealed, and every squeal eased something in his chest.

Outside the gate, Old Man Wang's flatbread oven was already smoking. He banged the rim with his iron tongs and hawked his wares, louder than he'd been before martial law.

Then the Shen family's camels came out.

Warden Zhao didn't count. Last time he'd counted horses, he'd lost track somewhere in the forties; the time before that, he'd gotten to sixty and then been blocked by camels. He just watched the first one. On the camel's back sat someone in a curtained hat, the gauze hanging to the shoulders, not a scrap of face visible.

The Kang family's manager. Steward Zhou had made a point of coming by yesterday evening to say that among the people the Kang family had sent with the horses, a Hu boy and a manager had stayed the night at the Shen house and would leave the ward with the camels in the morning. Their names were all entered in his register. The manager, Steward Zhou had said, had come down with an eye ailment on the road and couldn't bear the light.

Following the camels came a long file of men leading horses, dozens of them, not a sound out of any of them. Steward Zhou had mentioned this too: porters the Kang family had hired to meet the camels, who'd stayed the night in the east compound. Their names had been entered in the register, one by one.

"Warden Zhao." Sister Liu stood by the well platform with a bucket in hand. "The Shens are selling off all their camels?"

"Selling, nothing. The Shens bought new camels, and they're going west with the Kang family's camel train," Warden Zhao said. "It's business."

"And Young Master Shen? Haven't seen him out strolling today."

At the gate of the Shen house, Zhou One happened to be standing on the steps. He gave a folded-hands salute toward the well. "The Master caught a chill last night. He mustn't be out in the wind."

"A chill?" Sister Liu dropped her bucket. "I've got ginger! Old ginger!"

She hitched up her skirt and ran for home.

On the camel's back, the gauze of that curtained hat quivered and quivered, and went on quivering for quite some time.

***

Out of the west gate of Kaiming Ward lay Vermilion Bird Avenue.

On the first morning after martial law was lifted, the avenue—a hundred paces wide—seemed to have just woken up and was stretching. The wooden barricades that had stood ten days at the ward gates were being dragged against the walls one by one by the patrolmen. Pagoda-tree seedpods littered the ground and popped under the cartwheels. The carts were all heading north. One kind was piled with sacks of millet—farm families from south of the city coming in to sell grain, bound for the two markets. The other kind was piled with chests, crates, pots, and bowls, with old folks and children perched on top: households that had fled into the city ten-odd days ago, bound now for Kaiyuan Gate and Jinguang Gate, going home to the north side of the Wei. The bell of Daxingshan Temple carried from behind the ward walls to the northeast. One stroke, then another.

Up on the camel's back, Shen You sat a good head and shoulders above everyone else and could see a long way. Without thinking, he reached for his waist to take out his phone and snap a picture, grabbed at nothing, and turned the motion into a pat on his thigh, as if he were brushing off dust.

Zhou Four glanced at him and didn't ask.

A camel walks with both legs on one side moving together—both left legs at once, then both right legs. The person on top tipped left, then tipped right, no different from riding a boat in a swell. They hadn't even passed one ward before Shen You's stomach started climbing.

Zhou Six came up from behind and handed him two slices of ginger.

"Hold them in your mouth. Don't chew."

"Sister Liu's ginger?"

"Mine."

Shen You slipped the ginger under the curtain of his hat and held it in his mouth. It was so sharp and hot his eyes nearly watered, but his stomach really did settle.

The camel didn't care whether its rider was suffering. It walked step after step, perfectly steady, its pads soft, making no sound at all on the yellow earth of the road—only the bronze bell under its neck, one ring per step. They passed Lanling Ward, then Jingshan Ward. Jingshan Ward's west gate was open, and a few little novice monks were sweeping up pagoda seedpods with bamboo brooms—sweep once, look up at the camels, sweep again. The ward walls were a zhang or so high, and from the camel's back he could see over them: rooftops inside, patch after patch, fewer of gray tile than of thatch. In one yard a pole of washing hung out to dry. On one rooftop crouched a cat, which traded a look with him, then turned its head away and went back to licking its paw.

It occurred to him that traveling like this wasn't half bad.

Slow, swaying, his stomach churning like a stormy sea—but high. Chang'an passed beneath his feet one ward at a time, as if someone were slowly unrolling a scroll painting, and wherever it had unrolled to, that was what he saw.

"Zhou Four."

"Here."

"When we're back, have Zhou Five make me something I can lie down in on a camel."

Zhou Four said nothing. After a while he said, "Lie down, fall off."

At the south wall of the Imperial City they turned west and went half the length of Chunming Gate Avenue, and outside the West Market's north gate the street was already jammed solid.

A big cart loaded with firewood had snapped its axle right at the market gate, and bundles of firewood had rolled across half the street. Patrolmen waved their sticks and cursed people into hauling it clear. The camels stood nose to rump, moved a step, then stopped for an age. Shen You sat on the camel's back holding his ginger, watching the sun climb inch by inch above the tops of the ward walls.

The Kang family's own camel train stretched from the market gate all the way to the end of the street: two hundred and ninety camels, a hundred-odd horses, a hundred and seventy-odd people. Camel bells jingled and clanked; Sogdian, Kuchean, and Han speech were all stewed together in one pot; and the air smelled of camel piss, wool, pepper, and fresh-baked sesame flatbread. When the Shen family's twenty camels and thirty-odd horses slipped in among them, it was like pouring a bowl of water into a river. Not even a splash.

Kang Satuo came down from the head of the train on a red bay, green-eyed, curly-bearded, the string of glass beads at his waist clinking all the way. He reined in beside Shen You's camel, tipped his head back, and looked him over from top to bottom.

"Twenty years I've been in the West Market," he said. "The camel trains I've sent off—if not a hundred, then eighty. What rides on a camel is cargo. Or womenfolk."

"So which am I?"

"The soldiers at the gate," Kang Satuo said, giving his beard a stroke, "will just take you for somebody's womenfolk."

There was a moment of silence behind the gauze.

"...Fine. Womenfolk it is."

Kang Satuo threw back his head and laughed so loud his horse snorted along with him. He turned and bellowed at one of the Hu boys in the Hu tongue—probably cursing him for a crooked pack frame—then turned back and lowered his voice.

"Chu One's lot went out through Jinguang Gate with the He family's camel train. They're already out. Feng Five's lot is with the Shi family's, following behind me through Kaiyuan Gate. We meet at the fork west of Xianyang."

Having said it, he wheeled his horse and went back to the head of the train.

The whole way, Kang Satuo didn't look back at that curtained hat again.

He had worked this account over at home for two nights.

Eighty-seven horses and twenty camels, eleven hundred strings, fronted by him for now and to be docked from the Snow Sugar later—that was the open account. The hidden account was another book entirely. The "Kang Fuyan" on the travel pass was a nephew of his own Kang family. The hundred-odd extra camel guards and seventy horses in the three camel trains were all written down on the passes as his people and his horses. At a city gate, at a river crossing, anywhere at all—if someone got particular, the first man led away in ropes would be Kang Satuo.

In twenty years in the West Market, he'd seen plenty of patrons. Men with money, men with power, men with both. When a man with money and power borrowed your camel train, if it went well, he owed you a favor; if it went badly, you carried it yourself.

This one was different. What this one lacked wasn't money, and it wasn't nerve. It was years. The day after Mid-Autumn, he'd first set foot in that house to talk about white sugar, and there'd been no more than ten or twenty people to be seen in the courtyard. The day before yesterday, he'd passed by the kitchen, cast an eye over the flatbreads, and worked it out at a little over two hundred. Less than half a month, start to finish.

Kang Satuo fingered that string of glass beads, counting along it one bead at a time.

In another year, the line of people waiting at this family's door would run from the West Market to the East Market. Get in line then, and you'd never get in.

And besides, up north there were horses. Turkic horses, raised on the grassland, with legs for running and hides for the cold. Bring one to Chang'an, and it was worth two. He wanted a look first.
