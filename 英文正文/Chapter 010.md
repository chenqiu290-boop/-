# Chapter 10: The General Amnesty

The twelfth day, the Hour of the Dragon.

The twelve who had arrived at the Hour of the Rat had already been entered in the roster by Zhou One, one by one. Seventy-eight now.

Shen You was still soaking in the hot pool when men from the county showed up at the front gate.

Not Qian Biao. A county clerk in a yellow cloth robe, with two runners carrying staves behind him. He held a writ in both hands, stood at the gate with his back ramrod straight, and read it aloud to Zhou Four, who had opened the door:

"*Per an informant's report: Shen You of Kaiming Ward claims to be a monk of the capital returned to lay life, yet the monastic registers of the Bureau of Sacrifices contain no such person. He is suspected of fraudulently claiming monastic registration and forging official documents. By writ of the County Commandant: Shen You is summoned to appear at the county office at the Hour of the Goat today to answer the charge.*"

When he had finished, he passed the writ inside and added, "The informant will be there too. Don't be late, Young Master Shen."

By the time Zhou Four brought the writ to the door of the bath room, Shen You was leaning back against the side of the pool, watching a few osmanthus blossoms drift on the water.

"Read it to me."

Zhou Four read it.

The bath room was quiet for a while. The only sound was the water in the copper cauldron over by the stove, bubbling away.

"Who's the informant?"

"The writ says Liu Qi. Feng Four says he's a hanger-on of the Pei family."

Shen You said, "Huh," and stood up out of the pool, water sheeting off him.

"Get Zhou One, Qingluo, and Zheng Three. Main hall."

***

In the main hall, Zhou One read the writ three times, and his face darkened.

"Master, this move is cleverer than Qian Biao's."

"Tell me."

"Qian Biao wanted to arrest the Master's people and take the Master's box," Zhou One said. "What the Pei family wants is everything the Master has."

"Fraudulently claiming monastic registration, forging official documents—under the code, that's penal servitude, and in serious cases, exile. Once a man is in jail, his house is sealed and his shop is shut. If he can't account for where his money came from, it can be treated as ill-gotten and confiscated, and the slaves registered under his name go to the authorities to be sold off."

He glanced at Qingluo.

"Whatever the authorities sell off goes to the highest bidder. All the Pei family has to do is put in a bid at the sale, and Qingluo is theirs. So is the sugar works."

Qingluo, standing to one side, went pale for an instant, but said nothing.

"The sugar works..." Zheng Three frowned. "The Pei family has its eye on the sugar works too?"

"Shen's pastries in the East Market sell out every day. And how much sugar the Dou and Kang families haul away from us every month—every merchant in Chang'an can see it," Zhou One said. "A monk back in lay life with no roots and no backing, sitting on a gold mine. Pei Shisan runs the Pei family's business. It would be strange if he didn't notice."

Shen You leaned back in his chair, heard him out, and nodded.

"Two birds with one stone. He wants my people, and he wants my shops." He smiled a little. "And he doesn't even have to lift a finger. He's borrowing the county's knife."

"This Liu Qi," Zhou One said. "Feng Four asked around. He failed the Classics examination, and he's been a hanger-on of the Pei family for seven or eight years, handling their lawsuits. He knows the code better than the county's own clerks."

"So what's he got?"

"No such person in the Bureau of Sacrifices," Zhou One said. "That alone is enough for the Commandant to take the Master into custody first, then send a dispatch to Yizhou to check his original registration. Yizhou and back takes two months at the very least. For those two months, the Master sits in the county jail, the house stays sealed, and the sugar works stands idle."

"By the time Yizhou's reply arrives," he paused, "everything the Master owns will long since belong to the Pei family."

For a moment no one in the main hall spoke.

Shen You glanced at the sun. Halfway through the Hour of the Dragon. Less than three double-hours until the Hour of the Goat.

"Liu Qi did his math well," he said. "There's just one thing he didn't account for."

"What?"

"He checked the Bureau of Sacrifices," Shen You said. "But I was never on the Bureau of Sacrifices' registers to begin with."

***

Zhou One froze for a moment. Then his eyes lit up.

Qingluo raised her head too.

"Back in the fifth month, the court culled the monks and nuns," Shen You said, taking his time. "Who exactly got culled?"

Qingluo answered softly. "The edict said the Buddhist and Daoist temples of the capital were far from pure. The ones to be culled were false and unworthy monks and nuns—privately founded hermitages, and privately ordained monks with no official register."

"Right," Shen You said. "The big temples on the official registers kept more than half of what they had. The ones that got torn down hardest were the little hermitages that weren't on any official register."

"And the one I lived in—wasn't that a little hermitage?"

Zhou One got it all at once. "The ruined temple on the east side of Kaiming Ward."

The very same ruined temple, the one with the headless Buddha, where Shen You had slept his first night in Chang'an.

"The monks in a hermitage were never on the Bureau of Sacrifices' registers in the first place," Shen You said. "No such person in the Bureau of Sacrifices? That's exactly how it should be. If they *had* found me, that would be strange."

"But private ordination is against the code too," Zhou One reminded him. "A hundred blows of the stick, and forced back to lay life."

"When was the crime committed?"

"...When the Master took his vows."

"And what day did I arrive in Chang'an?"

Zhou One stopped short.

Shen You smiled, put out one finger, and tapped it lightly on the desk.

"The ninth day of the eighth month. The day the new emperor took the throne."

"And what did the notice the officials posted that day say?"

"A general amnesty for all under Heaven," Qingluo said.

"'*A general amnesty for all under Heaven. All crimes, grave or minor, whether already discovered or not yet discovered, are hereby pardoned,*'" Shen You said. "Private ordination isn't one of the Ten Abominations. Before the ninth day of the eighth month, I could have been a privately ordained monk for a hundred years, and it would still be pardoned."

"After the pardon, I got myself registered." He spread his hands. "A Wannian County clerk wrote out my registration with his own hand and stamped it with the seal with his own hand."

The main hall was quiet for a while.

Zhou One looked at him for a long time before he spoke. "That day—did the Master plan it, landing in that ruined temple?"

"Oh, no," Shen You said. "Just lucky."

***

But a good story alone wasn't enough.

Liu Qi was no fool. He would ask: Where was the hermitage? Who was your teacher? Who can vouch for you?

They had less than three double-hours to get all of that in hand.

Shen You didn't move. He sat in the main hall, drinking the tea Qingluo had brewed, and gave his orders one at a time.

"Feng Five, go find someone. An old farmer from south of the city who grows millet. Drives an ox cart, wears a white cloth wrapped around his head, son's in the militia. The day I got to Chang'an, he was the first person who spoke to me. He called me 'Little Master.'"

Feng Five turned and left.

"Feng Four, you've been at the popular sermons in the temples. You know a fair number of the monks who got thrown out in the fifth month. Go find out about that hermitage in Kaiming Ward: who the old monk was, how many disciples he had, where they all are now."

"Zhou One, go to the ward gate and copy down the amnesty edict they posted on the ninth day of the eighth month. Every last word."

Zhou One hesitated. "The Master doesn't want me to fill in a few pages of documents?"

"No," Shen You said. "Forge anything after the amnesty and it's a new crime. No amnesty covers that. Everything we use today has to be real. Every last thing."

"Zheng Three, take some money and go ask Warden Zhao and Sister Liu to come."

"Qingluo," he said, turning to her, "you're with me."

***

Feng Five found the old farmer in a millet field south of Baoning Ward, in the south of the city.

The old man was crouched on a field ridge tying up millet stalks. When he heard he was wanted at the county office as a witness, the straw rope dropped right out of his hand.

"A witness? Me? I just farm..."

"All you have to do is tell the truth," Feng Five said. "The evening of the ninth day of the eighth month, out in the waste ground in Kaiming Ward—did you see a young fellow with short hair?"

The old man thought about it and nodded. "I did. Hair about an inch long, and dressed in strange clothes. I took him for a little monk who'd been run out of his temple. Told him to go make do for the night in the ruined temple to the east..."

He broke off and eyed Feng Five warily. "That young fellow in some kind of trouble?"

"He's done nothing wrong. Someone's out to ruin him," Feng Five said. "Whether you come or not, this is yours."

He set a small string of coins on the old man's millet stalks.

The old man looked at the coins, then at Feng Five, and for a long while didn't move.

"Don't want your money." He pushed it back and sighed. "My eldest was called up just the day before yesterday. They say there's going to be fighting up north, and every militiaman has to go. My heart's been all in knots these past days. I was just fretting I had no way to earn some merit."

He stood up and slapped the dirt off his trouser legs.

"Let's go. I'll go tell the truth."

***

When Sister Liu heard she was wanted at the county office, she shrank back in fright at first. Then Zheng Three explained what it was about, and she slapped her thigh.

"That ruined temple—I know it! There used to be an old monk living there, him and three disciples. Two of them were mostly around the place. They grew a few rows of vegetables, and at night they'd light a lamp and chant sutras. The other one spent most of the year off copying sutras for people. Didn't come back but a few times a year. I can't even remember what he looked like. Back in the fifth month, when the officials came to tear the place down, the old monk sat in the doorway and cried the whole day. Wasn't two months before he took sick and died. He's buried right out back of the temple, in that graveyard."

"What was the old monk's Dharma name?"

"Who'd know a thing like that? Everybody in the ward just called him Old Master." As she talked, her eyes went red. "The poor old soul..."

Warden Zhao was even more straightforward. Back in the fifth month, when the hermitage was torn down, he had been the one to lead the county men there; the registering, the demolition, the dispersal had all passed through his hands. He dug out an old ledger sheet from that day. It read: *Kaiming Ward: one hermitage, unchartered; three monks; dispersed.*

Three monks. No names.

"That day there was only Old Master and two of his disciples in the temple." Warden Zhao scratched his head. "Old Master said there was one more off copying sutras somewhere, not around. He's the one who gave the number. I just wrote it down how he said."

Zheng Three glanced at it, and the corner of her mouth twitched.

At the Hour of the Horse, Feng Four came back too.

"The old monk at the hermitage was from Puzhou. When he was young he broke his vows at a temple and was expelled, so he put up that hermitage in Kaiming Ward himself. For decades it was never on any official register," he said. "Three disciples. One went home to Puzhou, one went into the Zhongnan Mountains. And the third spent most of the year off copying sutras for people. He wasn't there the day the temple came down either, and no one's seen him since."

Shen You nodded.

Of the three disciples, he was the one "no one's seen since." All anyone in the ward knew was that the hermitage had a disciple who copied sutras. Nobody remembered his face.

***

The Hour of the Goat. The Wannian County office.

The county office stood in Xuanyang Ward, facing south with its back to the north, two stone lions at its gate. Past the main gate ran a long paved walkway lined with the clerks' quarters, and farther in still was the hall where the County Commandant handled his business. The hall wasn't large. In the middle stood a high desk with the Commandant seated behind it, clerks and staff-bearing runners standing along both sides. On the floor before the desk lay an old mat: the spot where those summoned had to stand.

When Shen You arrived, the informant was already there.

Liu Qi was a little over thirty, thin, in a hemp robe washed thin with age, his brows and eyes so faint they barely seemed to be there. When he saw Shen You come in, he cupped his hands in a perfectly proper salute. There was not a trace of hostility in him.

The County Commandant, surnamed Lu, was around thirty, from a cadet branch of the Lu clan of Fanyang. He had come up through the Classics examination and had served three years as Commandant of a capital county, and his face had the weariness of a man who'd been cured in the smoke of paperwork. He looked at Shen You, then at the string of people trailing behind him: an old farmer, a woman, a ward warden, a man with a goatee holding a sheaf of documents, and a maid with her eyes meekly lowered.

"Well, you've brought the whole lot," Commandant Lu said. "Liu Qi, you first."

Liu Qi stepped forward and laid it out point by point, neither hurrying nor dragging.

He made his case well.

The monastic registers of the Bureau of Sacrifices, every temple in the capital, last year's and this year's—he'd had people go through them scroll by scroll. There was no Shen You. Nor was there the teacher's Dharma name Shen You had given when he was registered: Huiming. When Shen You was registered, the county had relied solely on a document he'd brought with him. It had not checked with the Bureau of Sacrifices, nor sent a dispatch to Yizhou. At present there were hundreds, even thousands, of returned monks being put on the registers in Chang'an, the genuine and the dubious all mixed together. Without a careful inquiry, that was as good as holding the door open for scoundrels.

"This man has been registered for no more than eight days," Liu Qi said in closing, "and already he has acquired a residence, opened a shop and two workshops, and keeps dozens of servants at his command. The source of his wealth is unclear. I submit that he should first be taken into custody, and a dispatch sent to Yizhou to verify his original registration. Once the facts are established, the matter can be considered further."

When he had finished, he stepped back and stood with his hands lowered.

The hall was very quiet. The clerks along both sides stole glances at Shen You.

Commandant Lu rubbed between his brows and looked at Shen You too. "What do you have to say?"

***

"Every word Mr. Liu said is true," Shen You said.

Liu Qi's eyelid twitched.

"The Bureau of Sacrifices really doesn't have me," Shen You said. "Because the place where I took my vows was never on the Bureau of Sacrifices' registers in the first place."

He told them where that hermitage had come from. East side of Kaiming Ward; a small, unchartered hermitage; an old monk with three disciples. He was the one who spent most of the year off copying sutras. The culling in the fifth month; the hermitage torn down, the old monk dead of illness, the disciples dispersed.

"Warden Zhao."

Warden Zhao stepped forward and handed up the ledger sheet. "*Kaiming Ward: one hermitage, unchartered; three monks; dispersed.* The seventeenth day of the fifth month. It was this humble one who led the county men there to tear it down."

Commandant Lu took the ledger sheet and glanced at it, and his expression shifted. The seal on that ledger sheet was the seal of Wannian County.

"Mistress Liu."

Sister Liu came forward trembling and told the story of the old monk. When she got to the part about him sitting in the doorway and crying all day, she started wiping her own eyes first.

"Elder."

The old farmer stepped out, rubbing his hands together, not knowing what to do with them, but he spoke very clearly. "The evening of the ninth day of the eighth month, I saw this young master out in the waste ground in Kaiming Ward. Hair about an inch long. I took him for a little monk who'd been run out of his temple, and I told him to go spend the night in the ruined temple to the east. That same noon, the officials had just posted the notice of the general amnesty."

When he had finished, he added, "My eldest is in the militia. I don't tell lies."

Commandant Lu listened, his fingers tapping on the desk, one beat at a time.

Liu Qi hadn't said a word the whole while. Only when the old farmer had stepped back did he speak, his voice as even as before.

"Warden Zhao. On the seventeenth day of the fifth month, among the three people you dispersed—was he one of them?"

Warden Zhao opened his mouth, glanced at Shen You, glanced at Commandant Lu, and answered honestly, "That day there was only two at the temple. The third, Old Master said he was off copying sutras. The number was what Old Master gave."

"Mistress Liu, did you ever see this disciple who copied sutras?"

Sister Liu thought about it for a long time, then shook her head. "I saw him, a time or two... I can't remember his face."

"In other words," Liu Qi said, "there is not one person in the ward who can identify him as a member of that hermitage."

The hall went still for a moment.

"These past years since I came to the capital, I've mostly been off copying sutras for people. I didn't get back but a few times a year," Shen You said. "It's no surprise the people in the ward don't remember me. If you don't believe it, Mr. Liu, you're welcome to go door to door and ask whether anyone remembers what that sutra-copying disciple looked like."

Liu Qi nodded, as though he'd expected that answer all along.

"Three monks at the hermitage. Young Master Shen says he is the one no one can recognize. The other two—where are they now?"

Every eye in the hall turned to Shen You.

That was the killer question. Find the other two, ask them once, "Was there a Shen You among your brother disciples?" and the whole thing would come apart.

"One went back to Puzhou, one went into the Zhongnan Mountains." Shen You's answer was perfectly calm. "We were brothers once, and now we've all gone our separate ways. I think about them too. If you do find them, Mr. Liu, give them my regards."

Liu Qi looked at him for a while and didn't press it.

Puzhou lay far away in Hedong, and the Zhongnan Mountains were a thousand peaks and ten thousand ravines; no one was finding anybody there anytime soon. He knew it, and so did Shen You. He had thought about that document, too. But whether its seal was real or fake, only Yizhou could say, and that meant two months there and back.

He tried another direction. "Even if this man truly did come out of a hermitage, private ordination is itself a crime under the code."

"You know the code, Mr. Liu." Shen You turned to him. "So tell me, Mr. Liu: in what year and what month did this private ordination take place?"

Liu Qi fell silent.

"The ninth day of the eighth month, the new emperor took the throne and declared a general amnesty for all under Heaven," Shen You said, tilting his head toward Zhou One.

Zhou One stepped forward and presented a sheet of paper with both hands. It was the amnesty edict he had copied from the ward gate that morning, word for word.

"'*All crimes, grave or minor, whether already discovered or not yet discovered, are hereby pardoned,*'" Shen You said. "Private ordination isn't one of the Ten Abominations. What came before the amnesty has been pardoned."

"After the amnesty, I was registered as a monk returned to lay life. I went through Wannian County's door, and it was Wannian County's seal that went on my papers."

He looked at Commandant Lu, his tone perfectly mild.

"Mr. Liu says the county was careless in registering me. I'd say the county did nothing wrong."

Commandant Lu's fingers went still.

He had understood that line perfectly well.

If Shen You was a fraud, then Wannian County, which had put him on the register, had been muddled and negligent. The clerk would be flogged, and he himself, as Commandant, would not come out clean either.

If Shen You was genuine, then Wannian County had acted according to the code. Registered after the amnesty; not a thing wrong with it.

Commandant Lu looked at Shen You, then at Liu Qi standing off to one side, and said nothing.

Liu Qi said nothing either. His gaze rested for a moment on Shen You's right hand, hanging at his side, then lifted again, and for the first time those faint eyes held something else.

***

"There's one more thing," Shen You went on. "I'm not sure whether I ought to bring it up here."

"Speak."

"Mr. Liu says the source of my wealth is unclear," Shen You said. "Shen's, in the East Market—I'm sure the Commandant has heard of it."

Commandant Lu nodded. Just the day before yesterday, his wife had sent someone to stand in that line.

"Tomorrow, the twenty-first day of the eighth month, the palace holds the investiture of the empress," Shen You said. "The white sugar the Dou family is procuring for the palace comes out of my sugar works."

Along both sides of the hall, the clerks exchanged looks.

Slowly, Commandant Lu's brows drew together.

He was a County Commandant, not a fool. On the day before the investiture, lock up the man supplying sugar for the palace banquet in the county jail, seal his house, shut down his sugar works. If tomorrow the palace banquet came up one dish of sugar short, the Dou family would come asking, the palace would come asking—and at the end of all that asking, who would they end up asking?

And all of it over a single informant's report from a hanger-on. A hanger-on with the Pei family behind him.

Commandant Lu ran the numbers in his head.

He looked at Liu Qi, and his tone cooled. "Liu Qi, you have informed against this man for fraudulently claiming monastic registration. Do you have any actual proof that he forged official documents?"

Liu Qi was silent a moment, then cupped his hands. "Not at present."

"Then it is suspicion without proof."

Commandant Lu took up his brush and wrote a few lines on the writ.

"*Shen You: came out of a hermitage; privately ordained before the amnesty, registered after the amnesty; no impediment under the code. As to his stated original registration in Yizhou, a dispatch shall be sent to Yizhou for verification. Pending verification, Shen You shall not leave the capital.*"

He set down the brush and looked at Liu Qi.

"The accusation is unfounded. Since the suspicion was not without cause, no further action will be taken." He paused. "A false accuser takes the punishment he sought for another, Liu Qi. You're a man who knows the code. Before you file your next report, think it through."

Liu Qi lowered his head and bowed deeply. "I am instructed."

***

By the time they came out of the county office, the sun was already slanting west.

On the streets of Xuanyang Ward, the gong from the East Market came drifting over from afar, stroke after stroke, sounding the close of the market.

Shen You was about to climb into the carriage when someone behind him called out.

It was Liu Qi.

He came up close and cupped his hands, his face as faint and mild as ever. "Well played, Young Master Shen."

"Well played yourself, Mr. Liu."

"I spent three days going through the Bureau of Sacrifices," Liu Qi said. "The one thing I never expected was that you'd be willing to admit to private ordination. When did it occur to you?"

"This morning. In the bath."

Liu Qi stared for a beat, then laughed—very softly—and shook his head.

"Young Master Shen," he said, lowering his voice, "the Pei family has lost this round. They won't come through the county's door again."

"Then which door will they use?"

Liu Qi didn't answer. He only gave him a look, then turned and walked away.

***

In the carriage, Qingluo said nothing for a long time.

They were nearly back to Kaiming Ward when she suddenly spoke, softly. "Just now in the hall, the Master said the sugar for the palace banquet was ours. Has Dou the Eleventh really taken the palace's order?"

"He has." Shen You leaned back against the side of the carriage, eyes closed. "Only it wasn't settled until last night."

"...Then how did the Master know the Commandant would believe it?"

"He doesn't have to believe it," Shen You said. "He just has to be afraid."

Qingluo looked at him. She looked at him for a long time.

The carriage swayed along. The wind lifted a corner of the curtain, and the setting sun slipped in through the gap and fell across his face.

"One more thing." Shen You opened his eyes. "About Yizhou. When we get back, tell Zhou One to pick two people. They leave tomorrow morning, the moment the city gates open."

"What are they going to Yizhou for?"

"Chengdu County." Shen You smiled. "To fill in a page of household registration for me."

***

By the time they got home, it was nearly dark.

The lanterns in the front courtyard were all lit, their glass shades glowing a warm yellow. The smell of Sichuan pepper and mutton drifted over from the back courtyard, and Zhou Three's booming voice carried through two walls.

Shen You didn't go inside. He went straight through to the back courtyard.

Under the pomegranate tree lay a new little round rug, one from Kang Satuo's shipment: a scarlet ground woven with gold-thread roundels, only about two chi across.

A'na was standing on the rug.

She had changed into a costume for the Hu whirling dance: a narrow-sleeved, crimson short robe, a brocade sash at her waist hung all over with little bronze bells, and below it a full, flowing pomegranate-red skirt, its hem embroidered with scrolling leaves in gold thread. Her chestnut braids had all been let loose and fell down her back, gleaming like honey in the lamplight. Her feet were bare, and a string of silver bells circled one ankle.

Chen Four sat off to one side, holding a *jiegu* drum.

The drum started, and A'na began to turn.

Slowly at first, the skirt opening out bit by bit, like a flower coming into bloom. The drumbeats came faster and faster, and she spun faster and faster, until the whole skirt flew up and the gold scrolling leaves blurred into ring after ring of light. The bronze bells at her waist and the silver bells at her ankle rang together, *ting-ting, dang-dang*, like a sudden burst of rain. And the whole time she stayed on that little round rug, barely two chi across, without one step straying off it.

The whole courtyard stood there stunned. Even Zhou Four, leaning against a veranda pillar, couldn't take his eyes off her.

The last drumbeat fell. A'na stopped dead. Her skirt was still settling slowly around her as she lifted her face and smiled at Shen You, her amber eyes shining, a fine sheen of sweat on her forehead.

Shen You clapped, and clapped hard.

"Wonderful!"

A'na laughed and said something in a rush of Sogdian. Zheng One, beside her, translated: "She says that back home, she was the best dancer in the whole city."

"I believe it."

He stretched out in the rocking chair. Qingluo handed him a bowl of freshly brewed plain tea, and A'xing brought over a dish of steamed sugared milk curd.

The night wind was cool, and the leaves of the pomegranate tree rustled.

Of the ten-day deadline, three days were left.

***

At that same moment, in the northeast of Chang'an, in the side hall of a great mansion.

Pei Shisan sat in a lower seat, with Liu Qi standing behind him.

In the seat of honor sat a man of about fifty, dressed in an everyday round-collared robe, though the cloth was the finest Shu brocade. Between his fingers he held a sugar cake, snow-white, in exactly the style of Shen's in the East Market.

He listened as Liu Qi told him everything that had happened at the county office, then slowly set the sugar cake back down on its dish.

"The amnesty," he said. "This Shen You even put the new emperor's general amnesty to use."

"Yes," Pei Shisan said. "The road through the county is closed."

The man didn't respond. His fingers tapped lightly on the table.

"A monk back in lay life," he said. "Twelve days. From a ruined temple to a house, a shop, a sugar works, a winery, and dozens of people."

"No one behind him?"

"We've looked into it," Pei Shisan said. "No one. Dou the Eleventh does business with him, but Dou the Eleventh is just a concubine's son living under someone else's roof."

The man smiled.

"No one behind him. That makes it easy."

He picked up the sugar cake again and took another look at it.

"Something this good," he said, "shouldn't be in the hands of a monk."
