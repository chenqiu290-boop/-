# Chapter 16: The White Horse

At the Hour of the Dog, the ward gates were locked for the night.

When Zhou Four brought the message, Zheng Three was squatting under the veranda, working out tomorrow's fodder for twenty oxen. She heard him out, set her counting rods down on the ground, and went to the back courtyard.

Shen You had just come out of the hot pool. His hair was still dripping, and he lay on his stomach on a bamboo couch, wrapped in a robe of fine cloth, while Qingluo knelt beside him wringing his hair dry with a cloth, one lock at a time.

"How far down does the Master want it counted?"

"Down to a single bolt."

Zheng Three's thumb wound once around the cord of her money pouch. Then once more.

"I can't take counting rods in there," she said. "The gate of the Left Treasury is crawling with soldiers of the Gate Guard. A commoner woman squatting at the treasury door laying out counting rods—they won't wait for me to finish. I'll be the first one they grab."

She turned and went to the kitchen to ask Sanniang for beans. Sanniang planted both floury hands on her hips. "A handful of soybeans grinds down to two bowls of bean curd!" Grumbling all the while, she grabbed three handfuls, wrapped them in a cloth, and slapped the bundle into Zheng Three's palm.

Back in the rear courtyard, Zheng Three ran through it once in front of Shen You: yellow soybeans for silk, black beans for brocade and figured silk, red adzuki beans for gold and silver. One bean per cart, moved from the left sleeve into the right. The beans were only a backup for her head, in case she lost count.

"And if someone asks what you're counting?"

"Carting fees."

Shen You pushed himself halfway up off the couch, took one look at her, and burst out laughing.

Zhou One had been standing by the screen the whole time. Only now did he take half a step forward, giving his goatee a single tug. "The drivers. I'd like to swap out three. The goods are going northwest, across the river. Where people speak Turkic, someone has to listen. The ruts and hoofprints on the road, someone has to read. The Turks' horses, someone has to know."

"Zheng One, Feng Five, Cao One," Shen You said.

Zhou One's hand stopped on his beard.

"What you were thinking, I thought of too." Shen You lay back down and rested his chin on the backs of his hands. "And add Chen Five. Just in case."

***

At the Hour of the Rat, twenty-one more.

Seven Shuis, seven Dous, seven Zhangs.

Zhou One questioned them one by one outside the sleeping hall. When he got to the Dous, his brush stopped in midair. "Master, this surname..."

"If it's in the *Hundred Family Surnames*, it goes in line." Behind the bed curtains, Shen You yawned. "Someday when the Eleventh comes over, he can meet his clansmen."

One poled ferries: Shui One. One mended bows: Dou Three. One played polo: Zhang One, who said, "On horseback, the ball grows on the end of my stick." And one herded sheep: Zhang Five—the grassland way, one man, one dog, a thousand sheep.

Zhou One's brush drew a line under "herded sheep."

Two hundred and thirty-one.

***

The twenty-ninth day of the eighth month. The start of the Hour of the Dragon, outside Hanguang Gate.

A hundred carts stood in a line that ran from Hanguang Gate all the way west to the corner of the street, hired by Chang'an and Wannian Counties. Some of the oxen were yellow, some black; some of the carts were new, some old. The drivers squatted by their shafts gnawing sesame flatbreads, and not one of them knew what they'd be hauling today. At the very front were the Shen house's twenty carts. Twenty oxen, every one the same height. Twenty drivers in matching short tunics of undyed coarse cloth, and not one of them squatting. Zheng Three sat on the shaft of the lead cart, with Zheng One beside her driving.

At the middle of the Hour of the Dragon, Hanguang Gate opened.

Inside the Imperial City, yellow dirt gave way to brick, and the wheels went *clack-clack* over it. The Left Treasury lay behind the Court of the Treasury, its walls a good stretch higher than anything around it, halberds planted along the top. The treasury door was thick elm sheathed in iron. Three men with three keys fitted them one at a time, then pushed together, and out rolled the smell of old camphorwood, with a little mildew in it.

Treasury porters began carrying out the silk. Behind a table by the door, one clerk called out the count while another ticked it off in a register.

"Silk: twenty bales, forty bolts to a bale, eight hundred bolts in all. Lead cart."

A soybean in Zheng Three's left sleeve moved into her right.

She was sitting high enough to see the ink stamps on the ends of the bales. *Anxi County, Dingzhou. Cloth levy, sixth year of Wude.* *Xiaqiu County, Yanzhou. Cloth levy, seventh year of Wude.* And one bale was stamped *Chengdu County, Yizhou*. Zheng Three gave that one a second look. On paper, that was where the Master's original registration was.

The Shen house's twenty carts were loaded in less than half a double-hour: eighteen carts of silk, two of brocade and figured silk. Behind them the other carts came up one after another, and the clerk called out one count after another. *Silk, eight hundred bolts. Silk, eight hundred bolts. Brocade, one hundred lengths; figured silk, four hundred bolts...*

Somewhere past the fiftieth cart, what came out changed. Not bales. Black lacquer chests, carried over from the courtyard gate of the Right Treasury next door, their lids pasted with the Court of the Treasury's seal strips. Four men to a chest, and the carrying poles bowed under them. The clerk's voice dropped a notch too.

"Gold and silver vessels: eight chests, one hundred sixty-two articles."

Each article was a single piece. A red bean moved into her right sleeve.

Two carts after that, the chests were smaller, and the poles bowed even worse than before.

"Gold: one thousand taels."

Inside her sleeve, Zheng Three's hand went still.

"Silver: five thousand taels."

Nothing showed on her face. She just stared straight at that chest and didn't blink for a long while. Zheng One turned his head and looked at her. In the ten-odd days they'd worked together, it was the first time he had ever seen her stunned.

***

"You. Woman."

The clerk with the register had risen from behind his table. He was about thirty, with dark hollows under his eyes and cuffs worn fuzzy at the edges. He walked to the lead cart and tipped his face up at her. "Whose house are you from? Something in your sleeves has been moving the whole time. What is it?"

Two soldiers of the Gate Guard looked over. Zheng One's hand rested on the ox's back. He didn't move.

Zheng Three climbed down off the shaft and made a folded-hands salute. "The Shen house of Kaiming Ward. This humble one keeps the accounts." She took a handful of beans from her sleeve and spread them on her palm, yellow, black, and red. "I'm counting carts. The county hired us, and the carting fee goes by the cart. But hauling silk is one price, and hauling gold ought to be another. When we get back, I'll need to have a word with the county about it."

"Hauling gold, and you want a bigger fee for it?"

"The ox doesn't know it's hauling gold," Zheng Three said. "All it knows is heavy."

The clerk opened his mouth. Behind him, an old porter carrying a chest laughed first—laughed so hard the pole shook—then quickly straightened his face. The clerk turned and glared at him. When he turned back, whatever menace had been in his face was gone. He watched the sealed chest being lifted onto a cart.

"Count, then." His voice was so low only Zheng Three could hear it. "Count it right, and at least someone will know how much went out this time."

The old porter came back after loading the chest. As he passed Zheng Three, he wiped the sweat off with his sleeve and muttered, "That time in the fifth year of Wude, I carried it out too. That time, it wasn't this much."

***

Early in the Hour of the Horse, all hundred carts were loaded. An official in a crimson robe came over from the direction of the Court of the Treasury, about fifty, and put his signature mark at the end of the register. He stood a while with his hands clasped behind his back, looking at the last cart. He said nothing, and left.

The carts went out through Anfu Gate and made west for Kaiyuan Gate. Before they turned north, Zheng Three got down. She wasn't going out of the city with the carts; that had been settled long before.

"A hundred carts. They're all in here." She patted her right sleeve. "When that crimson robe left, he said something to the clerk: 'first batch.' Whether there's more after this, I couldn't hear."

Zheng One nodded and shook out the reins. Zheng Three stood at the foot of the wall and watched the twenty carts lead the eighty behind them around the corner. Then, the handful of beans clutched in her fist, she went out through Hanguang Gate and headed south.

***

The Hour of the Goat, the back courtyard.

Shen You lay in the rocking chair with both feet soaking in a wooden tub, a few mugwort leaves floating on the water. Kneeling beside the tub was Qi Five, the one who did feet, who'd arrived a few days earlier. His hands were small and steady, and the little knife in them was as narrow as a willow leaf. He skimmed it along the toe, and a layer of white skin curled away and dropped. It didn't hurt at all. It tickled.

Shen You's toes curled.

"Master, hold still."

"It tickles."

"Tickles or not, hold still."

Shen You held in a laugh, and his toes curled again. Under the veranda, Qingluo was brewing tea. A'na sat on the steps tuning her strings—tune a string, steal a glance at that foot.

That was when Zheng Three came into the courtyard. She didn't stand to report. She squatted in front of the rocking chair, emptied the beans from both sleeves onto the gray bricks, and set them out one by one: eighty-two soybeans, ten black beans, eight red beans.

"A hundred carts."

Shen You glanced down at the bricks without sitting up. "Go on."

"Silk, eighty-two carts, eight hundred bolts a cart: sixty-five thousand six hundred bolts. Brocade and figured silk, ten carts: a thousand lengths of brocade, four thousand bolts of figured silk. Gold and silver vessels, six carts: forty-eight chests, nine hundred seventy-four articles." Her fingertip moved to the last two red beans and stopped there a moment. "Gold, one thousand taels. Silver, five thousand taels."

A'na's hand pressed flat on her strings. Qi Five's little knife stopped too.

"At West Market prices from before martial law, silk was three hundred fifty coins a bolt, give or take. Twenty-three thousand strings. Call the brocade and figured silk ten thousand. The gold and silver vessels are palace work; there's no market price for them, so I'll leave them out for now. Leaving out the gold and silver, thirty-three thousand strings."

"And what can thirty-three thousand strings do?"

"One person's food for a day is twenty coins," Zheng Three said. "If from today not one more coin came into this house, and we lived on nothing but those thirty-three thousand strings, they'd feed us to the two hundred and fourteenth day. On that day, there'd be twenty-three thousand and five people in this house."

Zhou One stood by a veranda pillar. The hand that had been tugging his beard went still.

Shen You looked at the beans on the bricks, row after row. Yesterday on the slope, the emperor had raised one hand, and tens of thousands of men had stood without a single one moving. At the time he'd thought that wave was magnificent. Magnificent it was. It turned out this was what had paid for it.

All at once he laughed and threw himself back, sinking deep into the rocking chair, laughing so hard the chair rocked back and forth. Half a bowl's worth of water sloshed out of the tub, all over Qi Five's knees.

"Thirty-three thousand strings, handed to Jieli, hauled back to the grassland, and doled out in front of the khan's tent at two bolts a head." He sat up straight, his foot still in Qi Five's hands. "Rather than give this money to the Turks, they might as well give it to me."

***

Zhou One glanced toward the courtyard gate first. It was shut. Only then did he step over beside the rocking chair. "The Master means..."

"Send for Ashina," Shen You said.

Ashina had been in the stable since early morning, helping Feng Three change the dressings on the injured horses. When he came, there was still chaff stuck to his sleeves.

"You said that day the Tang would come with silk to pay him to go home. What went out today was a hundred carts."

The bone thumb ring turned half a circle on Ashina's thumb and stopped. "A hundred carts, I never hear of."

"Once it's on the grassland, how is it shared out? And on the road?"

"The khan chooses first. Then the shads and the tegins choose. What is left after, that is each family's two bolts," Ashina said. "With all this, the camels walk slow. Back to the khan's camp is one month. They walk behind the main host, and the khan's *fuli* watch them. *Fuli*, it means wolf. The soldiers around the khan."

"And at night?"

It was Zheng Six, squatting by the steps, who spoke first, a stalk of grass between his teeth. "At night, they've got to get off their horses and sleep."

Ashina looked at Zheng Six and gave one nod. "The baggage, in the middle of the circle. The men sleep outside the baggage. The horse herds, outermost, grazing, and the herders watch them. A horse must graze all night, or next day it cannot run."

Shen You took his foot back out of Qi Five's hands and set it on the cloth, wriggling all ten toes.

"Zhou One, whatever you were about to say just now, say it."

"When the court sends gifts, it's to seal a treaty," Zhou One said. "Once the treaty's sealed, if the Turks lose anything inside Tang territory, the first thing out of Jieli's mouth will be that the Tang broke faith. He'll turn around and come back, and we'll have the Wei River all over again."

A'na took her hand off the strings.

"So it can't be the Tang that took it." Shen You looked at Ashina. "Did Tuli come too?"

"He comes. Yesterday on the slope, I see another standard, to the east. The lesser khan's men and the khan's men, they never camp in one place."

"A hundred carts go into Jieli's tent. What does Tuli get?"

"What the khan gives him."

"And he's happy with that?"

Ashina didn't answer. The bone thumb ring started turning again. Once around, then once again.

Shen You leaned forward a little. "Then let me put it another way. If Jieli loses something in the night, who's the first man he suspects?"

Ashina looked at him. Looked at him a long time.

"...Tuli."

At last Zhou One's tug on his beard came all the way down. "Then we need something of Tuli's. What do the Turks trust?"

"The golden arrow. When the khan calls up men, calls up horses, he sends one arrow. The head is gold, sealed with wax, with a mark in it. The lesser khan sends them also. Every man on the grassland, he has seen one."

"What does the mark look like?"

"I do not know."

"Feng One can cast the head. There's gold in the house. I can do the wax seal," Zhou One said. "The mark, no. What I haven't seen, I don't make."

"Feng Five went with the carts to the river," Shen You said. "He looks at everything."

He put his foot back in the tub. The water had gone lukewarm. He tipped his chin at Qi Five. "Other one."

The willow-leaf knife skimmed along the toes of the other foot.

"Ashina, you know the road north?"

"Coming, they take the Jingzhou road. Going back, most likely the same. Along the Jing River, past Binzhou, then north."

"Lead the way for me."

The thumb ring stopped. "The Turks know my face."

"All the better," Shen You said. "When somebody yells in Turkic in the dark, it has to be someone who sounds more Turkic than the Turks."

Ashina stood there a good while. A piece of chaff dropped from his sleeve onto the bricks.

"I go."

***

At the Hour of the Monkey, Kang Satuo came through the gate.

The gatekeeper showed him into the gatehouse. He'd taken just one sip of tea when he was asked to the back courtyard. Twenty years in the West Market, more houses than he could count, and this was the first time he'd ever been invited into the back courtyard to watch the master of the house have his feet washed.

Shen You didn't get up. He lifted a hand. "Mr. Kang. Sit."

Kang Satuo looked at the wooden tub, and the whiskers at the corners of his mouth twitched. "Young Master Shen, the whole West Market is saying that yesterday, on a slope by the Wei, some young master sat in a folding armchair shelling chestnuts and watched a fine show."

"I watched," Shen You said. "All it was missing was a melon."

Kang Satuo laughed out loud—*ha, ha*—and the string of glass beads at his waist clicked softly. "You didn't send for me today about sugar."

"About horses. The Hu merchants in the West Market who came into the city before martial law can't get out again. Their camel trains and horse trains are piled up in the lodging houses, and alfalfa's gone from thirty coins a bundle to a hundred. They eat it day after day."

"Can't sleep for worrying," Kang Satuo said.

"Good horses—as many as there are. Camels too." Shen You thought a moment. "Good legs on them. And ones that don't make noise at night."

Kang Satuo's tea bowl stopped at his lips.

A single breath.

He set the bowl down and asked nothing at all.

"Tomorrow afternoon I'll drive them over. Eighty or ninety horses, twenty camels. I'll beat the price down for you—eleven hundred strings all told, give or take. I'll front the money myself. Later, once the road west opens up, you can take it out of the Snow Sugar you send me."

"Deal."

Kang Satuo didn't get up. He fingered the glass beads at his waist, moving them along one at a time.

"There's one more thing. Even if Young Master Shen doesn't ask, I'll say it. The moment the Turks pull back and martial law is lifted, every camel train in the West Market is going to pour out at once. Kaiyuan Gate, Jinguang Gate—hundreds of camels a day going out, and hundreds of men driving and guarding them. The soldiers at the gates won't even be able to count the camels," he said. "They won't be able to count the men."

He hadn't asked a thing. And he'd said everything.

"Mr. Kang," Shen You said slowly, "what do you want?"

"The same as before. When you come back, any good horses, I get the first look."

Shen You leaned back and smiled.

On his way out, Kang Satuo took the side passage by the kitchen. Sanniang and several women were baking sesame flatbreads. He didn't slow his step, but his eyes swept over it all: twenty to a basket, twenty-one or twenty-two baskets in all. By the time his cart was out the ward gate, he'd finished the sum. Two apiece a meal—two hundred people, give or take. And that was only those eating supper in this one house.

He clutched that string of glass beads in his fist all the way home.

***

At the Hour of the Rat, twenty-two more.

Seven Yuns, seven Sus, seven Pans. The last one was Ge One.

One tanned hides: Su Three. One read the stars: Yun Five, who said, "The northern sky—I don't miss a single one." One cut and dressed hair: Pan Four.

Ge One's skill was raising camels.

Zhou One set down his brush and stood outside the screen for a good while. "Master, Kang Satuo said twenty camels, arriving tomorrow afternoon. Ge One arrived tonight."

Behind the bed curtains, Shen You rolled over, laughed once, and fell asleep.

Two hundred and fifty-three.

***

The thirtieth day of the eighth month. The Hour of the Dragon, the south end of the Bian Bridge.

The carts had reached the camp at the end of the Hour of the Monkey yesterday. The goods hadn't been unloaded, and soldiers had watched them all night. Cao One slept beside the oxen. Zheng One woke once in the middle of the night and heard him talking to them.

At first light, Feng Five squatted by a cartwheel and looked toward the north bank. The great camp was still there, but the horse herds had moved back four or five li, grazing in patch after patch.

"They're leaving," Feng Five said. "Yesterday evening, when we got to camp, their horses stood without eating, heads toward the river. That's waiting to charge. Today they've had their heads down eating all morning. That's getting ready for the road."

Halfway through the Hour of the Dragon, drums sounded on the south bank. The emperor came out of the camp. The day before yesterday, at the river, it had been six riders; this time it was more than a hundred, and the lead rider was on a white horse. On the far bank, the great standard with the golden wolf head came down onto the river flat. The two sides each halted at the middle of the Bian Bridge, several zhang apart.

A white horse was led out onto the bridge.

It was brought over from the north bank. Very tall, white all over without a single stray hair, its mane braided and wound with red silk. In the middle of the bridge, all four hooves planted themselves on the planks and its neck hauled backward. The man leading it pulled twice and couldn't budge it.

"That's not the one the emperor rides, is it?" A driver from the next carts over was standing with his mouth open.

"No," Cao One said, his eyes fixed on the horse. "Big head, short neck, small hooves. Raised on the grassland."

Someone stepped forward in the middle of the bridge. It was too far to see who. All they saw was a flash of light, one glint in the sun.

The white horse screamed. Shrill, long, carrying from the bridge into the camp. In the camp, a hundred oxen raised their heads at once. A few backed up, wrenching their cart shafts askew.

Then the horse went down. Its four legs kicked a few times against the planks of the bridge, then didn't kick anymore.

Someone in the middle of the bridge carried something in both hands and made a circuit between the men of both sides. At two hundred-odd paces, Zheng One couldn't hear what was said. All he saw was the Turks on the far bank raising their bows to the sky, one shout after another, tens of thousands of voices joining in.

"What are they shouting?" Chen Five asked.

Zheng One listened a while. "'Khan.' They're not shouting for the emperor. They're shouting for their own khan."

Cao One turned away and didn't watch anymore. He squatted down and pressed his face to the neck of the lead cart's ox. After a long while, he said:

"It knows."

Nobody answered.

***

At the Hour of the Snake, the order came from the camp: carts across the bridge.

The deck was wooden planks that creaked under the wheels. Below, the Wei ran yellow and murky, shoving eastward. On both sides, Tang soldiers' spears stood one after another all the way to the north end of the bridge. The Shen house's lead cart was first onto the bridge. As Zheng One passed the middle, he glanced down. The white horse had been carried away. On the planks was a great dark-red patch, seeping down through the cracks between the boards, dripping into the water, and gone the instant it touched.

The river flat on the north bank was nothing but Turks—hide robes and felt caps, the reek of mutton fat mixed with horse sweat. Someone reached out to touch a binding rope and got a riding whip across the back of the hand from his headman. Nothing that was unloaded went into the great camp. It was hauled off to the northeast, where a line of camels knelt on the ground, being loaded up one after another.

Zheng One sat on the shaft with his eyelids drooping, no different from any dozing carter.

His ears weren't asleep.

"The khan's. Hands off."

"Didn't they say everyone gets a share?"

"Back at the khan's camp, everyone gets a share."

"That's what they said last time." It was a young one who muttered it. The old Turk beside him gave him a kick.

"The lesser khan's people over on the east end were arguing all morning," another said. "Saying they put up the most horses, and lost the most men too."

"Whatever the khan gives him, that's what he gets."

"And how much does he get?"

Nobody answered.

Feng Five squatted by a cartwheel pretending to look at the axle, his eyes on the ground. Camel tracks: big, round, split in two, heading northeast, pressed deeper the farther back they went. Every horse had a brand on its rump. The herd here on the river flat all bore one brand. The few dozen riders standing far off at the east end, watching the excitement, bore another: a hook, with two short bars beneath it. Those few dozen riders watched a while, then turned and left. With one finger, Feng Five drew the brand once in the dirt under the cartwheel, looked at it, and rubbed it out.

Cao One had been watching the horse herds four or five li back from the river the whole time, going over them one herd after another. When he reached a herd off to the northeast, his eyes stopped. That herd was more unsettled than the others—a few mouthfuls of grass, then heads up, looking all around. Not one horse stood out in front.

"Zheng One," Cao One said. "The gray mare's herd. There. They're waiting for their lead mare. The lead mare's in our stable."

Halfway through the unloading, a troop of Tang cavalry came east along the river flat. At its head was a black horse, and the man on it carried a cavalry lance and had a voice like a struck gong. "Faster! The sun won't wait for you!"

The black horse drew level with the Shen house's lead cart and reined in. Painted along the shaft was a line of small characters: *Shen, Kaiming Ward.*

"Kaiming Ward. You lot again," the man on the horse said. "Where's your employer?"

Zheng One jumped down off the shaft and made a folded-hands salute. The night before last, the Master had told him: if you run into a general whose voice is like a gong, say this.

"At home. The Master says he promised the general—no more slopes."

The man on the horse stopped short. He rapped his riding whip once against his boot. "So he remembered." He looked again at the line of camels on the river flat, and the corners of his mouth turned down. "Once you're unloaded, get yourselves back to the south bank, and be quick about it."

The hoofbeats faded. Chen Five, standing beside the third cart, had kept his left hand under the cart bed the whole time. Only now did he take it out.

Early in the Hour of the Horse, a hundred empty carts came back to the south bank. Zheng One looked back once at the north bank. The camels were fully loaded now. String after string, they rose to their feet and set off northeast, followed by a troop of horsemen with bows on their backs.

Those camels walked slowly.

***

The thirtieth day of the eighth month. Afternoon, Kaiming Ward.

Eighty-seven Hu horses and twenty camels came in, led by Kang Satuo's Hu boys, with a few Gaochang and Kuchean merchants following behind to see them off. By the look of them, they'd sold too cheap, and were glad to have sold at all.

At the ward gate, Warden Zhao was counting again. Last time it had been twenty-four lame horses. This time he got into the forties and lost count, started over, reached sixty, and then a camel blocked his view completely and he lost it again.

Sister Liu stood by the well with a winnowing basket of jujubes. "Warden Zhao, what's the Shen house want with so many horses?"

"They're in trade, see. Trading folk buy horses for... for hauling goods, naturally," Warden Zhao said. "Sugar. Snow Sugar. Even the palace eats it."

Sister Liu decided that made sense, and also that something about it didn't. She tossed a handful of jujubes into her mouth and walked off chewing.

***

The horses and camels were penned on the open ground behind the east compound. It had been the Tian family's plot in the ward, just cleared of its millet; they'd rented it a few days before, and Feng Three had spent the morning putting up a wooden palisade around it. At the north end, Chu One drew three circles on the ground with a stick.

"The stampede team. Cao One leads it, with Ashina and Zhou Seven. Everyone who'll be shouting goes in this team. The haul team. The camels go with this team, and Ge One handles the camels. The rear guard. Zheng Six leads the archers." He looked up. "Three teams. Split within one double-hour, rejoin within one double-hour. No fires, no calls, no sound."

Feng Seven planted both hands on her hips. "No sound? Then how do we keep in step?"

"You tell me."

Feng Seven thought about it, turned, and left. A while later she came back with a coil of hemp rope in her arms, tied with a knot at every pace.

"We hold on and walk. One tug means go, two means stop, three in a row means scatter. My beat doesn't need calling out loud. You follow the rope."

Chu One looked at the coil of rope for a while and gave one nod. That was all the praise there was going to be. Feng Seven was so pleased she clapped her hands—twice—then remembered "no sound" and put them down again.

That afternoon, several dozen people held the rope and walked with their eyes shut. At first they trod on each other's heels and tangled into a knot, and Feng Seven stamped her feet and rushed over to straighten them out one by one. By the sixth run: two tugs, stop. Three tugs, and they all scattered at once and crouched, without a sound.

At the other end of the ground, Dou Three sat repairing the assorted bows bought at the West Market, a dozen-odd of them stacked beside him. Making a good bow took three years, he said. Mending them, he could do twenty a night. Feng One's clay forge hadn't gone out since last night.

In the medicine room, Zhou Six was grinding henbane seeds, a cloth tied over her face.

"How much to put one down?" Shen You stuck his head in at the door.

"To put down one Turkic man," Zhou Six said without looking up, "takes two times more than to put down the Master."

Shen You rubbed his nose and withdrew.

He got on a horse too. Feng Three picked him a chestnut, the gentlest-tempered of the lot. Shen You sprang for the saddle and didn't make it. He sprang again, ended up lying across the horse's back on his belly, and then swung one leg over. Feng Three steadied him, his face set very tight. Zhou Four stood three paces off, his face set very tight too.

The horse walked, and Shen You jolted along with it, tipping left, then tipping right. Halfway around, the horse abruptly stopped and lowered its head to crop a mouthful of millet stubble. Shen You hauled on the reins. It didn't budge.

Among the several dozen walking blind on the rope, somebody couldn't hold it in—a snort of laughter.

Feng Seven whipped around and glared. "No sound!"

Shen You laughed first himself, so hard he nearly slid off the horse, and Feng Three grabbed him. Back on the ground, hands on his hips, he waved at the horse, which was still cropping millet stubble.

"You know more than I do. You've got to eat your fill before you can run."

***

Early in the Hour of the Rooster, the carts came back.

Zheng One and the others came into the back courtyard covered in yellow dust. When Qingluo brought Shen You his tea, he waved it off and had her give them each a bowl first.

When Zheng One had drunk his, he reported everything he'd seen and heard on the bridge and around it, sentence by sentence, exactly as it had been. When he got to the lesser khan's people arguing all morning, Shen You's fingers stopped on the armrest.

Feng Five squatted down and drew the brand on the gray bricks with his finger: a hook, with two short bars beneath it.

"Those few dozen riders at the east end. This was on their horses' rumps."

Ashina glanced down at it. "Horses of Tuli's people. On the grassland, a family's horse brand, that is the family's mark. The wax seal on the arrow, it uses the same."

Zhou One stared at the brand for a long time, then gave Shen You one nod.

Cao One said only one thing: "The gray mare's herd is in among Jieli's herds. No lead mare."

Shen You leaned back, and the rocking chair creaked.

***

When it got dark, the lamps were lit under the veranda.

Chu One drew a map on the gray bricks: one horizontal line for the Wei, a slanting line for the Jing, Binzhou to the north. Farther north, and to the east, a thick vertical line.

"North of Binzhou, to the east, there's a great ridge. Several hundred li north to south, dense forest, ravines everywhere. Turkic horses in country like that are blind."

"The camels set off northeast at midday," Feng Five said. "The main host breaks camp tomorrow."

"With the baggage, fifty, sixty li a day. Not faster," Ashina said. "At night, still they camp all in one place."

"We trail them three or four days. Wait till they're far from the Wei and sleeping sound at night," Chu One said. "A hundred and twenty men, ninety-odd horses fit to ride, and the haul team walks with the camels. That's enough."

"And getting out of the city?"

Zhou One stepped forward. "Once martial law is lifted, Kang Satuo's camel train will be the first out of the city, heading west to Liangzhou. I'll write the travel passes. We go in two or three groups, with different camel trains, out through Kaiyuan and Jinguang Gates. A hundred-odd more men guarding camels, a hundred or so more horses—the soldiers at the gates won't be able to count them. A day west out of the city, then we turn north. The golden arrows will take two days. The mark will be cut from what Feng Five saw and Ashina recognized."

Shen You listened to each point and nodded at each one. But Zhou One didn't step back. He gave his beard a tug, and the tug didn't come all the way down.

"Master, Commandant Lu ruled that you may not leave the capital."

The veranda went quiet.

"I know," Shen You said. "The rule I set myself: crossing the Wei counts as leaving the capital."

"Then this trip..."

Shen You picked up his tea bowl, took a sip, and set it down on the armrest. "Who's in the camel trains—you write it up."

"Master—"

"I never said I was going."

Zhou One opened his mouth, and in the end said nothing. He bowed slightly and withdrew.

Zhou Four had been standing three paces off the whole time without moving. Only after Zhou One had gone did he speak. "This afternoon on the horse, the Master's legs tipped left eleven times and right nine times."

Shen You very nearly sprayed his tea.

***

The second watch. Every lamp in the east compound was lit.

Seven rows of thatched sheds had gone up, and five pits lay under plank covers. The sacks of millet on the planks had been moved aside tonight to clear the ground. At dusk, Shen You had said: "Tonight, call everyone back. I want to see."

They came in, group after group. The sugar boilers, the fire tenders, the brocade weavers came back from Baoning Ward before the ward gates closed. The people from the pastry shop came back, and the millet buyers. The household's eyes in the night came down from the tops of walls all over the city.

Nobody shouted. Feng Seven stood at the very front holding the end of the hemp rope, and wherever she stood, the people behind her stood in line with her—one row, two rows, three. Several coils of knotted rope were joined into one and passed back row by row, until it reached the last row and everyone had a length of it in hand.

The first three rows were the hundred and twenty Chu One had called by name. Some wore hide robes bought from the Hu merchants in the West Market and altered all afternoon by Su Three. Some carried bows Dou Three had mended. Some had Feng One's quivers at their waists, the new arrowheads showing a cold blue-green under the lamps. Zheng Six stood at the far right of the archers' row, half a head taller than the men beside him. The rows behind were the ones who, tomorrow, would go on boiling sugar, distilling liquor, weaving brocade, opening shop, buying millet, same as ever.

Cao One hadn't joined the ranks. He stood at the gate of the east compound with the gray mare beside him, her left forehoof still bound in cloth, her head down, nudging his hand again and again. Ashina stood at the edge of the ranks in hide robe and felt cap, the black-lacquered horn bow on his back. The bone thumb ring didn't turn once.

Shen You stood on the steps of the east compound and looked down.

He had been here twenty-two days. On the first day, in a ruined temple, there had been one Zhou One.

He had never seen them all standing in one place. Tonight was the first time. The lamplight fell from under the eaves of the sheds onto face after face, and didn't reach the last row. The people in the last row stood in the dark, nothing visible but their shapes, shoulder to shoulder, from one end of the east compound to the other.

Feng Seven turned her head and looked at Shen You.

Shen You nodded.

Feng Seven tugged the rope.

Two hundred and fifty-one people took one step forward together. Two more were, at that moment, on the road to Yizhou, filling in that page of his household registration.

One sound. The ground gave a dull thud beneath them. The lamps under the eaves swayed, the planks over the pits gave a low *hum*, and in the stable the dapple-gray threw up its head and snorted.

Then there was no sound at all.

Shen You looked at them. Looked at them for a long time. He opened his mouth, and in the end said only one thing.

"A hundred carts of goods, and the lead cart was ours."

Below the steps, no one made a sound.

"Thirty-three thousand strings. A thousand taels of gold. Five thousand taels of silver," Shen You said. "And a herd of horses waiting for their lead mare."

He tipped his chin at the gray mare beside Cao One. The gray mare lifted her head out of Cao One's hand and looked at him.

"Go get it back."
