# Setting Notes for Chapters 17–19

*A reader's guide to the background behind Chapters 17 to 19: the calendar, the paperwork, the road north, and what the Turks do on their way home. Nothing here is needed to follow the story, but it explains why things happen the way they do. Spoilers through Chapter 19. For terms, see the Glossary; for a one-page overview of the whole book so far, see "Setting Notes for New Readers"; for the chapters before these, see "Setting Notes for Chapters 9–16."*

---

## Where the story stands

At the end of Chapter 16 the treaty at the Bian Bridge has been sealed, the Tang has paid for it, and Shen You has 253 people. Martial law is still on. Shen You has bought 87 horses and 20 camels from Kang Satuo, and the household has been packing for a trip north, after horses.

Chapters 17 to 19 cover **a single day: Day 23, the first of the ninth month.** They run from the Hour of the Rat, as the eighth month ends, to the middle of the following night, in a dry gully north of the Wei River.

## The calendar, and the count

- **The eighth month has thirty days.** Chinese lunar months run twenty-nine or thirty days. In the book the eighth month of Wude 9 has thirty, so Day 22 is the thirtieth and **Day 23 is the first of the ninth month.** The old rule of thumb "Day N falls on day N + 8 of the eighth month" stops working here. In the ninth month, Day N falls on day N − 22. By the Western calendar this is late September 626.
- **The new day begins at the Hour of the Rat.** That is why Chapter 17 opens with "When the Hour of the Rat came, the eighth month was over." The summons that night is Day 23's: twenty-three people.
- **Running total: 276.** That is 23 × 24 ÷ 2. Zhou One counts it as "Two hundred and seventy-six, counting the two on the road to Yizhou": two of them are away on the road to Yizhou, the prefecture Shen You's papers say he comes from.
- **Who came on Day 23.** The surnames run in order, seven to a surname. Ge One arrived last on Day 22, so Day 23 brings the six remaining Ges (Ge Two to Ge Seven), seven Xis, seven Fans and the first three Pengs.
- **Why Shen You goes himself.** In Chapter 17 he spells out a rule of the system: each night's new people appear within ten paces of him, wherever he is. If he is in Kaiming Ward, they turn up in Kaiming Ward. If he is at the foot of the great ridge in the north, they turn up there, right beside the horses. The 120 people on the expedition can't drive a thousand horses home. The 130 who will be summoned from the second to the sixth of the month (24 + 25 + 26 + 27 + 28) can help. His second reason is simpler: "I want to see."
- **The sum in Chapter 19.** Zheng Three's beans in Chapter 16 put the treaty payment at about 33,000 strings. Shen You works out that, if nothing else came in, that money would feed his own household only until Day 214, when it would number 23,005.

## Chapter 17: Beyond the Pass

**The new deathsworn.** Zhou One takes down each newcomer's skill. Three of them matter at once. **Ge Four** cooks for an army in the field and knows how to make a fire that gives off no visible smoke. **Fan Two** does saddlery: she watches Shen You walk two steps, sees that his left leg is half a finger longer than his right, and alters his saddle overnight. That explains why he kept tipping left when he learned to ride. **Peng Three** cleans ears, which makes Shen You joke about opening a bathhouse. The others' skills include oil pressing, pot mending, basket weaving, well digging, dog raising and mat weaving.

**Zhou One's three conditions.** Shen You may go only as far as the foot of the great ridge, and must not enter the camp, join the fighting or give orders. He keeps the hat on by day. Zhou Four stays within ten paces of him. Shen You adds a fourth: when he's tired he rests, and nobody rushes him.

**The cover story at home.** Officially the Master has caught a chill, can't go out in the wind, and is seeing no one. If Dou the Eleventh calls, he will be told the Master is soaking in the hot pool for ten days.

**Deputy Commandant Liang's chit.** In Chapter 15 Shen You promised the general on the black horse, up on the slope, that Liang's chit for the wounded horses would reach the camp the next day. It didn't. Zhou One keeps a copy and sends the household's gatekeeper with the original as soon as the ward gate opens. It arrives two days late.

**The travel pass.** No one could travel without a *travel pass* (*guosuo*): a document listing every member of a party by name, age and appearance, followed by the count of animals. Gate guards and river crossings checked the pass against the people. Zhou One, the forger, writes the pass for the camel train himself. On it Shen You is **Kang Fuyan**, "nephew of Kang Satuo, aged twenty-nine, beardless, pale of face."

**Martial law lifts.** Martial law was declared on the night of the twenty-first of the eighth month. For ten days the ward gates opened only one leaf, and everyone going in or out was questioned. Before dawn on the first, word comes down from the palace that the city gates and ward gates will run as usual. The West Market heard it first, the afternoon before, from men of the Jinwu Guard, and the Hu merchants harnessed their camels overnight. The barricades at the ward gates are dragged aside, and the families who fled into the city from north of the Wei start heading home.

- **The Jinwu Guard.** The guard army that polices Chang'an's streets, the same one that seized Sogdians in the West Market as suspected spies in Chapter 14. Strictly, in 626 it was still the Left and Right Wuhou Guards and became the Jinwu Guard only in 662; the book uses the later, more familiar name throughout, as Chinese stories of the Tang do.

**The curtained hat.** Zheng Four sews Shen You a *curtained hat* (*weimao*): a hoop of split bamboo covered in fine rattan, with gauze cut from his own bed curtains hanging from the brim to his shoulders. The gauze is *qingrong*, a silk so thin the light comes through it. It hides his face completely, and by Zhou One's second condition it stays on all day.

**Hiding in plain sight.** Kang Satuo's own camel train is enormous: 290 camels, a hundred-odd horses and a hundred and seventy-odd people. Shen You's 20 camels and thirty-odd horses vanish into it "like pouring a bowl of water into a river." The household's 120 travelers are split three ways: Shen You's group with the Kang train out of Kaiyuan Gate, Chu One's group with the He family's train out of Jinguang Gate, and Feng Five's group with the Shi family's train, also out of Kaiyuan Gate. All the extra camel guards and horses are written on the passes as Kang's people and Kang's horses, so if anyone looks too closely, Kang is the first man led away in ropes. He has decided the risk is worth it. What Shen You lacks, he reckons, is neither money nor nerve but years, and in another year the line at this household's door will be too long to join.

**The horses that stay.** The 87 new horses are green and stay home behind the palisade, along with the 24 wounded horses from Jingyang. The mounts for the road are seventy picked from the household's own horses. The only exception is the gray mare. In Chapter 16 Cao One says her herd is somewhere among Jieli's herds, waiting for its lead mare, and she is that mare.

**Riding a camel.** The lead camel is a two-humped Bactrian. A camel gets up hind legs first, then forelegs, pitching its rider forward and then back. Shen You calls it "a self-test on boot." It walks with both legs on one side moving together, which rolls the rider like a boat. Zhou Six gives him ginger for the seasickness.

## Chapter 18: Across the Bian Bridge

**Kaiyuan Gate.** A Chang'an city gate had three tunnels through the wall. Here the middle one stays shut, traffic goes in on the left and out on the right, and on the morning martial law lifts, all of it is jammed. Clerk Dong, the gate clerk, checks the Kang pass: **310 camels, 131 horses, 217 people.** He starts counting camels and loses count in the forties, when a camel spits on a guard. Zhou One had written in the true figures the Kang family gave him, so the pass would have matched anyway. Clerk Dong never finishes counting, so he never finds that out.

**Twelfth Daughter.** In the gate tunnel, Shen You's camel takes a mouthful of a young woman's veil. She is from the **Lu** family: eighteen or nineteen, unpowdered, with a worn leather tally pouch at her waist. She travels with a gray-bearded manager, four household retainers, and a maid on a mule. She frees the veil with two taps of her whip on the camel's lip, and in two sentences tells Shen You that his camel looks more like a Hu merchant than he does, and that his camel guards don't look like camel guards. She is riding north of the Wei to count, in person, how many buildings burned on the family estate. Its foreman has reported drought every year, and every year handed in two-tenths less, though she found the well full when she went to look last summer. From the roadside she counts the horses behind the camel train: 47 carrying loads, 52 ridden, and 32 spares. The spares are young, well fleshed and newly shod, far too good for a camel train's relief horses. Then she decides it is "not her ledger" and rides on.

- **Her name.** *Shi'erniang* means "Twelfth Daughter." As with Dou the Eleventh, the birth order is usually counted across all the girls of a generation in the clan, so she need not have eleven older sisters. The manager calls her "Twelfth Daughter," and the English uses it as her name, without "the." Shen You addresses her as "miss." These chapters say nothing about whether her family is connected to Commandant Lu of Wannian County.
- **Curtained hat and veiled hat.** Both are Tang riding hats hung with gauze. Shen You's *curtained hat* (*weimao*) has a brim and a curtain to the shoulders. Twelfth Daughter wears a *veiled hat* (*mili*), the older style, whose veil can hide most of the rider; she has its front panel pinned up on the brim, leaving half her face showing. The English keeps the two names apart.
- **"The master."** Her manager worries about what "the master" will say if he finds out. The text never says whether the master is her father, and the English doesn't either.

**The general and the chit.** At the camp on the south bank of the Wei, the gatekeeper hands Liang's chit to the general on the black horse, the same general who questioned Shen You on the slope in Chapter 15. The narration of Chapter 18 calls him only "the general on the black horse." He reads it, is told the Master has caught a chill, remarks that the wind on the slope that day was stronger than it is today, and lets the matter of the horses drop: "The horses were Liang Laosan's to give. That's none of my business." Once the gatekeeper has gone, he tells his guardsman to "Note it down."

**The Bian Bridge, two days on.** Ten militiamen, a single mess, check passes at the south end. Asked why the man on the camel has his face covered, Kang Satuo says it's his nephew, with sores on his face. That morning at the city gate, Shen You was taken for somebody's womenfolk. Before he crosses, Shen You flicks a Kaiyuan Tongbao coin into the river. Since the county ordered him in Chapter 10 not to leave the capital while his papers are checked, the household's own rule is that "crossing the Wei counts as leaving the capital." He made the rule, so he pays the fine. In the middle of the bridge is a large black stain, soaked into the cracks between the planks. It is where the white horse fell when the treaty was sealed (Chapter 16). Nobody has scrubbed it.

**The empty camp.** The Turkic main host broke camp only at first light that morning. By noon the north bank is churned turf, fire pits beyond counting, broken arrows and flies. The gray mare stops, smells the dung of horses that grazed there, and blows one long breath toward the north.

**The fork west of Xianyang.** Early in the Hour of the Monkey, the three groups meet under an old pagoda tree at a fork ten-odd *li* west of Xianyang. The main road runs west to Wugong, Qizhou and Liangzhou; a narrow road goes north toward Liquan. They have left through two city gates in three trains. The clerk at Jinguang Gate counted Chu One's group twice and found nothing wrong; Kaiyuan Gate didn't count Feng Five's at all. All 120 are there. Zhou One's plan had been to go a day west before turning north, but Chu One changes it at the fork: every extra day west is a day to make up coming back. Ashina adds that the Liquan road runs straight and links up farther north with the Turks' route home. Kang Satuo leaves them here, heading west. His parting gift is a pair of padded Sogdian riding trousers for Shen You's raw thighs, and his parting request is to be sent for when the good horses are in hand.

**The militia going home.** On the road north they meet a squad of militiamen heading south. A Tang squad was fifty men under a squad leader; a mess was ten, under a mess leader. This squad came out fifty strong and is going back forty-three. One young soldier grumbles that "the emperor says no pursuit." The squad leader notices that the "camel guards" walk in perfect step (Feng Seven's rope keeps the beat, see "Square Dancing" in the Glossary), and that their bows are old but the strings are new. He asks no more questions and never asks for the pass. He is going home, not checking on people.

## Chapter 19: Willow Gully

**The village.** Willow Gully is a walled village of thirty-some households north of the Wei. The villagers had spent ten-odd days hiding in the gullies to the north. Around noon on the thirtieth, word came from the bridge that the treaty was made and the Turks were leaving, so they came out to bring in the harvest. That afternoon, twenty or thirty riders came. Feng Five, reading the road, counts **thirty-one riders**, two horses to a man, and **nineteen captives** walking on one rope, children among them. Two fell and were hauled up again. A woman wails over a cracked pot she had borrowed from the Wangs, whose two eldest daughters were taken. The old man digging a grave says his family's twenty *mu* yielded seventeen *shi* of millet, enough after rent to eat until the wheat harvest. The threshing floor is now a ring of black scars. In return for the meal Ge Four cooks, he draws the three gullies to the north in the ash.

**Beating the grass for grain.** Ashina's rendering of a Turkic saying. Turkic raiders brought no grain south: each man rode with two or three horses, the horses ate the grass by the road, and the men ate the grain. "Coming, they raid, it is to eat. Going back, they raid, it is to take." The treaty silk goes into the tents of the khan, the shads and the tegins. The ordinary families who did the fighting get too little of it to last a winter, so they take the rest on the road home, and the khan doesn't stop them, because if he did, no one would ride with him next time. On the way south they hold back, for fear of alarming what lies ahead; on the way home nobody is following, so they burn harder. People are worth more than sheep, because people walk by themselves. (The Chinese phrase, *da caogu*, is best known from later centuries, describing Khitan horsemen. In the book it is Ashina's own translation.)

**What the money bought.** Shen You asks what 33,000 strings, a thousand taels of gold and five thousand of silver actually bought. Ashina: "It buys south of the Wei." Historically, the 626 treaty is remembered as a humiliation the emperor later avenged, when the Tang destroyed Jieli's khaganate in 630. These chapters stay on the ground, with the villages the treaty didn't cover.

**The smokeless stove.** Ge Four digs a fire pit, then a slanting trench off it, roofed with branches and covered with earth. The smoke travels down the trench and comes out in the grass three *zhang* away, too thin to see. Soldiers today use the same trick.

**Choosing the camp.** The old man's three gullies: the west one is a dead end at a cliff; the Turks ride along the tableland above the wide middle one; the narrow east one has a spring that doesn't freeze. Feng Five picks the dead end, because travelers and raiders alike skip a gully that goes nowhere, whereas the old man and every shepherd in the villages around know about the spring. Chu One posts four sentries and changes them every double-hour.

**The *fuli* look back.** Feng Five rides north to scout and comes back after dark. On a ridge fifteen *li* north, three riders stood a while that afternoon, looking south, then went back north. Ashina names them: *fuli*, "wolves," the khan's own guard. He also explains the order of the Turkic withdrawal:

1. The camels carrying the gold and silk set out first, at noon on the thirtieth, the day of the treaty. They are slow.
2. The main host broke camp only at dawn on the first. It covers half as much ground again in a day as the camels, so within a day or two it passes them. The camels wait for it and go even slower than usual.
3. Once it has passed, the main host goes in front, the baggage falls behind it, and the *fuli* follow the baggage.
4. For the moment, what lies ahead of Shen You is the tail of the main host, mixed up with the slow baggage. Every so often a few riders are sent back to high ground to look south and see whether anyone is following.

North of the ridge, Feng Five also found camel tracks heading north and a little east, pressed a finger and a half deeper than an unladen camel's. Nobody needs to ask what they are carrying: he watched the treaty goods being loaded onto those camels on the north bank of the Bian Bridge the day before.

**Chu One's arithmetic.** Going on Ashina's figure of fifty or sixty *li* a day, the camels should have been eighty or ninety *li* away by that night, far enough that the household, posing as a horse train by day, would never meet them. Instead the camel tracks are fifteen *li* away, and the watchers were on that ridge that afternoon. So from that night on: sleep by day, move by night.

**The night sky.** The first of a lunar month is the new moon, so there is no moon and the stars are thick. Yun Five, the star reader, points out the Northern Dipper with its handle pointing west. An old Chinese saying holds that when the Dipper's handle points west, it is autumn everywhere under heaven. He also points out that the true pole, the spot that doesn't move, is empty sky. The bright star near it is nearly a fist away and circles it every night, so by night you steer by the empty spot, not the star. To the north, along the horizon, is a faint band of red: the glow of twenty or thirty fires, forty or fifty *li* beyond the ridge.

**Distances and lengths.** One *li* is about a third of a mile. Ten *chi* make a *zhang*. Kaiyuan Gate's wall is one *zhang* and eight *chi* high, and its gate tunnel some four *zhang* deep. The dry gully is some three *zhang* deep. The book never converts these.

**Time of day.** Chapter 17's departure is in the middle of the Hour of the Rabbit (around 6 a.m.). Kaiyuan Gate is early in the Hour of the Snake (soon after 9 a.m.), the Bian Bridge the middle of the Hour of the Horse (noon), the fork early in the Hour of the Monkey (soon after 3 p.m.), Willow Gully early in the Hour of the Rooster (soon after 5 p.m.), the camp the Hour of the Dog (7–9 p.m.), and Feng Five's return early in the Hour of the Pig (soon after 9 p.m.).
