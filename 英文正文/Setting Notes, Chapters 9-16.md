# Setting Notes for Chapters 9–16

*A reader's guide to the background behind Chapters 9 to 16: the calendar, the law, the money, the history and the people. Nothing here is needed to follow the story, but it explains why things happen the way they do. Spoilers through Chapter 16. For terms, see the Glossary; for a one-page overview of the whole book so far, see "Setting Notes for New Readers."*

---

## Where the story stands

By the morning of Chapter 9, Shen You has 45 deathsworn. A fortnight earlier he was a penniless man with no papers. Now he has a house in Kaiming Ward, a sugar workshop, and enemies on three sides. Chapters 9 to 16 cover **seventeen days (the seventeenth of the eighth month to the thirtieth), running from a duel of wits over a hidden box into the Turkic invasion of Chang'an.**

## The calendar, and why the summons are numbered the way they are

- The story runs on the **Chinese lunar calendar**, in which a month starts on the new moon. The eighth month of Wude 9 (626 CE) falls roughly in September. The English text keeps the Chinese dating: "the twenty-first of the eighth month."
- **Day N of the system is always day N + 8 of the eighth month.** So Day 9 is the seventeenth, Day 13 is the twenty-first (the empress's investiture), and Day 22 is the thirtieth.
- **The new day begins at the Hour of the Rat (11 p.m. to 1 a.m.).** A summons at midnight on the night of the twenty-third therefore counts toward the *next* day. This is why the Chapter 12 deadline night contains a summons of fifteen, and then, a few hours later and still in the dark, another of sixteen. The two belong to different days.
- **Running totals in these chapters:** 45 (Day 9), 55, 66, 78, 91, 105, 120 (Day 15, the deadline), 136, 153, 171, 190, 210, 231, 253 (Day 22, the end of Chapter 16). The total on Day N is N(N+1)/2. Shen You never counts the money, because it is not what he fears. What he fears is that every night adds more mouths than the night before.

## Chapter 9: Casting the Net

**Scattering the household.** Shen You doesn't keep his people together. He puts them into shops, a sugar workshop, a distillery, a pastry stall, a storyteller's pitch, and night watches on his enemies. Tang officials checked households by counting heads, rice and carts. A man with a hundred servants in one courtyard is a target. A man with the same people spread over six wards and two markets is simply a man with a lot of shops.

**The box and the seal.** The black lacquer box was fastened with sealing clay. Letters and boxes were sealed by pressing a private seal into a lump of clay over the cord. Whoever's seal it was is whoever is vouching for what is inside. Qingluo, who sealed letters for the Prince of Qi, can read them like a signature. That is why she can recognise the style of the seal.

**Cui Shouli and the Crown Prince's men.** Cui is a Colonel of the Left Encampment Guard. In the previous generation's struggle, the Crown Prince had quietly bought friends inside the guard armies and the palace, and a **roster** of those friends would be lethal in the wrong hands: many were still serving in the new emperor's court. Cui wants the box because it would protect him, or because he could sell it. The text doesn't say which.

**The border.** At the chapter's end, Cui says that if "the Turkic vanguard has passed Jingzhou," then "once the capital falls into chaos, that is our chance." In 626 this was simply true, and everyone in Chang'an knew the Turks were coming.

## Chapter 10: The General Amnesty

This whole chapter is a courtroom drama about one point of Tang law, so it is worth knowing the rules.

- **Monks needed a government licence.** Becoming a monk or nun meant being entered on the registers of the **Bureau of Sacrifices** (*cibu*), one of the ministries. Becoming one without permission ("private ordination") was a crime. Shen You's papers say he is a monk who returned to lay life. Liu Qi, the Pei family's lawyer, is trying to prove that no such monk exists on any register.
- **The general amnesty (*dashe*).** A new emperor's accession was marked by an amnesty that forgave "all crimes, grave or minor, whether already discovered or not yet discovered." There was one exception: the **Ten Abominations** (*shi'e*), the most serious crimes (rebellion, treason, killing one's parents, and so on). Shen You's argument is that private ordination is not among the ten, so his offence is wiped out.
- **The Temple Culling.** In the spring of 626, a decree had sent most monks and nuns back to lay life and demolished many small temples. The temple Shen You claims to come from was torn down on the seventeenth of the fifth month. That is the document he produces: the demolition ledger from the ward warden, listing "three monks, dismissed." Because the decree sent them home, the registers could have been wrong about them in either direction. It also explains why the registers are so thin on a poor little hermitage.
- **False accusation.** Tang law punished a false charge with the penalty the accuser had asked for. This is what "the false charge reverts on the accuser" (*fanzuo*) means. Commandant Lu warns Liu Qi's witness because, if the charge fails, the witness could be punished as the accused would have been.
- **"Not to leave the capital while the matter is checked."** The county can't settle a distant question by itself, so it sends an inquiry to Yizhou (modern Sichuan), the prefecture Shen You claims to come from. A round trip takes about two months. Meanwhile Shen You is on a short leash.

**The unnamed man.** The last scene is set in a great house in the northeast of the city, where an unnamed man in the seat of honor says of Shen You, "No one, so much the easier to deal with." The narration deliberately withholds who he is.

## Chapter 11: The Investiture of the Empress

- **Why the investiture matters.** On the twenty-first of the eighth month, Li Shimin's wife, of the Zhangsun clan, was made empress. The chapter opens before dawn, with drums and music from the palace.
- **The Dou family sends the sugar in.** Dou Shiyilang's family is the new emperor's *mother's* clan, and it has the right to send gifts into the palace. Shen You's white sugar goes in under the Dou name, 120 *jin* (about 160 lb) in forty gilt lacquer boxes. In an age when sugar was brown, dark and grainy, bright white sugar was a wonder.
- **The names "Snow Sugar" and "Fulin Fire."** The empress says the sugar is "white as snow," and the emperor names it. The lighter, a gift delivered by Zhangsun Wuji, her brother, is passed off as an exotic treasure from **Fulin**, the Tang name for the Eastern Roman Empire. The emperor puts it in his sleeve.
- **Patrolling the city as a disguise.** Qian Biao reports that Cui Shouli plans to ride into Kaiming Ward on the night of the twenty-third wearing guard-army armour, under the banner of a search for **Turkic spies**. This is plausible because, in a time of war, soldiers did search wards for spies, and no ward warden would refuse armed men from a guard army.
- **The Turks cross the border.** The chapter ends with an urgent report: **Jieli Khan** and **Tuli Khan** have brought more than a hundred thousand horse into Jingzhou, and the capital is put under **martial law**. Historically, the Turks chose the first weeks of a new and shaky reign to strike.

## Chapter 12: The Ten-Day Deadline

- **Why Cui calls out "Crow!"** It is the prearranged signal for his men to draw. Shen You answers by tapping the arm of his chair, and twenty-four glass lanterns light at once. Both sides had planned the night; only one was ready.
- **The gate and the law.** Armed men entering a ward at night needed a writ from the right office. Yuwen Yan, the Senior Colonel, points out that his guard has issued no search order that night and that "not one of these men is from the Left Encampment Guard." That one sentence is what makes Cui's men criminals rather than soldiers. Under Tang law, a man who enters another's house at night without cause could be struck forty times with the light cane, and a householder who killed him on the spot was not charged.
- **"Cui is after money."** When Cui falls, he does not name the people behind him. The book lets him claim he did it for the sake of money. Shen You promises that the box will never be handed over and never burned: "I'll keep it." He means that nobody will use it to kill anyone.
- **The emperor's interest.** The last scene is in the Eastern Palace. Li Shimin is reading it in the Eastern Palace, the crown prince's residence, where he took the throne. He reads the report and says that once the Turks are gone he wants to meet "this monk."

## Chapter 13: The Price of Grain

- **Martial law and the grain markets.** When the Turks were reported at the border, everyone expected a siege, so grain became money. Millet that sold for 30 coins a *dou* before martial law was over 100 in the West Market by the twenty-fourth. The grain dealers, meanwhile, were paying farmers 20 coins a *dou* and then 25. The difference is the whole of what Shen You is going to do.
- **Millet, rice, and the measures.** Millet (*su*) was the staple of the north. Rice was dearer. Shen You's own purchase price is 50 coins a *dou*: more than the dealers pay, much less than the market charges. He sells it back to his own ward at the same price, and limits each household to one *dou* a day.
- **The official measure.** He buys with a **fire-branded official measure**, so a farmer can see he is not being cheated.
- **Steward Wu and "the register."** Steward Wu works for **Zhangsun Shunde, Duke of Xue**, the empress's uncle. He comes to Shen You with ten *shi* of rice and a remark about "the register"; the text leaves open exactly what the duke's house wants to know. Shen You pays him at the going price in silk, and tells him to "say hello to the Duke of Xue." A favour accepted from a great house creates an obligation. A payment settled on the spot does not.
- **The old farmer.** Elder Meng sells eight *shi* of millet for four strings; his eldest son has been sent to the front. His son is in the **militia** (*fubing*), the farmer-soldiers who served in rotation and formed the backbone of the early Tang army.
- **The cellar.** Shen You has a storage cellar dug in the east courtyard, remembering that in the early years of the Zhenguan reign, "there was a great famine in Guanzhong, and a *dou* of rice cost a bolt of silk." He doesn't recall the year. (Historically, the great famine of 627 lay just ahead.)

## Chapter 14: Jingyang

- **Hiring carts for the army.** The government hired carts, oxen and drivers from ordinary people during campaigns, and wrote the owner's name in the county's ledger. Shen You's name goes into that book as the owner of twenty carts. That is his protection: a man who has served the army is harder to accuse. It is also a risk. He is on record.
- **Why the bridge.** The first load of grain is delivered to a depot on the north bank of the **Wei River**, over the **Middle Wei Bridge**. Jingyang lies farther northwest, on the Jing River.
- **"Victory at Jingyang."** The real **Yuchi Jingde** did win at Jingyang, and captured a great Turkic general of the Ashide clan. The victory was real but local. The Turkic main host was still advancing.
- **The horses that obey Cao One.** In the fog, Cao One hums a tune and the Turkic horses come out of the reeds and gather around him. Horses were valuable spoils. Deputy Commandant Liang rules that the 47 captured horses go to the state, and the 24 wounded ones to Shen You.
- **Ashina.** Ashina is a Turk from a surrendered household who was once a slave in the Pei family's service. He is freed by a **letter of manumission** and hired on at one string a month. He is not a deathsworn, and not a slave. Shen You's rule: "In my house we don't do that. If you want to come, come as a hired man." He knows the Turks' habits, and he warns Shen You that the khan will be at the Wei River "the day after tomorrow."
- **The "six riders."** Shen You half-remembers a story about the Wei River, a bridge, and six horsemen. He can't recall the day. In Chapter 15 it comes true.

## Chapter 15: Watching the Livestream

This is the chapter where the main character, who knows the history only as a modern reader does, stands on a hillside and watches the most famous scene of the early Tang.

- **The event.** In the real history, the Turkic army reached the north bank of the Wei River, very close to the capital. **Li Shimin rode out with only six companions** and spoke across the water to Jieli Khan. He reproached the khan for breaking the treaty, while the main Tang army came up behind him in battle array. The khan, who had expected a frightened court, was shaken. The two sides made a treaty at the **Bian Bridge** a few days later.
- **The date.** In the book, the meeting at the river is on the 28th (Day 20) and the treaty on the 30th (Chapter 16). The book follows the standard account closely; the "six riders" are all that the rest of the world sees.
- **"The Son of Heaven."** This is the emperor. The six horsemen are on the south bank; the Turks on the north bank dismount and bow.
- **Cheng Zhijie, "Yaojin."** The general who rides up to question Shen You is **Cheng Zhijie**. To a modern Chinese reader, he is Cheng Yaojin ("Bite Gold"), the swaggering general of countless stories, operas and television dramas. His real name at court was Zhijie. Shen You blurts out "Yaojin," and some of the general's younger riders have never heard the name. That is the chapter's quiet joke. The general lets Shen You go: "Be inside the city before dark. If I see you here again tomorrow, I'll take you for a spy."
- **Why the ledger matters.** Anyone near the front who wasn't in uniform could be taken for a spy. Shen You's convoy is on the county's hiring ledger.
- **The wagon.** The new cart has a cabin hung on three layers of ox-hide straps, iron-bound wheels, and two *hengdao* hidden in a compartment under the floor. The folding chair is a Tang-style *jiaoyi*, folded up like firewood and hung at the back.
- **The Treasury summons.** At the end of the chapter the **Court of the Treasury** (*Taifu si*) calls for a hundred carts at the Imperial City the next morning, with Shen You's twenty first in line at the gate of the **Left Treasury**. Shen You guesses what they will carry.

## Chapter 16: The White Horse

- **Buying peace.** Paying a raiding khan to go home was a bitter humiliation, and the later reign remembered it. The book gives the figures: 82 cartloads of silk at 800 bolts each (65,600 bolts), ten carts of brocade and figured silk, 1,000 *liang* of gold and 5,000 of silver, and six carts of gold and silver vessels. Zheng Three values it at about **33,000 strings**, a huge sum.
- **Counting with beans.** Zheng Three tracks the carts by dropping beans in a bag: yellow beans for silk, black for brocade, red for gold and silver. Her total is the first time anyone in the house sees what the treaty costs.
- **"Rather than give it to the Turks, give it to me."** Shen You isn't entirely joking. Ashina agrees to guide him, and Zhou One proposes a plan involving a forged gold-tipped arrow.
- **The white horse oath.** In old China an oath of alliance was sealed by sacrificing a white horse. In Chapter 16 the white horse is led onto the bridge and drops dead on the planks, and on the far bank a bow is raised and the Turks shout "Khan!" The narration doesn't say how it died.
- **Tuli Khan's brand.** The brand burned into the Turkic horses of Tuli's people is a hooked line with two short bars. Zhou One proposes a wax-sealed, gold-tipped arrow bearing the same mark, to pass as a message from Tuli. Tuli, "the lesser khan," camps apart from Jieli.
- **Kang Satuo's caravan.** The Sogdian merchant Kang Satuo sells Shen You 87 horses and 20 camels, paid for in white sugar. Camels carried the Silk Road trade. The caravan is to leave for Liangzhou the next day; whether Shen You goes with it is left open.

**Time of day.** The Hour of the Dog is 7–9 p.m. and is when the ward gates close. The Hour of the Goat is 1–3 p.m. The second watch (9–11 p.m.) is the usual hour for the book's night action. The fourth watch is 1–3 a.m.
