# Chapter 20: The Hidden Valley

"It's the third watch," Zhou Four said outside the tent.

"Mm," came from inside.

At the north end of the west gully, at the foot of the cliff, stood a white felt tent. Ge Four and Shui One had unloaded it from the pack frames and pitched it in the dark: one zhang and six chi across, four layers of felt, the very same tent from the east compound in Kaiming Ward. Zhou Four stood ten paces off with three men, backs to the tent, faces to the mouth of the gully. Ashina and Feng Five were still up on the rim, watching that band of red to the north; Chu One had told them to come down once the third watch had passed. The two of them had their backs to the gully floor and their faces to the north. The tent at the foot of the cliff was behind them, out of sight.

A small lamp burned inside. Shen You sat cross-legged on the felt. The two rubbed-raw places on the insides of his thighs throbbed, beat after beat.

The line of golden characters lit up before his eyes, right on time.

"Summon."

The lamplight wavered.

All at once the tent was crowded. Someone's elbow knocked the lamp dish. Someone said, "Huh?" Someone's bare feet whispered across the felt, and somebody sneezed. It was the second day of the ninth month now, and the nights were cold.

Zhou Four didn't turn around. "Zheng One."

Only then did Zheng One come over and draw back the tent flap.

The tent was packed to the walls, and two more had been squeezed out at the door, looking down at their own feet. Men and women, tall and short, all in plain undyed cloth.

Other nights, the one drawing back the flap at this moment was Zhou One, a brush tucked in his sleeve. Zhou One was in Chang'an, "catching a chill" for the Master.

"No need to question them one by one." Shen You leaned back against the felt. "I'll read, you write."

Zheng One answered yes. He fished a burnt-down stick of charcoal from beside Ge Four's stove, tore a piece of sheepskin off the cover of a pack frame, and squatted under the lamp.

Shen You looked at the golden characters and read down them, line by line.

"Two hundred and seventy-seven. Horseshoeing. You're Peng Four."

"Two hundred and seventy-eight. Curing hides. Peng Five."

"Peng Six, rope making. Peng Seven, barbering."

That filled out the Pengs. Shen You muttered under his breath, "Xi, Fan, Peng, Lang... Lang."

Zheng One's charcoal stopped. "Lang One?"

"Lang One."

The first of the Langs was a round-faced young fellow. He gave a folded-hands salute. "Lang One greets the Master."

Somebody in the tent couldn't hold it in. *Pfft.* Lang One caught it himself a beat later, and his face went red.

"From now on, call him by the whole thing," Shen You said, grinning himself. "Lang One, Lang Two. Don't just yell 'Lang.' You lot call me *langjun*. You yell 'Lang,' and I'll turn around too."

Seven Langs, seven Lus. When he reached Lu Three, it was "Cliff climbing." He was a small, skinny man whose arms hung down past his knees, all ten fingers curled, as if ready to grab hold of something at any moment.

"Beehives on a cliff face," Lu Three said. "The ones nobody else can reach, I can reach."

Next came the Weis.

"Wei One, Wei Two." Shen You paused. "Wei Four."

Zheng One looked up.

"Wei Three's already taken," Shen You said.

"Ah." Zheng One nodded. "Wei San. The one who walked off with a jar of white sugar."

Twenty-four people, in less time than it takes to drink a cup of tea. If Zhou One had been doing it, asking each one's background, each one's skill, making up a line about where each had come from, it would have run into the fourth watch.

Ge Four carried in an armful of hide robes and an armful of boots and handed them out one by one. They had packed a hundred and thirty sets when they left Kaiming Ward. From tonight on, the pile would get a little shorter every night.

Shen You leaned over to look at Zheng One's piece of sheepskin.

He couldn't read it.

The names were in Chinese characters. After the names, things fell apart. There was a looping string like the letters Sogdians kept their accounts in, and a few notches like the ones Turks cut into wood. There was a circle with three strokes inside it, a horse's hoof, a lopsided beehive.

"What's this?"

"The horseshoer." Zheng One pointed at the hoof.

"And this?"

"The hide curer. That's Sogdian. Their word for 'hide' is short. Quick to write."

Shen You didn't ask anything more. He watched Zheng One roll up the sheepskin and tuck it inside his own robe.

The household's records existed in exactly one copy, and it was Zhou One's.

Single point of failure, he thought. One database, no backup, and the guy who runs it isn't even in the server room.

***

At the Hour of the Ox, Chu One said one word. "Move."

A hundred and twenty people, twenty camels and ninety-odd horses, with twenty-four people in fresh boots trailing behind, went out of the west gully in the dark, up onto the tableland and north.

Ashina reined in and looked back. The string of shadows behind them was a good length longer than when they'd come. Zheng One said, "The Kang family's second batch. They followed the camel bells and caught up in the night." Ashina looked once and asked nothing more.

There were no lamps on the tableland, no fires. The second of the ninth month: no moon. Yun Five walked at the very front, lifting his head every so often to check the empty patch of sky. Feng Five was a li ahead of him, too far off to see at all.

The new people had no horses. They held on to camels' lead ropes and walked. Lang One dozed off on his feet and butted his head straight into a camel's rump. The camel turned to look at him, and he hurried to smile an apology.

Shen You rode wedged between two camels. The two places on his legs, salved with Zhou Six's ointment, had rubbed open again. He gritted his teeth and counted hoofbeats in his head. At three thousand he lost count and started over.

When the sky began to pale, Feng Five came back from ahead and led the column down off the tableland, turning east into a dry gully.

To the east, a great ridge lay across the land, a black mass blacker than the sky.

Feng Five dropped to the tail of the column with two men. Each of them dragged a bundle of wild jujube branches, and they swept away the hoofprints along the stretch where the column had turned off the tableland road, all the way down to the broken stones of the gully floor. It was more than a li.

***

In the middle of the Hour of the Rabbit, they entered the valley.

The mouth of the valley was squeezed between two cliffs, no more than three zhang wide, with a layer of loose dust on the ground. Forty-odd paces in, it bent north: the first bend. A hundred-odd paces farther, it bent again, and only then did it open out into a stretch of level ground of thirty or forty mu. A mountain stream ran down off the slope. Oaks and aspens grew on either side, their leaves half gone yellow, and the smoke trees were as red as if someone had splashed them with blood. At the far end was the slope, forest all the way up. A man could climb it. A horse couldn't.

A sack.

Chu One had chosen it for the same reason he'd picked the west gully at Willow Gully: where there was no way through, travelers didn't go in.

Shen You got down from his horse, his legs shaking. He looked all around and thought of Kaiming Ward. Kaiming Ward sat right on Vermilion Bird Avenue and had only two gates, east and west. This valley had only one.

About now, Warden Zhao would be pushing open the ward gate.

The horses were unsaddled and hobbled at the forelegs, and Cao One led them to the stream. By the water, Ge Four dug his smokeless stove, same as ever. The people unloaded the packs and dropped where they stood, sprawled every which way, asleep as soon as they were down. Sleep by day, march by night.

Chu One didn't sleep. He studied the mouth of the valley for a long time, then pointed to the cliff on the north side.

"From up there you can see twenty li," he said. "No one can get up it."

He turned. "Zheng One. The ones who came last night. Any climbers?"

Zheng One took the sheepskin from inside his robe, unrolled it, and held it up to the daylight.

He looked at it for quite a while.

His finger came to rest on a lopsided beehive. "...Lu Three."

Lu Three was shaken awake. Rubbing his eyes, he walked to the foot of the cliff, tipped his head back for a look, and spat in his palms. Before a cup of tea could have been drunk, he was squatting on the clifftop and waving down at them, as small as a monkey.

Shen You squatted by the stream washing his face. The water was so cold it made him suck in his breath. He watched Zheng One roll the sheepskin back up.

Only Zheng One being able to read it: that was a bad thing.

He shook the water off his hands.

Only their own people being able to read it, though. That would be a good thing.

***

Shen You couldn't sleep.

He lay in the tent for more than a double-hour. The horses were chewing grass, the stream was babbling, Cao One was humming in among the horses, and some bird, he had no idea what kind, was calling nonstop from a fork in the branches above the tent, never once changing its tune.

"That I'm good at" had been premature. What he was good at was sleeping in the hot pool.

Toward the end of the Hour of the Dragon, he crawled out of the tent and begged a parched-grain cake off Ge Four. It was parched-grain meal mixed with mutton fat and patted flat, long since gone stone-cold and as hard as a roof tile. The valley lay in shadow and was cold, so he took the cake and climbed the south slope at the first bend, found a big rock the sun could reach, sat down, and basked.

Zhou Four followed him up and stood three paces behind him.

He had taken exactly one bite of the cake when Lu Three, up on the clifftop, dropped a pebble.

It landed dead on the felt cap of the sentry at the valley mouth. The sentry looked up. Lu Three held out one hand, five fingers spread, then pointed west.

The sentry poked his head out of the valley mouth for one look down the gully, then turned and ran back in.

Chu One heard him out. Nothing moved in his face. "How soon?"

"They're riding slow. Looking down at the ground the whole way. A quarter of an hour."

Beside them, Feng Five's face went a shade grayer. "They're looking at the stretch I swept."

"They'll want to come in and look," Chu One said. "Once they're in, they don't leave."

Nobody asked why they didn't hide. Ninety-odd horses, dozens of piles of dung: there was no hiding that.

Chu One drew a few lines in the dirt with a twig and said a few sentences. He didn't repeat a single one, and when he was done, everyone scattered.

***

A pack rope as thick as a thumb went under the loose dust at the valley mouth, each end wrapped around a boulder at the foot of the cliffs. Once it was buried, seven camels were walked back and forth across the valley mouth to trample over the horses' hoofprints. Feng Seven lay behind the boulders where the rope was wrapped, with six of her people, three at each end.

In the first bend, the seven camels knelt in a row, their packs unloaded on the ground. Ge Four started a second fire in the bend, set a pot on it, and fed it wet wood, and the smoke rose high. A Hu merchant's caravan stopping to rest, and no smoke? Who'd ever seen that? Ashina, Zheng One and three camel drivers sat around the fire. Ashina had his robe hanging open at the chest, like a Turk hired to drive camels for some Hu merchant.

Wei One and Chu Four crouched behind a stack of packs, ten-odd paces inside the valley mouth. Chen Five pressed himself against the foot of the south cliff and didn't move, as if he'd grown out of the rock.

Zheng Six and two archers lay in the oak scrub on the north slope, twenty-odd paces from the bend. Feng Six was on the south slope, his little bronze-toothed crossbow cocked.

Everyone else, along with the ninety-odd horses and thirteen camels, was behind the second bend. Cao One squatted in the middle of the herd, humming that wordless tune of his, very low. One by one, the horses lowered their heads.

Shen You sat on the big rock on the south slope and didn't move.

Zhou Four had said, "Don't move." If he went down now, he'd be seen at a glance from the valley mouth.

So Shen You didn't move. He still had the parched-grain cake in his hand.

A honeypot, he thought. Set out an opening that looks easy to pick on, and wait for them to walk right in.

***

Five riders came in through the mouth of the valley. Not one stayed outside. That was the rule when you went beating the grass for grain: the ones who went in split the take, and the ones left outside to keep watch got nothing. No one ever wanted to stay.

Fur caps, sheepskin coats, a bow on every back, already strung, and curved sabers at their waists. The leader was in his thirties, his face weathered by the wind into something like old elm bark. As soon as he was inside the mouth, he reined in and looked up at the cliffs on either side.

Up on the cliff, Lu Three had long since pulled back behind a rock.

Hooves stepped over that strip of loose dust. One horse, two, five.

The leader pulled up in the bend, twenty paces from the fire. He looked at the camels, looked at the fire, looked at the men around it, and said something in Turkic.

Ashina stood and answered, also in Turkic.

Later, Zheng One repeated it all for Shen You, line by line.

"Whose camel train?"

"The Kangs'. Going to Binzhou to buy hides."

"Buying hides, and you take this gully?"

"To dodge you."

The leader laughed, baring a mouthful of yellow teeth. "Good dodging. Dodge all the way out here, you still can't dodge the toll."

"How much?"

"One camel."

Ashina shook his head and offered two bolts of silk. The two of them haggled back and forth, but the leader's eyes weren't on Ashina's face.

He was looking at the ground.

The stretch from the bend on toward the second bend had been churned up, hoofprints stacked on hoofprints, trampled into a mess. By the stream lay a great sprawl of horse dung, still sending up a wisp of white in the cold morning air.

Seven camels didn't drop that much horse dung.

"Fine," the leader said suddenly. "One camel. Bring it here."

Ashina turned to go lead a camel over.

The leader put two fingers in his mouth.

A whistle.

All five horses wheeled at once. The four at the back were the front now, charging for the valley mouth.

"Up!"

Feng Seven's shout, and at the foot of the cliffs six people threw themselves backward together. The pack rope sprang out of the loose dust, raising a cloud of grit as high as a man, and snapped taut, ramrod straight, at the height of a horse's knees.

The first horse hit it with both forelegs and went clean over. Its rider flew two zhang over its head, slammed onto the broken stones, rolled twice, and pushed himself up on one knee, already with a blade in his hand.

Wei One came out from behind the stack of packs and was on him in a few bounds.

"Pardon me."

His arms locked around the man's waist and heaved, and the man left the ground. Wei One turned and slammed him down over his back. The stones went *crunch*. The man lay with his mouth open, unable to get a breath, and his blade had gone who knew where.

The second horse couldn't pull up in time, tripped over the first, and threw its rider, who landed shoulder first. He rolled to his feet and slashed sideways.

Chu Four didn't dodge. His staff swept in low along the ground and cracked across the back of the man's knee. *Whack!* One leg folded under him right there. The tip of the staff flicked up and rapped his wrist. The blade fell.

The third rider's horse, seeing two horses down ahead of it, gathered itself to jump.

Up on the north slope, Zheng Six breathed out.

The string sang. The arrow drove into the horse's foreleg just above the joint. The horse came down, that leg buckled, and it pitched headfirst into the stones and went all the way over, pinning its rider underneath.

*Crack.*

A very faint sound. Shen You heard it all the way up the slope. The man's head had turned to a place no head should turn. He didn't move again.

The fourth rider had reined in earliest. His horse reared, and he saw the valley mouth clearly: the rope drawn tight, two horses kicking on the ground, men lying on the ground. He was an old hand. He jumped down, drew his blade, and went straight for the three people hauling the rope at the foot of the south cliff. Cut the rope, and the door was open.

Chen Five stepped out from the foot of the cliff.

Blade in his left hand, edge turned toward himself. The back of the blade knocked against the man's wrist, and the curved saber left his hand and spun twice in the air. The man grabbed for the short knife at his waist. Chen Five drove the hilt forward, straight in under his ribs. The man curled up like a shrimp and went down on his knees.

The leader hadn't charged the valley mouth.

The instant the rope came up, he'd reined in. Of the five, he was the only one who hadn't looked toward the mouth. He was looking at the north slope.

The oak scrub had stirred a moment ago. Zheng Six's arrow had come from there.

He turned in the saddle and loosed.

One of the archers hissed. A gash had opened along his left forearm, and blood ran down his fingers and dripped. He didn't let go of his bow.

The leader wrenched his horse around and charged the south slope.

The south slope was gentle. A path worn by sheep slanted up toward the top, and the top was forest. A horse could make it.

The path ran right underneath Shen You's big rock.

Zhou Four took one step forward and planted himself in front of Shen You. He rolled his left wrist once. He rolled his right wrist once.

Shen You's cake stopped at his lips.

Feng Six was partway up the slope, fifteen paces off. He brought up the little bronze-toothed crossbow.

The string went *thrum*. The bolt drove into the horse's neck on one side, and the point came out half an inch on the other. The horse ran three more steps, its forelegs folded, and horse and rider slid down the slope together.

The leader came off the saddle, hit the ground rolling, and when he came up his bow was already drawn, the arrow aimed up the slope.

Up the slope, ten-odd paces off, stood Zhou Four. Behind Zhou Four was the big rock.

"Turn around."

Ashina's voice, from behind him. In Turkic.

The leader didn't turn around. Ashina had long since circled from the fire to the foot of the slope, the black-lacquered horn bow drawn to the full, the bone thumb ring locked on the string, the arrowhead ten paces from the middle of the man's back. The leader couldn't see it. But he could hear the limbs of that bow creaking.

Up on the north slope, another bow creaked to full draw.

Two bows.

The leader's fingers went slack. The bow dropped to the ground.

"Done," Zhou Four said.

Only then did Shen You take that bite of cake.

From Feng Seven's "Up!" to Zhou Four's "Done," the water in Ge Four's pot hadn't come to a boil.

***

One dead, four alive.

Ge Four threw a ladle of water on the wet wood. *Hsss.* The column of smoke broke off.

One of their own archers had a gash on his left forearm. Zhou Six squatted and looked at it once. "Bone arrowhead. Grazed you. Didn't go in." She washed it, salved it and bound it. The archer wanted to go back and lie in the scrub again, and Zheng Six pushed him back down.

Of the five horses, the leader's was dead, with a hole clean through its neck. Feng Six squatted beside the carcass, prying at his bolt with a little file; the bronze teeth had lodged in the bone, and he pried a good long while, clucking his tongue as if it hurt him. The third horse had taken an arrow in the foreleg. Cao One felt it over for a long time, then looked up. "It can be nursed back."

Chu One climbed the south slope and stood before Shen You. "One dead, four alive. On our side, one scrape."

Shen You nodded and took another bite of cake.

Feng Five stood at the valley mouth, looking at that strip of loose dust the hooves had torn up. He looked at it a long time.

"I swept it," he said. "Swept it too clean. The whole road's covered in hoofprints, and that one stretch doesn't have a single one. Like a face with one patch where the beard won't grow."

Deleting the logs had stood out worse than the logs, Shen You thought. Out loud he said, "Next time, sweep it dirtier."

Feng Five gave one nod. He had truly taken it to heart.

For now, he patched what he could. He took two men, mounted two of the captured Tuli horses, and laid a trail of hoofprints heading west from the valley mouth. Then he used branches to scuff up the stretch where the five riders had come in, until it looked as if the wind had blown across it. He came back, took a look at it himself, and shook his head. "Still rough."

Ashina squatted beside the leader's dead horse and parted the hair on its haunch.

A hook, with two short bars beneath it.

He took a leather tube from inside his robe. In it was the golden arrow, and wrapped in oilcloth at the arrow's tail was a bronze seal, cut by Zhou One. He set the seal beside the horse's haunch and compared the two.

The tail of the hook curled inward by half a fen. Of the two short bars, the left was shorter, the right longer.

Not a hair's difference.

"Feng Five saw it on the river flat on the north bank, squatting beside a cartwheel," Zheng One said. "Zhou One cut it from what Feng Five drew on the bricks."

"If Zhou One were here," Shen You said, "he'd be pleased for half a day."

Ashina put the seal away and went to look at the other four horses. All four bore that same brand on the haunch.

"The lesser khan's horses," he said. "Real."

***

At the Hour of the Goat, one shift of sleepers woke. The prisoners were tied to trees at the second bend, and Ashina questioned them.

Zheng One squatted beside Shen You and Chu One and passed it on in a low voice, line by line.

The five were loose riders from Tuli's tribe. They had left the lesser khan's camp at first light and ridden right past the sentries south of the main camp, telling the sentries posted to look back south that they were hunting for strayed horses.

Jieli's main camp was on the river plain to the north, twenty-five li away. It had pitched camp there and stopped moving.

The treasure carts stood at the heart of the camp, drawn up in a ring, with the *fuli* guarding them.

"At dawn on the fifth," said the young man whose knee Chu Four had cracked, "the khan divides the silk. After that, every tribe goes back to its own pastures."

"Then what were you doing out here?"

The young man glanced at the leader. The leader said nothing, so he answered for himself.

"The lesser khan's tribe, we put up the most horses, and we lost the most men. A hundred men, and we don't get one cartload." He licked his cracked lips. "South of here, there's a few villages. Not all burned yet."

When Zheng One got to that line, he stopped for a moment.

Wei One was standing not far off. He didn't understand Turkic, but he understood Zheng One.

The leader hadn't opened his mouth the whole time. Now he lifted his head. He wasn't looking at Ashina. He was looking at Shen You, squatting beside Chu One.

"That one who was eating the cake," he said in Turkic. "He's your chief?"

Ashina didn't answer.

The leader gave a short laugh. "Twenty years I've been beating the grass for grain, and that's a first. Men fighting down below, him up top watching, and he never dropped his cake."

He glanced at the stack of packs, then at Wei One. He'd been tied to that tree a long time, listening a long time, and every man in this valley spoke with a Guanzhong accent.

"Tang," he said slowly. "The Tang just bought a treaty with silk, and now they come all the way out here..."

Bit by bit, his eyes went wide.

"What you're after. It's before the fifth."

Nobody picked that up.

Chu One stood. "We're done asking."

They couldn't let him go. Let him go, and those words would be the first thing out of his mouth when he got back. They couldn't take him along either. Four Turkic riders: kept tied up in the valley, they needed guarding; taken on the road, they needed escorting; for the nights ahead, either way, they'd be dead weight. Chu One picked out four men. They untied the prisoners from the trees, led them up the slope, and went into the woods.

Wei One turned his back and faced the stream.

Shen You walked over and stood beside him for a while, saying nothing. Wei One's shoulders heaved and heaved. He made no sound either.

Soon there was no sound from the woods at all.

***

Early in the Hour of the Rooster, the sun dropped behind the ridge to the west, and the valley went dark all at once.

Chu One cleared a patch of ground at the second bend and drew the camp with a twig. A curving line was the river plain. A big circle on the plain was the camp. A small circle at its heart was the treasure carts.

"The khan's tent, it is north of the treasure carts." Ashina squatted beside him and tapped the spot with a finger. "The khan's people make camp, the rule, it does not change."

"Horses, they are tied downwind," Ashina went on. "The smell of horse dung, it does not go into the khan's tent."

Chu One drew a big patch at one corner outside the large circle, then a ring of little dots all around it. "The *fuli*. Three or four hundred riders."

"When a herd bolts, which way it runs," Cao One said, squatting at the edge to watch, "the lead mare decides."

Zhou Six opened that cloth bundle of hers and laid it out on the ground packet by packet: all henbane seed, ground fine, every packet wrapped in oiled paper.

"Seven jin," she said. "Light it upwind, and it'll drop the sentries in one ring. Burn it once, and it's gone."

"And getting more?"

"Go back to Chang'an and buy it up," Zhou Six said. "The apothecaries in Chang'an were stripped bare during martial law. This was all there was to be had."

Cao One got up and led the gray mare over. The gray mare still wasn't eating. Her head pointed north.

"The lead mare can only call once," Cao One said.

"Why?"

"First call, the herd follows her." Cao One stroked her neck. "The Turks hear it too. Second call, they know which horse is calling, and where."

He paused. "Then it's one arrow."

Shen You looked at the henbane, then at the gray mare.

"One go with the drug," he said. "One call from the mare."

Chu One tossed the twig away.

"Dawn on the fifth, they divide the silk," he said. "The night of the fourth."

Just the one night.

***

At that same hour, on the river plain twenty-five li away, Duoxi Wulue was counting carts.

He counted them every day before dark, one by one, pressing his palm to each cart's shaft. Eighty-two carts of silk, six carts of gold and silver vessels, and not one could be missing. This ring was everything the khan had brought home from one trip south. One bolt of silk short in this ring, and he, the irkin, would lose a layer of skin.

He was past fifty, one of Jieli's own clansmen, and had spent thirty years fighting on horseback. He didn't trust a Tang treaty, but he trusted Tang silk. If the Tang were willing to hand over this much to buy the khan's withdrawal, they were afraid. Men who were afraid didn't chase you across the Wei to take their things back.

The report from the sentries who looked back south came when he'd counted up to the sixty-first cart.

"At first light, five riders went out south. The lesser khan's men. Said they were looking for horses. It's nearly dark. They're not back."

Duoxi Wulue's hand stayed on the cart's shaft.

Men looking for horses came back before dark. Men who deserted took their wives and children. Gone beating the grass for grain? Maybe. Men out beating the grass for grain often didn't come back at night.

And maybe not. A man guarding the treasure carts couldn't afford to think the best.

He glanced east. The lesser khan's tents were pitched behind the slope at the east end of the plain, ten-odd li from the khan's main camp; the two never camped together. Yet these past few days, the lesser khan's men had been riding around behind the baggage, and more than once. At first he'd taken them for gawkers.

Dawn on the fifth, the silk would be divided. The lesser khan had been grumbling that it was too little, grumbling the whole way.

Duoxi Wulue took his hand off the shaft.

"Trip ropes. From tonight, another line outside the camp, and another outside the treasure carts."

"Double the roving sentries. At first light tomorrow, send a party south to bring those five back. If not the men, then the horses will do."

Not tonight. The roving sentries were already out, and if the southern sentries were shuffled around in the dark, the first ones it would alarm were the lesser khan's men.

"When the lesser khan's men come into camp, they take off their bows and hang them at the camp gate."

He paused, looking at the ring of carts.

"Until the silk is divided, nobody's men come within thirty paces of the treasure carts. The lesser khan's men included."

He had thought of everything.

What he was guarding against was Tuli.

In a valley twenty-five li away: seven jin of henbane, one lead mare.

Dawn on the fifth, the silk would be divided.

There was only one night: the fourth.
