# Chapter 15: Watching the Livestream

At the Hour of the Rat, nineteen more.

A hundred and ninety.

This time Shen You didn't fall asleep. He was soaking in the hot pool. Once he'd said "Summon," he rested his chin on the bluestone rim and listened to Zhou One, on the other side of the screen, questioning them one by one.

Jiang Four, Jiang Five, Jiang Six, Jiang Seven. Seven Qis. Seven Xies. The last one in line came out to Zou One.

One could burn charcoal. One could trim toenails and pare calluses. One could train hawks. When Zhou One got to the hawk trainer, he coughed once on the other side of the screen. Shen You guessed he was thinking of Dou the Eleventh's falcon.

"The one who does feet," Shen You said, eyes closed. "Have him see to mine tomorrow."

Beyond the screen there was a moment's silence.

"I have noted it."

In the east compound they were on the third pit now. The nineteen newcomers took over from the ones who'd been digging all day, and the spades bit into the raw earth, stroke after stroke, until the fourth watch.

***

The twenty-seventh day of the eighth month. The city gates opened at daybreak.

Ashina and Zheng One rode out through Kaiyuan Gate and headed north.

The road was full of people heading south. Families from north of the Wei pushed wheelbarrows loaded with bedding, earthenware jars, a chicken with its feet tied. Someone was driving sheep; the sheep didn't want to go, and a woman was switching them with a branch. One lash, three steps.

Only the two of them were going north.

Zheng One rode the yellow dun, half a length behind Ashina. He was an interpreter, and along the way he'd heard plenty. Some said the Turks had burned three villages outside Gaoling last night. Some said Jingyang had been won. An old charcoal seller said what had been beaten was only the vanguard; the khan's main host was still coming. His nephew, a militiaman at Jingyang, had sent word home through someone: run, and fast.

Ashina hadn't said a word the whole way. He rode the old chestnut with the black-lacquered horn bow on his back, and the bone thumb ring on his right thumb showed pale in the morning light. The people coming south saw his curly hair and high-bridged nose and moved to the side of the road well before they reached him, then turned to look back. The city was rounding up spies. A Turk going against the crowd toward the Wei—anyone would have taken a second look.

At the south end of the Middle Wei Bridge there were soldiers, ten of them, a single mess, with a big cart pulled crosswise to block half the bridgehead.

The mess leader was a dark-faced man. He swung his spear shaft out across their path.

"North?" He took one look at Ashina, and his hand went to the blade at his waist. "What for?"

"To bring people in," Zheng One said. "The Shen house, Kaiming Ward. Day before yesterday we answered the county's hiring call, hauling grain to Jingyang. They're due back today."

The mess leader's hand stopped on the hilt.

"The Shens? The ones with twenty carts?"

"That's right."

The mess leader thought about it, then pulled back his spear.

"The supply depot on the north bank cleared out this morning," he said. "Their outriders are just to the north. Ten li, eight li, can't say. Watch yourselves." He looked at Ashina again. "He one of yours too?"

"He tends the horses," Zheng One said.

The mess leader asked nothing more and waved them through.

The Middle Wei Bridge was over three hundred paces long. Leading their horses, the two of them kept tight to the railing and pushed against the flow of people for a good quarter of a double-hour before they reached the north end.

The depot on the north bank was empty. Where several thousand shi of grain had been piled the day before yesterday, there was nothing left but trampled straw rope and a few torn hemp sacks.

They waited beside the depot until the Hour of the Horse.

***

The first thing they saw was dust.

A long strip of yellow dust had risen on the high road to the north, and it was creeping their way. People in front, horses behind. The horses didn't keep together. They hobbled, they limped.

It was the pack train. Twenty people—three riding, seventeen on foot. The mules had been handed back to the army yesterday once the rations were delivered. Behind them came a herd of horses, untethered, with nobody driving them. They just followed. At the very front of the herd walked a short, bowlegged man with empty hands, who seemed to be humming something.

Cao One.

Zheng One let out a breath and was about to spur forward to meet them when Ashina suddenly reached out and pressed down on his reins.

"East."

On an earthen ridge some two hundred paces east of the pack train stood five men on horseback. Sheepskin coats, felt caps. The horses were small and short-legged, their manes hanging in tangles. When the pack train moved on a stretch, they moved along the ridge a stretch too.

Chu One raised a hand. The herd closed in toward the west side of the road, and the twenty people turned to face outward and spread into an arc—people on the outside, horses within. Nobody shouted. Nobody ran. In the saddle, Zheng Six unslung the Turkic bow he'd picked up and nocked an arrow, but didn't draw.

The five on the ridge stopped too.

"They want the horses," Ashina said.

"Those are wounded horses."

"They don't know," Ashina said. "They know only, those are their horses."

Only then did it come back to Zheng One: those twenty-four horses had belonged to the Turks in the first place.

Ashina pressed his heels in. The old chestnut trotted out, angling toward the ridge. About a hundred paces short of it, he reined in, unslung his bow, raised it crosswise over his head, and slung it on his back again.

Then he called out. In Turkic, with a northern-steppe accent, not fast. Zheng One nudged his horse after him a little way and stopped behind to listen.

First he asked which tribe they were.

The men on the ridge called back. Zheng One caught it clearly: Ashide.

All along the road, people had been passing the word that a great general surnamed Ashide had been taken at Jingyang. Now it fit.

Ashina called out again. This part was long. Zheng One put it into Tang speech in his head, committing it to memory word by word.

"Those horses are lame. Come down and see for yourselves. The men minding them are hard. The one with the bow can put an arrow through your felt cap at a hundred paces. There are five of you. There are twenty of them."

The men on the ridge made no sound.

"The khan reaches the Wei tomorrow," Ashina went on. "Once he's at the Wei, the Tang will come with silk to pay him to go home. When has it ever been otherwise? Then everyone gets a share. Die here over a few lame horses, and your share—who gets it?"

The wind came down off the ridge, carrying the rank smell of sheep.

After a long while, one of the men on the ridge turned his horse and left. The other four followed him over the ridge one after another. The last one, before he went, looked back and shouted one more thing in their direction.

Ashina didn't answer. He rode back and passed Zheng One with nothing at all on his face.

"That last part," Zheng One said. "What did he say?"

Ashina gave him a look. He knew Zheng One had understood.

"He says, tomorrow, the khan waters his horses north of the Bian Bridge," Ashina said. "He says I should come too."

***

At the Hour of the Monkey, the pack train came in through Kaiyuan Gate.

Twenty-four Turkic horses hobbled around the southwest corner of the Imperial City, turned onto Vermilion Bird Avenue, and went south all the way. They had no halters and no reins. Cao One walked in front, and they followed behind him—not in neat order, but not one fell behind. The lame gray mare kept close on Cao One's heels. When Cao One stopped to let a cart pass, she stopped too, and the rest all stopped with her and stood in the middle of the road, waiting.

Since martial law, the streets had emptied by more than half. Now, though, plenty of people came squeezing out of the ward gates to stand along the drainage ditches and watch.

"Turkic horses for the Shen house in Kaiming Ward? Nobody's leading them—how do they know to follow?"

A half-grown boy ran alongside the herd for two wards before his mother caught him by the ear and hauled him home. A patrolman opened his mouth to bark a challenge, saw the chit in the last man's hand with two officials' marks on it, and shut it again.

The herd entered Kaiming Ward. Warden Zhao stood by the ward gate counting. When he got to twenty-four, his mouth hung open and stayed that way.

***

At the south end of the east compound, a stable shed had been put up for the time being. Feng Three went over the horses one by one. When he'd finished the nineteenth, he straightened up and said to Shen You:

"Same as what I saw at Jingyang. Nineteen, rideable in half a month. Three, in a month."

"And the other two?"

Feng Three scratched his head.

"Those two are in foal," he said. "Yesterday I was afraid of getting it wrong. Today I felt them over again. No mistake. They'll drop come spring."

Shen You crouched at the edge of the stable shed, teasing the gray mare with a handful of hay. The gray mare ignored him, twisting her neck to look for Cao One.

"Come spring, that's twenty-six," Shen You said.

Feng Three blinked, then gave a little laugh and scratched his head again.

Shen You put the hay in Cao One's hand, stood up, and took a look around. Chu One had gone a shade darker. Zheng Six's lips had chapped and peeled. Feng Five stood at the very back with one hand clenched, the gray-white tip of a bone just showing from his fist. Twenty people, three days, a hundred and sixty or seventy li, and not one of them hurt.

"Go get washed up," he said. "Sanniang's stewing mutton tonight."

Only then did the twenty break up and head for the kitchen. Feng Five didn't go. He said a word to Zhou One, went out the ward's west gate, took Vermilion Bird Avenue south, and turned into Baoning Ward.

In the Meng family's threshing yard, Erlang was sweeping the floor where they spread the millet to dry. Feng Five walked in, took the bone arrowhead from the front of his tunic, set it in the boy's hand, and said one sentence.

Erlang stood in the middle of the threshing yard, gripping the arrowhead. Elder Meng came out of the house and wiped his sleeve across his forehead, then again. He didn't say anything. He turned, went back inside, and came out with a bowl of water.

Feng Five drank the water and left.

He was back just before the ward gate closed, and told Zhou One, "Delivered."

***

Zheng One told Shen You everything that had been said at the ridge, exactly as it was said.

Shen You was leaning back in the rocking chair. When he heard *the Tang will come with silk to pay him to go home*, his fingers went still on the armrest. The Wei River, silk, buying off an army so it would leave—those he recognized. What he hadn't expected was that people on the grassland had long since come to take it as the usual way of things. He looked over at Ashina under the veranda. Ashina still hadn't taken the bow off his back.

"'When has it ever been otherwise,'" Shen You repeated. "Which times?"

"The first years the Tang has the world, every year they send things to the grassland," Ashina said. "Fifth year of Wude, the khan comes as far as Fenzhou. The Tang envoy comes, and the khan goes home again, with things. I am already in Mayi then. People coming from the grassland, they all talk of it."

"How much did they get?"

"Each family gets two bolts. That is what I hear," Ashina said. "The khan's share, how much, nobody knows."

It was quiet under the veranda for a while.

Off to one side, Qingluo was brewing tea. The bamboo tongs in her hand paused, then went on stirring.

Shen You picked up his tea bowl and took a sip.

"'Tomorrow, the khan waters his horses north of the Bian Bridge,'" he said. "Then somebody in Chang'an has to go out and talk to him."

Zhou One looked at him. "The Master wants to go see who it is?"

"I do."

Zhou One's fingers went to twist his goatee and stopped partway.

"Commandant Lu ruled that the Master may not leave the capital."

"The rule I set myself was that crossing the Wei counts as leaving the capital. That's stricter than the code." Shen You smiled. "The south bank of the Bian Bridge. I stand on the south bank, and I don't take a single step north. Zhou One, you know the code. You tell me—does that count as leaving the capital?"

Zhou One said nothing. Nowhere did the code say where the edge of "the capital" was.

"Besides," Shen You said, "somebody's already paved my way out of the city."

Before the words were out of his mouth, Warden Zhao was shouting from over by the gatehouse.

He'd run so hard his forehead was beaded with sweat. Clutching a notice from the county, he stopped at the foot of the steps and bowed, lower than last time.

"Young Master Shen, the county wants carts again," he panted. "First thing tomorrow, loading fodder outside Kaiyuan Gate, to go to the camp south of the Wei. The scribe said your house is the first name on the ledger..."

"We'll go," Shen You said.

Warden Zhao had evidently not expected it to be that easy. He stood there a moment, bowed again, and ran off.

Zhou One stroked his goatee and gave a short laugh.

"The county ledger," he said. "I had been thinking of it as something to keep people out with, down the road."

"It keeps people out, and it lets you out too," Shen You said. "One sheet of paper. There's writing on both sides."

***

Zhou Four had straightened up the moment Shen You said "I do," and hadn't moved or spoken since.

"Zhou Four."

"The river," Zhou Four said. "Turks on the other side."

"How far?"

Zhou Four couldn't answer. He turned to look at Zheng Six.

Zheng Six was gnawing on a mutton bone. He put the bone down and wiped his hands on his sleeve.

"The soldiers at the depot said the river's narrow along the Bian Bridge stretch, a hundred-some paces across," he said. "Turkic bows are seven dou. Shooting flat, they're accurate to fifty paces. Lobbing, a hundred and fifty at the very most, and by then there's no force left in it. The Master stands on the south bank, two hundred paces back from the water. They can't reach."

"Three hundred-some paces," Zheng Six said. "To hit the Master, they'd have to cross the river first."

"And if they cross?"

Zheng Six thought about it.

"Then every soldier in Chang'an gets a good look at them."

Chu One was crouched at the edge of the steps. With a twig, he drew a line across the dirt—the river. South of it, a few circles—slopes. Around the circles he dotted a few points.

"How many are going?" he asked.

"Your call."

Chu One tapped the points on the ground with the twig. "Zhou Four stays with the Master, never more than three paces off. Wei One drives. Zheng Six and I ride—one watches the road in, one watches the river." He paused and looked at Ashina. "One more."

Ashina raised his head.

"Turkic business," Chu One said. "A Turk sees it clearest."

Shen You looked at Ashina.

The thumb ring on Ashina's thumb turned half a circle.

"I go," he said.

"Six people," Shen You said. "A bit thin?"

"More people draws eyes," Chu One said. "Tomorrow at the river, soldiers are the one thing there'll be no shortage of. Take Ashina, with the letter of manumission on him. If anyone asks, he shows it."

Shen You tapped a finger once on the armrest.

***

Only after everyone had dispersed did Zhou Five come over from the east compound, and behind him came a middle-aged man—not tall, with short, thick hands and calluses on every knuckle. This was Kong Five, who'd arrived only a few days earlier. His skill was "Cartwrighting."

"Master," Zhou Five said, "that carriage is done."

Shen You raised an eyebrow.

He'd long known a carriage was being built in the shed at the west end of the east compound. Zhou Five never said a thing until a job was finished. A few days ago Kong Five had arrived, crawled under it for one look, and torn out the whole underside to build it over.

Kong Five pulled off the hemp cover. It was a small, two-wheeled horse carriage. At first glance, it was no different from the carts people in the ward hired to go to market: plain wood with a coat of tung oil, no carving, no brass fittings, a square box, a bamboo blind across the front.

Shen You walked once around it, bent down, and looked under the box for a while. His eyes lit up.

The box didn't sit on the axle. At each of its four corners was an oxhide strap, two fingers wide, three layers of hide stitched together, and the other ends of the straps hung from two curved hardwood beams, one in front and one behind. The box was suspended.

"Have a seat inside, Master," Kong Five said.

Shen You climbed in. The box sank a little, swayed twice, and steadied. Inside was a silk-floss cushion, and the backrest was padded too. On his right was a small table with a round recess cut into the top, just the size for a tea bowl.

Outside, Kong Five lifted the shafts and let them drop, then lifted them again. The wheels jolted twice. All Shen You felt was a soft sway beneath him.

"A bowl," he said.

Zhou Five handed up a bowl of water, filled to the brim. Shen You set it in the round recess, and Kong Five lifted the shafts twice more.

The surface of the water rocked. Not a drop spilled.

Shen You looked at that bowl of water, and the corners of his mouth curled up, bit by bit.

"The panels of the box," Zhou Five said from outside, "are two layers, inside and out, with oil-soaked oxhide in between. Feng One's idea. An arrow from beyond thirty paces won't go through."

"The wheels are iron-shod. Feng One forged them," Kong Five said. "There's a hidden compartment under the box. It'll hold two *hengdao*."

"And the thing hanging off the back," Zhou Five said, pointing, "is the Master's chair."

Strapped to the tail of the carriage was a bundle of wood, held by leather buckles. It looked like a bundle of firewood. Zhou Five undid the buckles and, with a pull and a spread of his hands, the bundle opened with a *click*. It was a folding armchair: a backrest, armrests, two legs crossing each other, with a bronze pin through the crossing and a seat of tanned oxhide.

Shen You sat down in it and leaned back.

The angle of the backrest was exactly the same as the rocking chair in his back courtyard.

"Last time, the Master said," Zhou Five said, "he couldn't get used to kneeling, and couldn't get used to a folding stool either. A folding stool has no back."

Shen You sat in the folding chair and swung his feet.

"Zhou Five," he said, "if we took this carriage to market..."

"It's not for sale," Zhou Five said. "Making one takes half a month."

Shen You laughed out loud.

***

At the Hour of the Rat, twenty more.

Zou Two through Zou Seven. Seven Yus. Seven Bais.

Two hundred and ten.

Shen You was already asleep. He mumbled "Summon" in a daze and rolled over. Zhou One received the new arrivals outside the sleeping hall. When he got to Yu Four, whose skill was "Ice Cellaring," his brush paused, and he drew an extra circle on the roster.

The pits in the east compound had twenty more diggers.

***

The twenty-eighth day of the eighth month. The moment the ward gate opened, the convoy rolled out of Kaiming Ward. By the start of the Hour of the Dragon, it was outside Kaiyuan Gate.

In the transfer yard, millet stalks, alfalfa, and bean straw were stacked higher than a man. The county's scribe was still behind that same table. When the Shen house's turn came, he looked up and saw twenty carts, and twenty drivers in identical short tunics standing in a row.

He didn't ask "All yours?" He just picked up his brush and wrote: *Shen You, Kaiming Ward: twenty carts.* When he'd finished, he glanced again toward the back of the convoy.

At the back of the convoy was an extra carriage, a small one, pulled by the yellow dun. Its driver was a big man with shoulders as broad as a door panel, and on the shaft beside him sat a man who wasn't tall, one forearm wrapped in white cloth. Alongside the carriage were three riders: one with a square jaw, one tall man with a short bow on his back, and one more, who was a Turk.

The bamboo blind of the carriage lifted, and the man inside gave the scribe a nod.

Another drop of ink fell from the tip of the scribe's brush.

***

Northwest out of Kaiyuan Gate ran the high road to the Bian Bridge, which was also the high road to the Western Regions. On an ordinary day, camel bells rang along it from morning till night. Today there wasn't a single camel. The road was full of soldiers. A troop of cavalry overtook them from behind, and the dust they kicked up hit the drivers full in the face. A column of foot soldiers with long spears on their shoulders marched northwest without a word.

Shen You sat in the carriage with the bamboo blind half raised. The box swayed gently on its straps. Outside, the ox carts went *clunk, clunk*, while in his carriage the tea bowl sat steady in its round recess. He leaned back against the soft cushion shelling boiled chestnuts—shell one, eat one.

"Sanniang made these?"

"Sanniang made them." Zhou Four sat on the shaft with his back to him. "She said it's the end of the eighth month. Melons are done for the year. There's only chestnuts."

Shen You sighed.

"Watching a show like this," he said, "you need melon."

After a while, Zhou Four asked, "What melon?"

"Any melon."

Zhou Four thought about it, couldn't make sense of it, and stopped asking.

Early in the Hour of the Snake, the people on the high road suddenly began making way to either side.

A dozen-odd Tang horsemen came from the northwest with three men between them: tall horses, brocade robes with turned-down lapels, gold belt plaques, braids hanging down their backs. They didn't look like prisoners under guard, and they didn't look like guests under escort either.

One of them, a Turk of about forty with a very broad face, let his eyes sweep both sides of the road. They passed over the Shen carriage, paused for a moment on Ashina in the saddle, and swept on.

Ashina kept his head down until the party was well past. Only then did he raise it.

"Zhishi family," he said.

Shen You lifted the bamboo blind.

"You know him?"

"Zhishi Sili. A man beside the khan whose word counts," Ashina said. "The khan sends him into the city, to see how strong the Tang is, how weak."

"I'm guessing he won't be coming back today," Shen You said.

Ashina turned his head to look at him.

"Just a guess," Shen You said, and let the blind drop.

Chu One, on horseback, glanced into the carriage, said nothing, and went back to watching the road.

***

At the Hour of the Horse, the convoy reached the camp south of the Bian Bridge.

The camp had been pitched on a stretch of high ground five or six li from the river, its wooden palisade only half up. A man who looked like a granary officer checked the carts one by one against his ledger. When he'd finished, he waved a hand: empty carts back to the city.

Zheng One and Chen Five led the twenty empty carts back. Chen Five hugged the ledger to his chest. Thin and pale, he looked like a bookkeeper. He'd always looked like a bookkeeper.

Shen You's carriage didn't go back. It went north, toward the river. Chu One rode in front, Zheng Six to the side, and Ashina behind the carriage. With a little over two li still to go to the river, the soldiers along the road grew fewer. Ahead rose an earthen slope, three zhang or so high, its top flat, with a crooked jujube tree growing on it, the jujubes hanging half red, half green. Chu One studied it from the foot of the slope for a while, then nodded back at the others.

There were already people squatting on the slope—laborers who'd unloaded their carts and wouldn't leave. Some were chewing roasted beans. One old man held a green-skinned muskmelon in his arms, a length of wilted vine still attached to the stem.

Wei One stopped the carriage on the south side of the slope, where it couldn't be seen from the river. The folding chair went up beneath the jujube tree at the top. Zhou Four looked all around, then moved the chair two steps to the east: the jujube tree at its back, the path up from below on its left, a thicket of wild jujube on its right that no one could get through.

Shen You sat down and looked north.

***

The Wei was some two hundred paces below the slope—yellow, broad, the water shoving the water ahead of it eastward. The Bian Bridge was a li and more to the west, row upon row of wooden piers planted in the river, and not a single person on the deck.

At the far end of the bridge, on the north bank of the Wei, were the Turks.

Never in his life had Shen You seen so many people, or so many horses.

North from the river, east to west, as far as the eye could reach, the ground was black, brown, gray, and moving. Felt tents stood one against the next, from the river flats all the way up onto the tableland to the north, and there were more beyond the tableland. Horse herds flowed back and forth between the tents like a pot of porridge at a rolling boil. The wind came from the north, crossing the river and bringing with it a smell so thick you could almost chew it: sheep, horse dung, the smoke of burning cow dung, and the char of roasting meat.

Shen You opened his mouth, then closed it again. None of the laborers on the slope were talking either.

In the middle stood a great standard, taller than all the other banners. At the top of its pole was something that glittered gold, too far away to make out.

"Wolf head," Ashina said. "Golden wolf head. The khan's standard."

He stood half a step behind the folding chair with nothing on his face. Only the bone thumb ring on his right thumb turned slowly in the palm of his left hand.

Once around. Then once around again.

Shen You didn't turn. He felt in the food box for a cake, broke it in half, and held one half out behind him.

Ashina hesitated, then took it.

***

The food box had three tiers. In the bottom tier lay a few pieces of charcoal burned through, with an iron plate pressed over them, and in the tier above the plate were sesame flatbreads, still warm. The second tier held boiled chestnuts and jujubes. The third held Snow Sugar cakes from the pastry shop, white as snow. One bite and you were covered in sugar dust.

Shen You finished a cake and brushed the sugar off his robe. The old man with the melon was squatting on the other side of the jujube tree, seven or eight paces off, sneaking looks at his folding chair.

"Elder," Shen You said, "would you sell that melon?"

The old man clutched the melon tight.

"No," he said. "Our land's north of the Wei. The millet we never got in, it's lying out in the field. This one melon's all I carried out. My grandson's in the city, waiting on it."

"Then don't sell it," Shen You said.

The old man looked at the people standing behind him, then at the chair, and seemed to decide this young master wasn't going to grab it. Slowly, his arms loosened a little.

"What's that you're sitting on, young master?"

"A chair."

"What's a chair?"

Shen You thought about it. "A folding stool you can lean back in."

The old man said, "Oh." Then: "Sit in it too long, don't your back ache?"

"It's because I'm worried my back'll ache that I lean back."

The old man shook his head, evidently feeling that a young fellow who needed something to lean on even on a folding stool would never amount to much. He shifted the melon to his other arm and turned to look north.

Shen You shelled a chestnut, looked at that melon, and sighed.

A show, and no melon.

***

At the Hour of the Goat, it was the north bank that moved first.

Horns came first, rising from beneath the great standard—low, long, like lowing cattle—and then the whole north bank was sounding. The horse herds parted to either side, and row upon row of cavalry formed up along the river flats.

Every laborer squatting on the slope stood up.

"Here they come, here they come!"

"Are they coming across?"

"They're not across, they're not across..."

Zhou Four moved half a step forward and took up a place at Shen You's front left.

Zheng Six stood under the jujube tree, squinting east. "East."

On the south bank of the Wei, along the riverside road, a few black dots were bobbing—horses at a trot. Zheng Six counted them one by one:

"One, two, three, four, five, six. Six riders."

Everyone on the slope went quiet.

The six riders came west along the south bank. No banners, no drums. Just six men, six horses. As they drew nearer, the lead rider's horse showed white, and the armor on the man flashed in the sun, and flashed again.

"The one in front isn't wearing a helmet," Zheng Six said. "Of the five behind, two aren't either. One sits unsteady—not used to the saddle. A civil official."

Shen You sat in the folding chair and didn't move. He was holding a half-shelled chestnut and had forgotten to put it in his mouth.

The six riders reached the south end of the Bian Bridge and halted a few dozen paces from the water. The din of tens of thousands on the other bank dropped all at once.

Then, in the row of cavalry nearest the river on the north bank, the riders dismounted one after another and looked across. Some bent at the waist. Some went down on their knees.

"They bow," Ashina said.

His voice was very soft, as if he was afraid of being heard.

"What are they bowing to?" Shen You asked.

"To the khan of the Tang," Ashina said. "He rides to the river himself, only six men. On the grassland, only a true khan dares this."

Shen You put the half chestnut in his mouth and chewed it slowly.

Where he'd read about it didn't matter. How that line had gone didn't matter either. He was sitting on this slope now, watching these six horses, and it was exactly like those few lines of text.

Exactly like it. And nothing like it at all.

Those few lines of text had no wind in them, no smell of sheep, none of that silence when tens of thousands of people on the far bank went quiet all at once.

***

"Behind us." Chu One stood at the very top of the slope. He'd been watching the southeast the whole time.

Dust was rising in the southeast.

Not a strip. A whole expanse. The dust rolled up off the horizon, wider and wider, and beneath the dust were banners, spearpoints, rank upon rank of armor. Banner after banner came into view—red, yellow, black—and the armor plates in the sun ran together into a single blaze too bright to look at.

"How many?" Shen You asked.

Chu One watched for a while.

"The ones we can see," he said, "twenty thousand and up. The ones we can't, still coming."

The old man with the melon stood with his mouth open and very nearly dropped the melon.

The army pressed forward to two or three li from the river and slowed.

At the south end of the Bian Bridge, the unsteady rider among the six came out to meet the lead rider and reached over to grab his horse's reins.

"An old man," Zheng Six said. "Trying to talk him out of it."

The lead rider didn't move. After a while, the old man let go.

The lead rider turned toward the army behind him, raised one hand, and waved it back.

The army halted.

In less time than it takes to eat a meal, the high ground on the south bank had formed into one great array: foot soldiers in the center, cavalry on both wings, banners ranked in a single line at the front.

Then the lead rider rode on alone, all the way to the water's edge. The five behind him stayed where they were, and farther back, of the tens of thousands in the great array, not a single man moved.

On the far bank, a dozen-odd riders came out from beneath the golden wolf-head standard, rode out onto the river flat as well, and stopped. The one in the middle was on a black horse.

Two men, with a hundred-some paces of the Wei between them.

One had tens of thousands of Tang troops at his back. The other had more than a hundred thousand Turks at his.

Between them was the yellow water, shoving the water ahead of it, rushing east.

On the slope, nobody made a sound.

Shen You sat in the folding chair and leaned back a little.

He suddenly wanted to laugh. A show this big, with more than a hundred thousand in the audience, and he had a front-row seat—and a chair he could lean back in, at that. All he was missing was a melon.

But the laugh didn't come out. He was watching that rider at the water's edge.

"If he didn't have those tens of thousands behind him," Shen You said, "this would be suicide."

Chu One glanced at him.

"If those tens of thousands were marching out in front of him," Shen You went on, "this would be war."

"And what does the Master say it is now?"

"He's out in front, with tens of thousands standing behind him, and not one of them moves," Shen You said. "This is haggling over the price."

Chu One said nothing. He looked at the array a while longer, his palm sketching out the left and right wings at his side, and nodded.

***

The two men talked across the river for a long time. What they said, no one on the slope could hear. The one on the black horse on the far bank would ride forward two steps, then rein in and circle in place; the rider on this side, at the water's edge, never moved.

Ashina kept watching the one on the black horse.

"Jieli," he said.

Shen You didn't ask him how he could tell. At this distance, no one could make out a face. What Ashina recognized probably wasn't the face.

The bone thumb ring went still in Ashina's palm. It didn't turn anymore. He watched for a long time.

"He is old now."

That was all. He said nothing more.

The sun slid a little to the west. On the far bank, the man on the black horse turned and rode back, and the dozen-odd riders with him went faster than they had come.

The rows of cavalry near the river on the north bank pulled back, row by row. They withdrew a hundred-odd paces, halted, then withdrew a stretch further. On the south bank, the rider at the water's edge also turned his horse, rejoined the other five, and rode back into the great array.

Only then did the laborers on the slope let out their breath. Some sat down hard on the ground.

"No fighting?"

"No fighting."

"The emperor... that was the emperor, wasn't it?"

"Six men! Just six men!"

Shen You shelled the last chestnut in his hand, ate it, and dusted the crumbs off his hands.

"And that's the end of the stream," he said under his breath.

Zhou Four looked back at him.

"What did the Master say?"

"I said the show's over."

***

It wasn't only the slope that was clearing out.

Tang outriders came out of the array and fanned out along the riverbank in both directions, driving the onlookers south.

"Move! Everybody move! No standing by the river!"

The laborers scattered. The old man with the melon ran out in front of all of them, the melon clutched tight in his arms.

Shen You didn't leave.

He felt in the food box for another cake.

Chu One whistled—two short notes. None of them turned around, but every one of them was standing somewhere else now: Wei One had moved onto the path up the south side of the slope; Zheng Six stood on a high spot at the top that looked out three ways; Ashina had drawn back behind the carriage. Zhou Four was still where he'd been, except that his body had turned half an inch toward his right rear.

A dozen-odd riders came up the slope.

In the lead was a black horse. The man on its back was under forty, very tall, his iron armor polished to a shine, no helmet. His face was purplish red, and his short whiskers stuck out every which way, like a brush nobody had bound properly. In his hand he carried a cavalry lance whose shaft was longer than he was tall.

The riders behind him spread out left and right without waiting for orders and ringed half the hilltop.

"Whose people are you?" His voice was like a struck gong.

Shen You stood, put the cake back in the food box, and made a folded-hands salute.

"The Shen house, Kaiming Ward. We answered the county's hiring call—hauling fodder to the camp."

The man on the black horse looked past him, taking his time over it: the folding chair, the food box, the ground under the jujube tree littered with chestnut shells.

"Fodder goes to the camp," he said. "You're sitting here. That's hauling fodder?"

"It's been delivered," Shen You said. "The carts went back ahead. The people are slower."

"Slower, and lugging a chair?"

"Get tired walking, a man's got to sit."

One of the riders behind him snorted a laugh and hurriedly swallowed it.

The man on the black horse didn't laugh. He tapped his riding whip against his boot, tap after tap, and looked at Wei One, at Zheng Six, at the short bow in Zheng Six's hand.

The whip stopped.

"A Turkic bow," he said.

He looked toward the back of the carriage. Ashina stood there—curly hair, high nose, the black-lacquered horn bow on his back.

A dozen-odd riders on the slope, a dozen-odd hands went to their hilts at once.

"A Turk. A Turkic bow," the man on the black horse said slowly. "By the river, up on a slope. The emperor's down there talking with Jieli, and you're up here sitting, shelling chestnuts."

He shifted the lance to his left hand.

"You tell me," he said. "What am I supposed to take you for?"

***

The wind blew in from the river.

Zhou Four didn't move. Chu One didn't move. Zheng Six's arrows were in his quiver; his hand hung at his side, a palm's width from it.

Shen You didn't move either.

"Ashina," he said, "show the general that paper of yours."

Ashina took a sheet of paper, folded several times, from the front of his robe, stepped forward, and held it up in both hands. One of the riders took it, unfolded it, and read aloud:

"*...the household slave so-and-so, of a surrendered Turkic household, having served his master for some years, is now released to commoner status, free to go wherever he pleases... The ninth year of Wude, the twenty-sixth day of the eighth month.*"

"Freed the day before yesterday," said the man on the black horse.

"Freed the day before yesterday, came through my gate the day before yesterday, registered with the ward warden the day before yesterday, with a copy of the letter of manumission left on file there. The patrol post knows too," Shen You said. "Which room he lives in in Kaiming Ward—anyone in the ward can tell you."

"And the bow?"

"Picked it up at Jingyang," Zheng Six said.

"The day before yesterday, twenty of my people were at Jingyang hauling rations for the army," Shen You said. "Deputy Commandant Liang wrote us a chit—twenty-four wounded horses, as our carting fee. The chit's at home. If the general wants to see it, I'll have someone bring it to the camp tomorrow."

"Deputy Commandant Liang?" The man on the black horse narrowed his eyes. "Which Deputy Commandant Liang?"

"The one with his right arm in a sling," Zheng Six said.

The man on the black horse grunted, and the whip went back to tapping his boot.

"Liang Laosan," he said. "That arm still in one piece?"

"In one piece," Zheng Six said. "In a sling."

The man on the black horse fell silent. He looked at Shen You for a while, then asked:

"The emperor leaving the city—hardly anyone in the camp even knew. And yet you'd already taken the slope."

He didn't ask it fast, but everyone on the slope could hear it: this was the question that could get a man killed.

"When I took the slope, I came to watch the Turks make camp," Shen You said. "There were dozens of people squatting on this slope from the Hour of the Horse on. Your men just chased them off. The emperor came later."

He paused.

"I got lucky."

The man on the black horse stared at him.

"Lucky," he repeated.

"Then what were you watching?"

"The emperor."

Among the dozen-odd riders on the slope, someone sucked in a sharp breath.

"The emperor's for you to watch?"

"The emperor rode right up to the riverbank and stood there—wasn't that so people would see him?" Shen You said. "Six of them standing there, that was for the far bank to see. More than a hundred thousand on the far bank get to watch, and his own people on the south bank don't?"

The man on the black horse glared at him, his purplish-red face going redder still—and then he threw back his head and roared with laughter. The laugh was as loud as his voice. Down the slope, a horse snorted in alarm.

"That mouth of yours..." He pointed his whip at Shen You. "At Wagang, at Luoyang, in the Prince of Qin's household, I've seen plenty of hard-mouthed men. Never one so brazenly sure he was in the right."

Wagang.

Shen You's eyes lit up.

The build. The voice. The lance. The face full of unruly whiskers. Out of Wagang. A man of the Prince of Qin's household. He looked the man up and down all over again, the way he'd look over some new curiosity at the West Market.

"Cheng..." It was out before he could stop it. The second half rolled around in his mouth for a moment and came out anyway. "...Yaojin?"

***

The slope went quiet.

The laugh vanished from the face of the man on the black horse. The whip stopped against his boot.

"What did you call me?"

The riders behind him exchanged looks. Two of the young ones had probably never even known their general had that name.

Shen You rubbed his nose.

"I heard the general was called Yaojin," he said. "Slip of the tongue."

"Heard it from whom?"

"There are plenty of Wagang men in Chang'an," Shen You said. "Plenty of loose lips, too."

Cheng Zhijie, as the court rolls had him, looked at him for a long time.

"Out of Wagang, still alive, in Chang'an, and with the nerve to call me that to my face," he said. "I can count them on my ten fingers."

"Then the general can go back and ask them one by one."

"I will."

"Then you'll be asking for a good while," Shen You said.

Cheng Zhijie's eyes narrowed to slits. He let that one go. His gaze shifted half an inch to the side and landed on Zhou Four.

"You." He pointed with the whip. "Name?"

"Zhou Four."

"When I came up the slope, I sent three men around the back," Cheng Zhijie said. "You didn't turn your head once."

"Saw them," Zhou Four said.

Behind the slope, the three riders who'd circled around only now poked their heads out from the wild jujube thicket. They looked up at the slope, then at their general, not sure whether to come over or stay put.

Cheng Zhijie looked at Chu One, at the high spot where Zheng Six stood, at the path Wei One was blocking. When he'd finished looking, he shifted the lance back to his right hand.

"Kaiming Ward. Shen." Something had come back to him. "A few days ago, Yuwen Yan of the Left Encampment Guard sent an urgent dispatch into the Eastern Palace. Said there was a monk in Kaiming Ward who'd gone back to lay life, and every one of his house guards could serve as a squad leader. That's you?"

Without thinking, Shen You touched his *futou*. Underneath it was a crew cut, too short to tie up into a topknot.

"Back to lay life now," he said.

"Black Yuwen's led troops for ten years, and he doesn't hand out praise," Cheng Zhijie said. "I figured he'd had too much to drink."

He looked at Zhou Four again.

"Now I see it. He hadn't had enough."

***

Cheng Zhijie turned his horse's head as though to leave, then stopped.

"Be inside the city before dark," he said. "If I see you by the river again tomorrow, I'll take you in as a spy."

"I won't be coming tomorrow," Shen You said.

"Why not?"

"Nothing's on at the river tomorrow."

Cheng Zhijie gave him a look.

"How do you know nothing's on?"

"Today's performance is over," Shen You said. "Tomorrow's all backstage."

Cheng Zhijie didn't understand "backstage," but he understood the first half. He looked at this young man—twenty-eight or twenty-nine, not a single callus on his hands, shelling chestnuts across the river from more than a hundred thousand Turks—and spun the whip once in his hand.

His gaze fell on the food box beside the folding chair.

"What's that?"

Shen You opened the top tier of the food box, took out a Snow Sugar cake, and handed it up.

Cheng Zhijie took a bite, chewed twice, and his chewing slowed.

"This sugar..."

"Snow Sugar."

"The palace's Snow Sugar?"

"Same house," Shen You said.

Cheng Zhijie chewed the cake and stared at Shen You for a while. Then he reached into the food box and took two more. One went into his mouth; the other he tossed over his shoulder, and a rider behind him fumbled and caught it.

"Move out!"

The dozen-odd riders followed the black horse down the slope.

***

Cheng Zhijie and his men were down the slope and half a li away before a guardsman pulled up alongside him.

"General, we're just letting them go?"

"We're letting them go."

"That Turk..."

"The letter of manumission has a signature mark on it and a thumbprint, and the date checks out," Cheng Zhijie said. "Liang Laosan's arm is in a sling on the right side, and the one with the bow could tell me so."

The guardsman said nothing more.

Cheng Zhijie jerked his chin at two of his guardsmen. "You two. Follow them at a distance and watch them go in through Kaiyuan Gate. Whatever's in the carriage, don't lift the curtain. Just see whose gate they go in."

Cheng Zhijie rode on, the sweetness of Snow Sugar still in his mouth.

It wasn't the letter of manumission he was thinking about.

He was thinking about that chair. A bronze pin through the place where the legs crossed—folded up, it would probably look like nothing more than a bundle of firewood. He was thinking about the carriage parked on the south side of the slope, its box hung from leather straps, its panels too thick to be ordinary carriage panels. He was thinking about where those few men had stood: one blocking the road in, one holding the high ground, one guarding the carriage, and a short one at the young man's front left. Three soldiers had come up from behind, and the short one hadn't turned his head once—but his shoulders had turned first, half an inch.

He'd been fighting wars for more than ten years. He knew what that half inch meant.

He thought, too, about how that young man, watching the six riders, had held half a chestnut and forgotten to put it in his mouth. Watching the emperor like he was watching a variety show.

And then there was "Yaojin."

"Note it down," he said. "Kaiming Ward. Shen You."

The guardsman acknowledged it.

"And," Cheng Zhijie said, "when we get back, find out which of the Wagang men now in Chang'an has loose lips."

***

Early in the Hour of the Monkey, Shen You's carriage started back.

Going back, it was only the one carriage and three riders. The yellow dun trotted along easily, and the box swung on its straps, back and forth, like a cradle.

Shen You leaned on the soft cushion, watching the fields slide away outside. The millet had been cut. One patch hadn't been finished, its golden heads drooping, with no one to tend it.

"Zhou Four."

"Master."

"That general back there looked at you three times."

"Four," Zhou Four said. "Once coming up the slope. Once asking my name. Once when he talked about Senior Colonel Yuwen. Once before he left."

Shen You laughed and leaned back.

"If he tries to poach you..."

"I won't go," Zhou Four said.

Shen You laughed out loud, so hard the box swayed on its straps. When he'd finished laughing, he looked out the window and thought about the man who'd halted tens of thousands with one wave of his hand, and about the one on the black horse across the river, who'd ridden a very long way to get there, circled a few times on the riverbank, and turned around and gone back.

More than a hundred thousand men, one afternoon, and not a single arrow loosed.

He remembered there was more to this story after this part. What came next again... a treaty, a white horse, and...

Ashina's words sounded in his ear again: *the Tang will come with silk to pay him to go home.*

Those times before, they'd paid. In the fifth year of Wude, they'd paid.

And this time?

***

The Hour of the Rooster. Kaiyuan Gate.

The sun hung over the west of the city, red as a piece of charcoal burned through. In the gate tunnel, the soldiers were already shouting for people to hurry.

The county's scribe was packing up that same table. When he saw the Shen carriage, he put down his brush and came over to meet it.

"Young Master Shen!"

The bamboo blind lifted.

"Your carts just went in," the scribe said. "Don't disband them tomorrow. The county's received a notice from the Court of the Treasury. They want carts—a hundred—at the Imperial City before the middle of the Hour of the Dragon, and your twenty are first in line."

"The Court of the Treasury," Shen You said. "Hauling what?"

The scribe glanced left and right and lowered his voice.

"We're not allowed to ask," he said. "All they said was, the carts load up at the gate of the Left Treasury."

The Left Treasury.

On the shaft, Zhou Four's back straightened a little. On horseback, Chu One turned his head.

"Hauling it where?"

The scribe didn't answer. He tipped his chin toward the northwest. To the northwest was the Bian Bridge, and at the far end of the Bian Bridge were more than a hundred thousand Turks.

For a long moment Shen You didn't say a word.

"A hundred carts," he said.

"A hundred to start," the scribe said. "How many more after that, even the county doesn't know."

The shouting in the gate tunnel rose another notch. The scribe cupped his hands in salute, hugged his table to him, and ran.

Wei One shook out the reins, and the carriage rolled into the gate tunnel. It was dark in there, and cool, and the sound of the wheels droned back at them off the walls.

Shen You leaned back against the soft cushion and smiled in the dark.

"Zhou Four."

"Master."

"When we get back, tell Zheng Three," he said, "she goes with the carts tomorrow."

"Zheng Three?"

"She can count," Shen You said. "How many bolts to a cart, how many carts in all. At the gate of the Left Treasury, it's our house's carts hauling for the court. However much we haul, that's how much we count."

The carriage came out of the tunnel, and daylight fell into it again, landing on the round recess in the little table. The tea bowl still sat in it. Not a drop had spilled.

"I'd like to see," he said, "just how much the court is going to spend this time to send the Turks home."
