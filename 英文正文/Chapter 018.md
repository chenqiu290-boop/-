# Chapter 18: Across the Bian Bridge

Early in the Hour of the Snake. Kaiyuan Gate.

The city wall stood one zhang and eight chi high, the gate tunnel some four zhang deep. Walking into it brought a breath of cool air, and in the cool air was the smell of camel piss and cow dung. There were three tunnels. The middle one stayed shut; traffic went in on the left and out on the right. Today both were jammed solid—cart after cart of millet and firewood coming in, family after family going out, dragging their pots and bowls home to the north side of the Wei. Outside the tunnels, carts were lined up for two li. One old woman had a chicken cage lashed to her back with three hens in it, clucking all the way. One man was pushing a wheelbarrow with a dismantled loom tied on top, the shuttle sticking out between the hemp ropes; it scraped a camel beside him, the camel turned its head and glared, and he hurried to smile an apology. At the mouth of the tunnel, a sesame-flatbread seller with a shoulder pole was pushing out against the flow, shouting "Hot ones! Hot ones!" as he went. After a long while of pushing he still hadn't gotten out, so he simply started selling right where he stood.

The gate clerk on duty was surnamed Dong, in his forties, his eyes bloodshot from too many sleepless nights. He clutched a roll of name lists and checked every travel pass one by one, counted every head one by one. He hadn't been home in ten days. Yesterday evening his wife had sent word through someone: if he didn't come home soon, she wouldn't be leaving the door unbarred for him at night.

"Brother Dong!" Kang Satuo dismounted a good way off and walked over leading his horse. "Is Sister-in-law's cough any better?"

When Clerk Dong saw who it was, the misery on his face eased a little. "Better. That packet of pepper of yours did the trick."

"This'll do the trick today." Kang Satuo pressed a small paper packet into his hand. "Snow Sugar. For the children."

Clutching the packet, Clerk Dong unrolled the travel pass and read. Three hundred and ten camels, a hundred and thirty-one horses, two hundred and seventeen people. He tucked the pass under his arm, squinted, and began counting camels.

"One, two, three..."

Somewhere in the forties, the forty-somethingth camel twisted its neck and spat at a guard standing beside him. The gob was yellow and green, and it splattered square across the guard's breastplate. The guard hopped about cursing, everyone around burst out laughing, and Clerk Dong stood there with his mouth open, his count gone.

He looked at the spit. He looked at the two hundred-odd camels that hadn't yet come through the tunnel. He looked at the two li of carts outside it.

"Go, go, go!" He waved them on. "Count them one by one till dark, and everyone back there sleeps in the gate tunnel tonight!"

The numbers on the travel pass matched the people and animals in the train without a single discrepancy—Zhou One had added them in one by one according to the true figures the Kang family reported. Since Clerk Dong never finished counting, he would never know that.

One camel after another, the train entered the tunnel. Alongside Shen You's camel went a party on horseback, also heading out: a gray-bearded manager, four strapping household retainers, a maid riding a mule, and in their midst a young woman on a pale gray horse, wearing a veiled hat.

In the middle of the tunnel, an ox cart coming into the city sank into a rut. The wheel jammed fast; the ox hauled forward, the cart slid back, and the whole outbound lane was blocked at a stroke. People ahead and behind were crushed together, horses' heads up against horses' rumps.

Shen You's camel, with nothing better to do, stretched out its neck and took a mouthful of the gauze on the veiled hat beside it.

The hands of two retainers went to their hilts at the same moment.

Zhou Four's left hand went to the bell under the camel's neck and closed around it. The ringing stopped. Chen Five, two paces ahead, and Chu Four, two paces behind, shifted two paces each, one left and one right, shifting just as if the crowd had pushed them there.

The young woman didn't cry out and didn't pull back. Her left hand caught hold of the gauze in the camel's mouth. Her right hand turned her rattan whip around, and with its silver-capped tip she tapped the camel twice on the upper lip—not too hard, not too soft, and exactly on the spot.

The camel let go, pulled its neck back with a thoroughly wronged look, and turned its head away to ignore everyone.

Shen You leaned down from the camel's back. "Sorry about that. It hasn't had breakfast."

She looked up.

The panel of gauze at the front of her veiled hat had been lifted and pinned up on the brim, leaving half her face showing. Eighteen or nineteen. Thick, straight brows that had never seen a brush; a pointed chin; long, narrow eyes, the lids half lowered, so that when she looked at someone it was as if she were checking a ledger. No powder on her face, and a few faint freckles where the sun had caught the bridge of her nose. She wore a brown jacket with narrow sleeves, a blue-green skirt tucked into her boots, and a leather tally pouch at her waist, its surface worn to a shine.

She gave him one look from below. Looking down through a curtained hat's gauze was like looking through mist. Looking up through it from below, you could see the chin.

"That camel of yours," she said, "looks more like a Hu merchant than you do."

Behind the gauze, Shen You's eyebrows went up.

"Then what do I look like?"

"Like some family's young master"—her eyes passed over Zhou Four's hand clamped on the camel bell, then flicked once to either side and landed on Chen Five and Chu Four, not near and not far—"out with a pack of camel guards who don't look like camel guards."

Shen You laughed out loud. He glanced down at the tally pouch at her waist in turn.

"Your tally pouch, miss, is more worn than my bookkeeper's. Going out to collect debts?"

"Counting houses," she said. "To see how many burned."

Up ahead, seven or eight men pushing and lifting together finally got the ox cart out of the rut. The crowd loosened all at once and surged forward. The gray-bearded manager called from the front: "Twelfth Daughter! We're going!"

She turned her horse's head, and the pale gray slipped sideways past the camel's legs and was gone. She didn't look back.

Shen You sat on the camel's back and was carried out of the tunnel step by step. The sun flashed full onto the gauze of his hat.

"Twelfth Daughter," he said, trying it out.

"The four retainers have seen blood," Zhou Four said. "The manager's got a blade at his waist. Left-handed."

"She saw."

"She saw."

"Saw, and didn't shout." Behind the gauze curtain, with the slice of ginger still in his mouth, Shen You smiled. "Interesting."

***

Outside Kaiyuan Gate, the main road bent northwest.

The Lu family party reined in at the roadside below an earthen milepost to let the camel train go by. The gray-bearded manager muttered that somebody ought to keep the Hu merchants' camels in line, too, and muttered that if the master knew Twelfth Daughter had gone out of the city by herself to look at the estate... Twelfth Daughter didn't answer.

If the master did find out, it would only be that same old line: a girl should look over the accounts and leave it at that—what business has she looking at estates? But she had been looking over those accounts for three years. The estate north of the Wei—every year the foreman reported drought, and every year he handed in two-tenths less. Last summer she'd gone to see for herself, and the well at the edge of the fields had been full. This time, with the Turks passing through, the foreman's report said "most of it burned." How many buildings had burned, she meant to count herself.

She was counting horses.

Behind the camel train, a line of horses followed on strings. The ones carrying loads—she'd counted—came to forty-seven. The ones with riders, fifty-two. The ones with empty backs, strung together and trailing along, thirty-two.

A camel train taking a few spare horses to change mounts wasn't unusual. Spares were always mixed nags—as long as they could walk, they'd do. She'd kept the estate accounts for three years and could recite horse prices from memory. These thirty-two spares were all well fleshed and young in the mouth, with new shoes on their hooves, the heads of the nails still bright. To use horses like these as relief for a camel train—there wasn't a Hu merchant in the West Market that free with money.

She looked once more at the curtained hat on that camel.

Not her ledger.

"Let's go," she said, and pressed her heels in. The pale gray broke into a run, and in the blink of an eye the camel train was left behind.

***

Early in the Hour of the Horse. The camp on the south bank of the Wei.

The Shen family's gatekeeper had come on a donkey. He'd left as soon as the ward gate opened, been stuck in the city gate tunnel for the better part of a double-hour, and bounced along all the way to the Wei. The donkey was tied to a willow outside the camp, and the man stood outside the camp gate—stood there for half a double-hour. He was in his forties, in a robe of plain undyed hemp washed nearly white, holding a flat wooden box in both hands, and from start to finish he never once changed his stance. The soldiers at the camp gate had glared at him sidelong at first. After a while they couldn't keep it up, and went in to announce him.

The one who came out was the general on the black horse himself—no armor, an old robe hanging open, half a sesame flatbread still in his hand.

The gatekeeper bowed. Not too high, not too low: down, one breath's pause, up.

"From the Shen family of Kaiming Ward, a chit bearing the mark of Deputy Garrison Commandant Liang, for the General's inspection." He held up the wooden box in both hands. "Some days ago, on the slope, the Master promised the General it would be delivered the next day. There's been a great deal going on at home, and it is two days late. The Master bade this humble one offer his apologies first."

The general on the black horse took the box, opened it, shook out the chit, and read it through. When he got to the two marks at the end, he gave a lopsided grin.

"Liang Laosan's writing still looks like a dog crawled across it." He stuffed the chit back into the box. "Where's your employer?"

"The Master caught a chill last night. He mustn't be out in the wind."

"A chill?"

The riding whip in the general's hand tapped once against his boot, then once more, then stopped.

"That day on the slope, the wind was stronger than it is today."

The gatekeeper stood with his hands at his sides and didn't add one word.

The general looked at him a while, then handed the wooden box back. "Go back and tell your employer I've seen the chit. The horses were Liang Laosan's to give. That's none of my business. Tell him to take good care of them."

The gatekeeper bowed again—exactly the same depth as when he'd arrived—then turned and left.

The general stood at the camp gate watching that washed-out hemp robe recede into the distance, and stuffed the rest of the flatbread into his mouth.

"Martial law's just been lifted, the city gates have just opened," he said, chewing, the words coming out muffled, "and he's caught a chill."

His guardsman stood by, waiting.

"Note it down."

***

Past the transfer yard outside the city, the road angled off to the northwest. Off to the right, beyond a stretch of harvested fields, ran the west wall of the Forbidden Park—rammed earth, twice the height of a ward wall. There were half as many soldiers on top of it as there had been during the days of martial law. The dust kicked up by that Twelfth Daughter's party had long since settled.

The road widened, the camels lengthened their stride, and the swaying grew worse. Shen You held the ginger Zhou Six had given him in his mouth, now hugging the front hump, now leaning back on the rear crossbar, now bracing both hands on the seat to lift his backside and give it some air. Ten li along, he'd finally figured out the knack: you couldn't sway along with it. You had to let it do the swaying, and hang on top as limp as a sack of millet.

"Zhou Four."

"Here."

"Do I look like a sack of millet now?"

Zhou Four turned to look at him and studied him seriously for a while.

"Yes."

All along the way, the road was full of people heading north for home. Ten-odd days ago they'd fled south; now they were walking north again, with half as many things on their carts, and a few fewer people too. A child of three or four sat on a wheelbarrow, staring at the camels, staring so hard his mouth hung open. Behind the gauze curtain, Shen You waggled his fingers at him, and the child buried his head in his mother's arms—then, after a while, poked half his head back out.

Five or six li short of the bridge, under a crooked willow by the road, squatted a melon seller.

A skinny old man with a bamboo basket in front of him, seven or eight muskmelons in it, green rinds dusted white with bloom. Beside it, on a ragged mat, lay a pile of jujubes, half red and half green. He didn't hawk them, just squatted there. When he saw the camel train coming, he lifted one melon and waved it toward the road.

Shen You sat bolt upright.

"Stop a second."

Zhou Four looked at him.

"Just a second," Shen You said. "Last time by the Wei, I watched a whole afternoon's show and didn't get one bite of melon."

Zhou Four pulled the camel up. One of the Kang family's Hu boys ran over to haggle with the old man. He haggled a long while, the old man wouldn't come down by a single coin, and the Hu boy swore once in the Hu tongue and meekly paid up.

When the melon was handed up, Zhou Six took it first, cut it open with a small knife, looked at the flesh, sniffed it, cut off one wedge, and passed it to Shen You.

Shen You lifted the gauze of his hat a crack and took a bite. The melon was the sandy, crumbly kind—sweet and full of juice. It ran down his chin and into his collar, and he couldn't be bothered to wipe it, just went bite after bite until he'd gnawed it down to a thin green rind.

"This time there's melon," he said, and contentedly asked for another wedge.

The old man squatted under the tree, watching the Hu merchant in the curtained hat on the camel get melon juice all over his chin, and shook his head—presumably thinking that a Hu couldn't even eat a melon with any dignity. He tucked the money into the front of his robe, tucked it in deep, and glanced off toward the north.

"Heading north, Elder?" Zheng One asked in passing.

"Home's north of Jingyang," the old man said. "Sell this basket of melons, trade for a few sheng of seed, go back and see."

He didn't say go back and see what.

The middle of the Hour of the Horse. They reached the south end of the Bian Bridge.

A mess of militiamen stood at the bridgehead, spears leaning against the railing. A mess leader checked each travel pass, then waved them through. The camel train lined up at the south end and inched onto the bridge one camel at a time.

When the mess leader got to Shen You's camel, he looked up at the curtained hat, then down at the words on the pass: *beardless, pale of face*.

"Why's this one got his face covered?"

Kang Satuo, beside him, heaved a sigh and lowered his voice, like a man keeping a family embarrassment within the family. "My nephew. Broke out in sores on his face. Can't show it to anyone, and can't take the wind either."

The mess leader stepped back half a pace, waved a hand, and let them through.

The camel moved forward two steps. From under the gauze curtain came a remark, not loud: "This morning I was womenfolk. Now I've got sores. By tonight, will there be anything left of me?"

Kang Satuo, on his horse, pretended not to hear. The glass beads were clenched in his fist, clenched so hard they clicked.

Shen You's camel stopped at the head of the bridge.

The Wei ran yellow and murky, shouldering its way east. It was over a hundred paces across. The Bian Bridge was wooden, its planks worn into two shiny grooves by cartwheels. Three days ago, Shen You had sat on the slope on the south bank, shelling chestnuts and watching more than a hundred thousand men on the far side. Yesterday, Zheng One had taken a hundred carts of goods across this very bridge.

"Zhou Four."

"Here."

"The rule I set—you remember it."

"Crossing the Wei counts as leaving the capital."

"Good memory." Shen You took a Kaiyuan Tongbao coin from his sleeve, flipped it over twice on his fingers, and flicked it with his thumb. The coin spun, flashing once in the sunlight, and dropped into the Wei without so much as a splash.

"The fine," he said. "I made the rule. I pay."

Zhou Four looked at the spot where the coin had vanished, nodded once, and led the camel onto the bridge.

The planks creaked underfoot. In the middle of the bridge, Zheng One said in a low voice, "Right here."

There was a large black patch on the planks, shaped like a splash of thrown water, the edges soaked into the cracks between the boards. It had been more than a day. No one had scrubbed it, and scrubbing wouldn't have gotten it out. When the camel reached the black patch, it lowered its nose and sniffed, faltered half a step, then walked on across.

Shen You didn't lower his head, but the gauze curtain of his hat stayed turned toward the boards.

He remembered what Zheng One had said when he came back: how the horse had reached the middle of the bridge and planted all four hooves on the planks, and the man leading it had hauled twice and couldn't budge it. He remembered Cao One, face pressed against the ox's neck, saying, "It knows." The horse knew. What the horse didn't know was whether the treaty its fall had bought—one sheet of paper—would reach even as far as the north end of the bridge.

On the river flat along the north bank, there was nothing left.

The great camp had only broken at first light today, and in half a day the flat had emptied out, leaving behind a stretch of ruined ground. The turf had been churned upside down by hooves. Ring after ring of blackened fire pits—more than anyone could count at a glance—some still holding half-burned mutton bones. Scraps of felt, broken arrow shafts, a split cart shaft, a leather boot with the sole rotted out. Flies rose in clouds and settled in clouds. The wind came from the north, and what it carried wasn't the smell of grass but a sour, rank stench of mutton fat gone off.

The camel knelt on a patch of higher ground. This time the forelegs went down first, Shen You pitched forward, and once again he was hugging the hump. He slid down off the camel's back, and when his legs touched the ground they were so weak he nearly couldn't stand. He held on to Zhou Four's arm for a good long while.

"The ground is flat," he said, his voice full of gratitude.

The chestnut was brought over. The moment he sat in the saddle Fan Two had altered, he felt the difference: his backside no longer went hunting to the left, and both feet sat in the stirrups at the same height, equally firm. He wriggled in the saddle. "Fan Two, was it? You're getting a raise when I'm home."

Across the river, the land was flat all around, with nothing to block the view or give cover. Zhou Four spread the people out: Chen Five twenty paces in front, Chu Four twenty paces behind, Zheng One on the left, Zhou Six on the right, and Shen You's horse tucked in between two camels. To anyone watching, it was just a crew of camel guards each going his own way, nobody close to anybody else.

At the tail of the train, the gray mare Cao One was leading suddenly stopped walking.

She stood on a patch of trampled grass, head low, nose to the ground, sniffing again and again. The ground was covered with horse dung, pile after pile, dried through, crumbling at a kick. She sniffed a long time. Then she raised her head toward the north, nostrils flared, and blew out one long breath.

She made no sound. As if she'd been about to ask something, and swallowed it.

Cao One put out a hand and cupped her nose, laid his face against her, and hummed a few bars of that wordless tune by her ear.

"She can smell them," he said. "They grazed here."

Early in the Hour of the Monkey. A fork in the road, ten-odd li west of Xianyang.

The main road ran west, to Wugong, to Qizhou, to Liangzhou. Branching off it, a narrow road went north, to Liquan. At the fork stood an old pagoda tree, and under it rested thirty-odd people and twenty-some horses—Chu One's group, which had gone out through Jinguang Gate with the He family's camel train and arrived half a double-hour early. A quarter of an hour later, Feng Five's group arrived with the Shi family's camel train.

The three groups didn't greet each other. They each pulled out of their camel trains, went to stand under the pagoda tree, and were simply one group. From splitting up at Kaiming Ward to meeting at the Hour of the Monkey—more than four double-hours, two city gates, and not one person lost.

Shen You unstopped the waterskin Su Three had sewn and took a big swig. The skin was double-layered—sheep's stomach on the inside, oxhide on the outside—and though the water had been sloshing around in it half the day, it was still cool in the mouth.

The Hu merchants of the He and Shi families watched from a long way off and took them for another horse train the Kang family had sent along. Nothing unusual about it. They drove their camels on west.

What Zhou One had said at home was a day's travel west out of the city, then turn north. At the fork, Chu One changed it: every extra day west was an extra day to make up coming back north. Ashina said, too, that the little road to Liquan ran straight and linked up farther north with the route the Turks were taking home. They were fifty-odd li from Chang'an here. The eyes at the city gates couldn't reach this far.

Chu One came over, stopped in front of Shen You's horse, and gave his report in three sentences.

"The gate clerk at Jinguang Gate counted twice. Not one off."

"Feng Five's group—Kaiyuan Gate didn't count."

"A hundred and twenty. All here."

The clerk at Jinguang Gate had gotten particular and counted from front to back, twice. However many the travel pass listed, that was how many were in the train—not one over, not one under. When he'd finished, he studied the pass a long while more, couldn't find a thing wrong with it, and waved them through.

Shen You listened from the saddle, remembered Zhou One's "What else am I for?", and let out a laugh under his hat.

Kang Satuo dismounted at the fork.

First he hung his reins over a fork of the pagoda tree and sent the Hu boy attending him to go keep an eye on the camel train up ahead. Only once the boy had run off did he walk up to the chestnut and stand by its shoulder, his back to the camel train, his voice pitched very low.

"Camel trains are slow," he said. "The day the good horses are in hand, send someone for me. I'll ride out to see them. Three hundred li, I'll still come."

"A first look," Shen You said.

"A first look." Kang Satuo laughed. "Twenty years in the West Market, I have one saying. Those people up north are good at taking things. And good at losing them."

He untied a bundle from behind his own saddle and handed it up.

"Trousers Sogdians wear for riding. There's a layer of soft sheepskin sewn into the seat, and down the insides of the legs as well." He cast a glance at Shen You's legs, his green eyes brimming with laughter. "I saw how Young Master Shen got down off that camel. I was right behind."

Shen You took the bundle, weighed it in his hand, and gave him a solemn salute with his hands cupped.

"This is worth more than the horses."

Kang Satuo mounted and rode off, glass beads clinking all the way, heading west.

He hadn't gone far when, under the pagoda tree, Zhou Seven tipped his head back and laughed three times—"*Ha! Ha! Ha!*"—exactly the way Kang Satuo laughed, right down to the snort through the nose as he stroked his beard at the end. A few of the camel guards couldn't hold it in and turned their faces away. Far off, Kang Satuo looked back once from the saddle, glanced all around, couldn't tell who it had been, and turned forward again.

Zhou Seven grinned.

A hundred and twenty people stood under the old pagoda tree, all looking at Shen You.

Shen You said nothing. He raised his right hand and pointed one finger north.

Feng Seven gave the hemp rope a tug. A hundred and twenty people turned as one.

***

The road north was a dirt road.

On both sides were millet fields. The millet should have been cut long ago, and hadn't been. The heads drooped, and when the wind blew, the grain dropped out kernel by kernel, with no one to gather it. Some plots had been trampled by horses, trampled to mud, and the mud had dried into hoofprints, one pocked hollow after another.

Fan Two's saddle was comfortable to sit in, but the two legs underneath belonged to Shen You. Before they'd gone two or three li, the tender flesh on the insides of his thighs was on fire again.

"It's a good saddle," he told Zhou Six. "Pity about the legs under it."

"Three days of chafing and they'll be fine," Zhou Six said.

"And after three days?"

"They'll have calluses."

Shen You looked down at his thighs and didn't say anything more.

At first Shen You still had it in him to chat. He asked Cao One whether the gray mare's hoof still hurt, asked Yun Five whether there'd be a moon tonight, asked Zheng One how to say "hello" in Turkic. Zheng One told him, and he repeated it once. "Master," Zheng One said, "the way you just said it, you're cursing the man's horse." Everyone in front and behind laughed.

Seven or eight li on, nobody was talking.

In the ditch by the road lay an ox, its belly bloated round as a drum, all four legs pointing at the sky, flies droning around it. Farther along, a big cart lay overturned at the edge of a field, one wheel missing, everything it had carried gone except a flattened wicker basket. One family's yard gate stood open, and an arrow was stuck in the door panel. The shaft was birch, the fletching trimmed short and even—not like a Tang arrow.

Zheng Six rode over, pulled the arrow out, and looked at the head: bone, ground very sharp. He snapped the arrow in two and threw it into the ditch.

There were fewer people on the road, too. Now and then there'd be one or two, carrying shoulder poles, also heading north. When they saw this column of horses and men, they ducked into the crops by the roadside first. Only after they'd made out that these weren't Turks did they come out again, heads down, walking quickly, and were soon gone.

A squad of militia came toward them, forty-odd men, spears on their shoulders, heading south. Every one of them was gray with dust, the soles of their shoes worn through. The squad leader in front was in his forties, half of his left ear missing. When he saw this column heading north, he stopped.

"North?"

Zheng One dismounted, gave a folded-hands salute, and said slowly, "The Kang family's horse train. Going to Binzhou to buy hides."

"Buying hides?" The squad leader looked him up and down. "The Turks just came through. Any hides left are on their backs."

"Still have to go and see."

The squad leader spat. "Go see, then. All they've left up north is their shit."

A young militiaman behind him muttered, "The emperor says no pursuit, so no pursuit. All that fighting for nothing."

The squad leader turned and planted a kick on his backside. "Shut it!" Having kicked him, he stood there himself and didn't move for a long moment.

He watched these "camel guards" go by. A hundred and twenty people, leading horses, driving camels, not one of them talking—and yet all on one step, left feet down together, right feet down together, like a single man walking. He had led soldiers for eight years. His own men couldn't march like that.

At the tail of the column, Feng Seven seemed only now to remember, and let the hemp rope in her hand go slack. With the beat gone, the steps fell apart at once—some fast, some slow, like any ordinary people on the road. They fell apart a little too well on cue.

The squad leader's eyes lingered on those backs. Hide buyers, carrying bows. The bows were all old. The strings were all new.

"Buying hides, and you need this many bows?"

"The roads aren't safe," Zheng One said. "Like you said yourself, squad leader—all that's left up north is their shit. And beside the shit, who knows, there might still be somebody squatting."

The squad leader stared at Zheng One a while. The corner of his mouth pulled once, but no smile came of it.

"My squad came out fifty strong," he said. "Going back, it's forty-three. The emperor made a treaty. I don't understand treaties. All I know is those seven aren't going home."

He took one more look at the curtained hat and asked nothing further—didn't even ask for the travel pass—and led his men off south. He was going home, not checking on people.

Under his hat, Shen You turned to watch the forty-three walk away. Soles worn through, limping, the tips of their shouldered spears bobbing—not one of them looked back north.

They had won at Jingyang and held the Wei, and now they were going home. As for what happened up north—the emperor said no pursuit, so they didn't pursue.
