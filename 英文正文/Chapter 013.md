# Chapter 13: The Price of Grain

The twenty-fourth day of the eighth month. The sixteenth day.

At the Hour of the Dragon, a crowd had packed in around the sweet-water well in Kaiming Ward.

The well sat on the north side of the ward's east-west dirt street, at the foot of somebody's wall. No windlass stood over it; every household had to bring its own rope, and decades of ropes had worn seven or eight deep grooves into the bluestone rim. At this hour on an ordinary day, the people who came for water were the women of the ward, carrying buckets, trading a word or two of gossip, then going on their way. Today the men had come too. Their buckets were long since full, but they squatted around the well platform and didn't leave.

Warden Zhao stood up on the platform, a head taller than everyone else.

This was the fourth time he'd told it this morning.

"...Chief Qian sat right there in my front room," he said. "Thirty-some constables, packed into my little yard so tight there was even men squatting in the kitchen, and not one of 'em dared breathe loud. My old woman brought Chief Qian a bowl of tea. He never touched it. Just kept his eyes on the crack in the door."

"And then?"

"And then that pack of soldiers got to the ward gate and called for it to be opened." Warden Zhao swallowed. "Chief Qian gave me a nod, so I went and opened it. Armor plates going *clatter-clatter*, and the one in front up on his horse with a face like cast iron. Soon as I had the gate open, my legs gave out. Chief Qian's men had to drag me back."

Someone in the crowd laughed out loud. Warden Zhao glared at him, and the man hastily swallowed the laugh.

"After that, over in the northeast corner, all of a sudden it lit up."

"Lit up?"

"The top of the wall went white all at once, like it'd caught fire." Warden Zhao stretched out his arm and pointed toward the northeast corner. "I took it for somebody's house going up. Then came the shouting, and then the running. That pack of soldiers came running for the ward gate, and when they got there, Chief Qian stepped out with his men, raised the torches—"

He paused, and lowered his voice.

"You didn't hear that step."

"What step?"

"Seventy, eighty men," Warden Zhao said, "all took one step forward together. *Boom!* The ground shook. I was peeking through the crack in the gate, and those soldiers—the blades in their hands, they went dropping one after another."

For a while it was quiet around the well, except for somebody's wooden bucket knocking against the shaft with a hollow *thunk*.

Sister Liu hauled up her own rope hand over hand, set her bucket down on the platform, and shook the water off her hands. "Seventy or eighty? The day they moved in, there was only ten of them, all told. I counted them myself."

"Ten, a dozen days back." Old Man Wang, the sesame-flatbread seller, was squatting off to one side, counting on his greasy fingers. "Day before yesterday I went to deliver flatbread, and there was twenty-some just standing at the gate."

"Those are soldiers." A man in a short tunic of coarse cloth spoke up in a low voice. "My uncle on my mother's side is a mess leader in the Left Encampment Guard. His unit was the one came at the fourth watch to take the prisoners away. On his way out, at the ward gate, he let something slip to me."

"Let what slip?"

"One of the Senior Colonel's guardsmen told him: the Senior Colonel said it with his own mouth, right there in that courtyard—put the Shen household's people on a battle line, and every one of them could be a squad leader."

This time, even Warden Zhao had nothing to say.

Sister Liu picked her bucket up, then set it down again. She glanced toward the house in the northeast corner. The lanterns at its gate hadn't been taken down yet. The gate stood open, and two people with bamboo brooms were sweeping in front of it—slowly, carefully, as if they were sweeping their own front room.

"He came out of a temple," she said. "Monks have Dharma guardians around them. What would you lot know?"

A few of the women laughed. Sister Liu laughed too, and when she was done laughing, she sighed.

"Even Dharma guardians have to eat," said Old Man Wang.

He squatted there and broke a cold sesame flatbread in two. He didn't eat it. He put the halves back together.

"Grain's gone up again. Yesterday in the West Market, millet was ninety coins a dou, and today they say it'll top a hundred. Before martial law it was thirty. Wheat flour's gone up even worse." He shook his head. "My flatbreads, I lose on every one I sell. Tomorrow I'm not baking."

Sister Liu didn't answer. Her husband drove a cart. The moment the news about the Turks turned bad, the work outside the city had dried up; since martial law, with the gates checking everyone, the cart couldn't get out at all, and it had been days since a single coin came in. Five mouths at home, and the millet in the crock would last three more days.

"The Shen house has a hundred-some mouths," Old Man Wang said slowly, looking toward the northeast corner. "What they eat in a day would feed my family a month."

He paused.

"I heard from the oil seller in the West Market—these last couple days, the grain shops in both markets won't sell to them."

***

Shen You slept until the Hour of the Horse.

When he woke, the room was very quiet. The *qingrong* gauze curtains sifted the daylight into a thin layer of white that settled on the silk-floss mattress, warm and drowsy. He lay sunk in the bedding for a while, listening: in the courtyard, a bamboo broom swished across the gray bricks; farther off, someone was talking in a hushed voice; farther still, A'na was plucking her pipa strings, tuning them one at a time.

Last night's courtyard full of flashing blades felt like something from a previous life.

A'xing came in carrying a copper basin of warm water, two sprigs of Zheng Seven's fragrant herbs floating on the surface. A hog-bristle toothbrush, green salt, and a fine cotton cloth were laid out neatly on a tray.

She had changed into new clothes today: an apricot-red jacket and skirt, a goose-yellow sash at her waist, and two new red yarn cords tied around her twin topknots. Three days ago she'd still been slopping water when she carried the basin; today she carried it steady as could be—through the door, basin down, one step back, all in one smooth motion.

"Master," she said, "Sanniang wants to know if it's all right to make breakfast and lunch one meal. Mutton soup noodles."

"Fine."

"Sanniang also says"—A'xing blinked—"the white rice is down to the bottom, and the mutton's the last sheep, so the Master should eat two extra bowls today, because tomorrow there won't be any."

Shen You mumbled around his toothbrush. "Who told her to say that?"

"Nobody. Her own idea." A'xing pressed her lips together on a smile, and her two dimples sank in. "She says the Master isn't worried, but Zheng Three is, so she's passing it along for Zheng Three."

Shen You spat out his rinse water and laughed.

***

After a soak in the hot pool, he lay down in the rocking chair under the pomegranate tree.

His hair was still short, too short to put up in a knot. Qingluo knelt behind the rocking chair with a fine cotton cloth, blotting the water from the roots of his hair bit by bit; then she took a rhinoceros-horn comb and drew it slowly from front to back. Her hands were very steady. The teeth of the comb ran along his scalp, cool and light, one stroke, then another.

Zhou One stood at the moon gate holding the roster and didn't come any closer.

Behind him stood Zheng Three, Zheng Five, and Feng Five. None of the three moved either.

Qingluo finished the last stroke, put the comb away, and stepped aside.

Only then did Shen You lift a finger.

Zhou One came over, stopped three paces away, and gave his goatee a single stroke first.

"About last night," he said. "Senior Colonel Yuwen escorted the prisoners into the Imperial City at daybreak. Cui Shouli and his men—the forty still living—are all to go into the prison of the Court of Judicial Review, so the Senior Colonel said when he left. Commandant Lu took statements until dawn. One copy goes to the county, one to the Guard."

"Mm."

"The sixteen who came at the Hour of the Rat—I've questioned them all. They run from Kong Two to Yan Three." Zhou One opened the roster. "One in mathematics, one in blacksmithing, one in pickling, one in cartwrighting... And one, Cao One, whose skill is listed as 'Horse Whispering.'"

"Horse whispering?"

"He's been squatting in the stable all morning and hasn't come out," Zhou One said. "Jiang Three, who keeps the horses, says that dapple-gray that's kicked him three times now follows Cao One around. Cao One goes left, it goes left. Cao One stops, it stops."

Shen You's eyebrows moved. He didn't say anything, but the corners of his mouth turned up a little.

Zhou One closed the roster and stepped half a pace aside.

Zheng Three came forward.

She was used to squatting, and standing up to give a report always made her a little awkward. She held her hands crossed in front of her stomach, the money pouch pressed beneath them. Her round face showed nothing much, as usual, but Shen You saw her thumb winding round and round the pouch's drawstring.

"Master. Grain."

"Go on."

"Since the day before yesterday, every grain shop in the East Market and the West Market has stopped selling to us," Zheng Three said. "Sanniang's people went, Feng Two's people went, people went in different clothes—same every time. The man in the shop opens his mouth and it's 'Sold out.' Then he turns around and sells millet at a hundred coins a dou to whoever's in line behind."

"The day before yesterday?"

"Yes." Zheng Three lowered her head for a moment. "Those two days, the Master had the Cui Shouli business to deal with, and we still had grain for five days. I thought reporting it two days late wouldn't do any harm."

Shen You looked at her. He said nothing, only nodded once.

Zheng Three let out a breath and went on with her report.

"A hundred and thirty-six mouths, two sheng a person a day—that's close to three shi a day. Feng Two's winery needs two shi of rice a day. Chen Two's pastry shop, one shi of flour a day. We have sixteen shi left at home." She paused. "It won't last three days. And tomorrow there'll be seventeen more mouths."

"And money?"

"There's money," Zheng Three said. "Cash, silk, and gold cakes, all told, come to over eleven hundred strings."

Her thumb stopped on the drawstring.

"We have money, and nothing to buy."

For a moment it was quiet under the pomegranate tree. Behind the bamboo blind, A'na's pipa was picking out a slow tune one note at a time, like water dripping onto stone.

Feng Five stepped forward. He hadn't slept all night; there was a gray film over his face, but his eyes were bright.

"The errand boys at the grain shops have loose mouths. They say the word came from the row head," he said. "The head of the West Market's rice row is the manager of the Pei family's grain shop. Day before yesterday, first thing, the moment the ward gates opened, Chen Six followed him—followed him all the way to the corner gate of a great house in the northeast of the city."

"Whose house?"

"The Duke of Xue's."

Zhou One added a line from the side, not raising his voice. "Zhangsun Shunde, Duke of Xue. The empress's clan uncle. Grand General of the Left Valiant Guard. One of the original followers from the rising at Taiyuan. On the day at Xuanwu Gate, he was there too."

Feng Five went on. "Outside the corner gate, Chen Six saw someone else, too. Red mole on his left earlobe. The gatekeeper called him Steward Wu. A while back, the man who came to the pastry shop in the East Market and ordered a special batch to be sent to the household—that was him."

No one said anything more. They waited.

Shen You lay in the rocking chair, looking up at the pomegranates over his head. They had ripened through to deep red, and one had split open, showing row upon row of seeds inside, like a mouthful of broken red teeth.

"Zheng Five."

Dark, wiry Zheng Five stepped forward.

"The grain in the city," Shen You asked. "That millet in the grain shops—where does it all come from?"

Zheng Five didn't even have to think.

"Three routes. Tribute grain from north of the Wei and from Tongzhou comes by the Wei River. With the Turks at Gaoling now, that route's cut. Grain from the counties outside the city—with the checks at the gates, what's getting in is under a tenth of the usual." He paused. "The third route is the south of the city."

"The south of the city?"

"South of the sixth cross street below Vermilion Bird Gate, there are dozens of wards, and more than half of it is fields," Zheng Five said. "I've been walking around down there these past days, picking up the soil. The millet was only just harvested this month. A lot of families still have it heaped on their threshing floors, unsold."

He paused.

"But the grain dealers thought of this route too. I heard it out on the field ridges yesterday. Day before yesterday, the grain dealers' people went down south of the city. The Daxingshan Temple's estate fields and a few officials' estates—several hundred shi apiece—they put down deposits on all of it, bought it outright. Then yesterday they had the ward wardens pass a word along to the small households: these next few days, if the Shen house of Kaiming Ward comes buying millet, whoever sells to them, the grain dealers won't take that family's grain ever again."

"And what's left?"

"What's left is all odds and ends. Three or five shi a family, seven or eight. A dozen-odd wards south of the city, a few hundred households," Zheng Five said. "The grain dealers have only ever bought from the big holders. The odds and ends, they can't be bothered to chase house by house. They wait for those folks to carry it to the market themselves, then beat the price down. Twenty coins a dou—and with their own dou measures, at that."

"Their own dou measures?"

"The bottom's built up a layer, and the mouth is half a finger wider than the official dou," Zheng Five said. "What goes in as one dou is one dou and two sheng."

Shen You laughed.

"The big ones, they've bought up," he said. "The small ones, they can't run around fast enough to get."

He sat up in the rocking chair.

"We can."

"Zheng Three."

"Here."

"Pick sixty of the people who can be spared, three to a team, and send them south of the city with Zheng Five to buy house by house. Fifty coins a dou, cash, with official measures. The measures-and-scales row in the West Market has ready-made ones with the brand already burned in—buy twenty," Shen You said. "If we don't have enough carts, hire every carter in the ward, every last one."

Zheng Three's hand had already gone into her money pouch and found the counting rods.

"How much should we buy?"

"As much as there is."

Zheng Three was taken aback.

"And the households in Kaiming Ward," Shen You went on, "we sell to them at fifty coins a dou. One dou per household per day, right there at the gate of the east compound."

This time Zheng Three's brows drew together. "Buy at fifty, sell at fifty? With hauling, labor, and spoilage, we lose two or three coins on every dou."

"Last night there were forty men walking around the ward with blades," Shen You said. "Nobody in the ward slept a wink. We owe them something."

Zheng Three opened her mouth and nothing came out.

She lowered her head and moved the counting rods one by one across her palm. As she moved them, her hands slowed.

Twenty coins. Fifty coins. A hundred coins.

If the farm families south of the city learned that someone in Kaiming Ward was paying fifty coins, with an official measure, in cash—who would that line of the grain dealers' about "never again" still scare? And from then on, who would sell to the grain dealers for twenty?

The grain dealers wouldn't sell millet to the Shen house.

So the millet south of the city wouldn't be sold to the grain dealers either.

Zheng Three's counting rods stopped in midair. On that round face, for the first time, there appeared something like a smile she was holding back.

"I'll go right now," she said.

"One more thing." Shen You lay back down. "Zheng Five, how long does millet keep?"

"The *Statutes on Granaries and Storehouses* say millet keeps nine years in storage, husked grain five," Zheng Five said. "Dig a pit, dry the walls by burning them with fire, spread a layer of plant ash, then mats, then chaff, and it'll keep even longer."

"Those two mu of land in the east compound—dig pits there," Shen You said. "Have Zheng Two and Zhou Five do it."

He couldn't remember exactly how the books had put it. All he vaguely remembered was one line: *In the early Zhenguan years, there was a great famine in Guanzhong, and a dou of grain cost a bolt of silk.*

Which year, he couldn't recall.

But there were more people every day. When it came to grain, more was always better.

He raised a hand and tapped the arm of the rocking chair lightly, once.

Settled.

The others withdrew. Behind the bamboo blind, A'na's pipa paused, then moved into a slightly quicker tune.

***

Elder Meng was winnowing on his threshing floor.

The threshing floor was a stretch of rolled-flat ground in front of his own door, three zhang on a side. A dozen-odd years under the stone roller had packed the earth as hard as flagstone. Gripping his wooden shovel in both hands, he scooped up a shovelful of millet, faced into the northwest wind, and tossed it high into the air.

The millet grains were heavy and fell back, pattering into a heap at his feet, a spread of gold. The chaff was light; the wind caught it and carried it off far to the southeast, glinting in fine, broken flecks under the sun.

His old wife squatted at the edge of the heap with a winnowing basket, picking out the clods of dirt that had fallen into the millet one at a time. Fourteen-year-old Erlang was driving the ox at the other end, dragging the stone roller round and round over the millet stalks.

This season's millet had come to twenty-six shi—several shi more than he'd reckoned before the sickles went in. The sparrows hadn't spoiled much of it after all.

Elder Meng kept a reckoning in his head. Rent at year's end, two shi. Seed, one shi. Food for the three of them until next year's wheat harvest, twelve shi. When Meng the Eldest left, the family had set aside a shi of dry rations for him, loaded onto the pack horse his mess shared. What remained came to ten shi, give or take.

A few days ago, the grain dealers' big cart had come along the field ridge with their own dou measure on board, and the errand boy had stood on the cart shaft, shouting: New millet, twenty coins a dou.

Elder Meng had gone over, measured the dou against his hand, said nothing, and come back.

Yesterday the ward warden had come to the door. He said he was passing along a word for the grain dealers: these next few days, if the Shen house of Kaiming Ward came buying millet, whoever sold to them, the grain dealers would never take that family's grain again.

Elder Meng had listened and said nothing.

The grain dealers beat the price down every year, but sending the ward warden to a man's door with threats—that was a first. He'd farmed his whole life. He knew what it meant: they were afraid. Afraid of the Shen house of Kaiming Ward. But what the Shen house wanted with so much millet, he couldn't work out.

He set down the wooden shovel, straightened his back, and looked north.

To the north, beyond the rooftops and pagoda-tree crowns of seven or eight wards, stood the gate towers of the Imperial City. Beyond that, he couldn't see.

Meng the Eldest was up north.

The militia were spared the grain tax and the cloth levy, but bows and blades, clothing and rations—those they had to supply themselves. The day Meng the Eldest left, he carried a bow, thirty arrows, a *hengdao*, and the twenty flatbreads his mother had baked through the night. When he reached the ward gate, he'd turned back and grinned at his father.

Six days now, and not a word.

Elder Meng brought his gaze back and saw two ox carts coming along the far end of the field ridge.

In the first cart sat a dark, wiry man of middle years. Elder Meng recognized him. These past days, the man had been wandering the fields south of the city every day—a few steps, then crouching to grab a handful of soil and hold it under his nose. Elder Meng had taken him for some geomancer out of a temple, reading the land.

Beside him sat a round-faced young woman with a money pouch in her arms and a dou measure laid across her knees. The driver was a strapping, solidly built man.

The second cart was driven by a hired carter. It was stacked with strings of coins, string upon string, tied up with hemp rope, so heavy that the bed of the cart creaked.

The carts stopped at the edge of the threshing floor. The dark, wiry man jumped down. Before saying anything, he crouched, scooped up a handful of the millet at Elder Meng's feet, rubbed it between his palms, blew off the chaff, and bit down on a single grain.

"Dried through," he said. "Good millet."

Elder Meng narrowed his eyes. "And you are?"

"Kaiming Ward. The Shen house," the man said. "The name's Zheng. You went to the county office and spoke up for my master, Elder."

Something moved in Elder Meng's eyes.

The round-faced woman lifted the dou measure down from the cart and set it beside the heap of millet.

"Fifty coins a dou," she said. "Cash. Measured with this."

Elder Meng didn't look at the money first. He looked at the measure.

The dou was new, made of cypress wood, perfectly square, and burned into its rim was a brand, the marks still a fresh, scorched black. He leaned in close and recognized the shapes of the characters. Every eighth month, official and private measures and scales alike had to be taken to the Court of the Treasury to be checked once, and the ones that passed were branded as proof.

He reached out and measured from the mouth of the dou to the bottom, then felt the bottom. Flat. Nothing built up under it.

"Fifty coins," he said slowly. "The grain dealers give twenty."

"The grain dealers sell for a hundred in the West Market."

Elder Meng said nothing.

His old wife's winnowing basket had gone still. The hand gripping its rim had gone white at the knuckles.

"Let me ask one thing," Elder Meng said. "What does your master want with so much millet? Sitting on it till the price climbs, then hauling it off to the West Market to sell?"

It was a hard question, not at all the way he usually spoke.

The round-faced woman looked at him and didn't take offense.

"In Kaiming Ward, at fifty coins a dou, to the households in the ward," she said. "One dou per household per day. We start selling this afternoon."

"Buy at fifty, sell at fifty?"

"Yes."

"What for?"

The dark, wiry man beside her gave a small laugh. "The Master says we kept the ward up last night with our racket. We owe them something."

Elder Meng stood stock-still.

He thought of that day, the ninth of the eighth month, in the waste ground of Kaiming Ward: the young man with hair barely an inch long and a strange outfit on, standing on the field ridge, asking him what year and month it was.

Back then, the man hadn't had a single coin to his name.

A dozen-odd days.

Elder Meng lowered his head and looked at the heap of gold millet at his feet for a long time.

"Eight shi," he said. "The other two I'll keep, to see us through the New Year. And just in case."

The round-faced woman had already set the figure out on her counting rods. She untied four strings of coins from the cart, a thousand coins to a string, and set them down on the heap of millet. The four strings sank in, pressing four little hollows into the pile.

Elder Meng's old wife dropped her winnowing basket on the ground.

***

The news ran faster than the ox carts.

What Elder Meng didn't know was that in that same double-hour, along the field ridges of a dozen-odd wards south of the city, some twenty carts of the Shen house were making the rounds—three people to a team, one branded dou, one cartload of coins—asking house by house. The grain dealers' threats could scare people off a deal where a few coins were at stake. They couldn't scare anyone off fifty. Besides, the Meng family had sold first.

In less than half a double-hour, the farm families nearby couldn't wait for the Shen carts to reach their doors. They came on their own toward the Meng threshing floor, sacks on their shoulders, pushing wheelbarrows. A line formed along the edge of the threshing floor. Zheng Three sat in the ox cart and measured them one after another, counted out coins one after another. Once the millet was measured, each family hitched up its own cart and hauled it to Kaiming Ward, with an extra twenty coins per cart for the carting.

When the sun had tilted toward the west, another big cart came along the far end of the field ridge.

The grain dealers' errand boy jumped down, took one look at what was going on at the threshing floor, and his face darkened on the spot.

He shoved his way up to Elder Meng and kept his voice low. "Elder Meng, you sell to them, and the grain dealers won't take your family's grain ever again."

Elder Meng was bent over, hoisting a sack onto an ox cart. At those words he straightened up and wiped the sweat off his face with his sleeve.

"Ever again?" he said. "Ever again, when you lot come around, it'll still be twenty coins."

The man opened his mouth.

"Twenty-five." He gritted his teeth. "Starting today, the grain dealers pay twenty-five too."

Somebody in the line laughed out loud.

The man's face flushed bright red. He stood there a moment, then turned, climbed back onto the cart, cracked the whip hard—*crack, crack*—and left.

Before dark, Zheng Three had measured the last sack. The Meng threshing floor alone had taken in seventy-odd shi.

As they were leaving, Elder Meng walked Zheng Three out to the field ridge. He hesitated, then spoke up anyway.

"Mistress Zheng, I'd ask you to look into something for me," he said. "My eldest's garrison—I hear it's been sent off toward Jingyang. Your master knows a lot of people. If any news comes from the north, news of the militia..."

He didn't finish.

Zheng Three nodded. "I'll ask for you, Elder."

Before the ward gates were locked, all twenty teams were back in Kaiming Ward. When the numbers were reported: four hundred and thirty shi.

***

Steward Wu's carriage entered Kaiming Ward at the Hour of the Monkey.

When it came through the ward gate, the dirt street was still empty. At the gate of a compound to the east, two people were setting up a long table, for what purpose he couldn't tell. He glanced at it and paid it no mind.

He had served in the Duke of Xue's household for thirty years. Back in the Sui, his master had served in the Right Guard of Merit, and rather than go die in Liaodong, had fled all the way to Taiyuan—and it was Steward Wu who drove the cart. These days, of all the great houses in Chang'an, Steward Wu had been inside more than half.

When it came to sizing up a household, he had his own method.

Not the walls. Not the gate. First, the gatekeeper.

The Shen gatekeeper was a man of about forty in a clean robe of plain, undyed fine hemp. When Steward Wu's carriage stopped, he came down the steps and made a folded-hands bow. Not too high, not too low—exactly the courtesy due a guest of standing, and without the slightest hint of servility.

"May I ask your honorable surname, sir?"

"Wu."

The gatekeeper didn't ask where he'd come from, or what he'd come for. He only said, "Please, Mr. Wu, have a seat in the gatehouse. I'll send word in."

In the gatehouse there was a folding stool and a small table. He had barely sat down when a cup of tea was brought. He shifted the cup to his right hand, and out of habit turned the jade thumb ring on his left thumb half a circle. His master had given it to him. He didn't shoot, and he'd worn it for twenty years.

The page carrying the message went in from the back of the gatehouse, his steps quick and light, without a sound.

Holding his tea, Steward Wu felt something stir in his mind.

This was the way things were done at a chancellor's gate.

He waited as long as it takes to drink a cup of tea.

Not long, not short. Longer would have been a slight; shorter would have been fawning. A cup of tea was exactly right.

The page came back and said a word in the gatekeeper's ear, and the gatekeeper showed him in.

The gray bricks of the front courtyard had just been sluiced with water. The cracks between them were still wet, and the air held the scent of soap pods, with a faint sting of lime underneath. Two maids stood under the veranda wiping glass lamps one by one with soft cloths. Steward Wu cast an eye over them and counted. Twenty-four. Glass lamps from the Kang family in the West Market went for five strings apiece.

A commoner's front courtyard, hung with a hundred and twenty strings' worth of lamps.

A man stood at the moon gate. Not tall, very broad in the shoulder, his right forearm wrapped in white cloth. He didn't look at Steward Wu; his eyes were on the front courtyard. Yet as Steward Wu walked past him, he felt as though he'd been measured from head to foot.

So this was the man who, last night, at this very gate, had put an old Liaodong veteran's arm out of its socket with his eyes shut. The whole city was talking about it.

The back courtyard was larger than the front. An old pomegranate tree, a few clumps of newly transplanted bamboo, an open pavilion with its bamboo blinds half rolled up. Someone in the pavilion was playing the pipa—a Western Regions melody, played very slowly.

Under the pomegranate tree stood two chairs.

Not folding stools. Not rope chairs. Two tall wooden chairs with backs and arms, a Persian rug spread beneath them. In one of them sat a young man.

A moon-white round-collared robe, a *futou* freshly tied, its two tails not yet hanging still. A maid in a pale green skirt of figured silk was just withdrawing from behind him. She went to a small brazier, knelt, and picked up a tea grinder.

Steward Wu's gaze rested on the maid for the space of a breath.

He knew that face. Last year, delivering a card to the Prince of Qi's residence for his master, he'd seen her beneath the eaves outside the study. The girl from the Prince of Qi's study. Pei Shisan had bid as high as forty strings and still hadn't gotten her.

And here she still was. In a skirt of figured silk, brewing tea.

Steward Wu walked up to the tree, folded his hands, and bowed slightly.

The young man didn't rise.

He lifted a hand and pointed to the other chair. "Mr. Wu. Have a seat."

Steward Wu sat down in the tall chair. Hard wood under his backside, something at his back to lean against, both feet on the ground. He'd knelt to sit all his life; on the rare occasion he used a folding stool, it had neither back nor arms. Sitting in a thing like this was like riding a horse he didn't know. He held his back straight and set both hands on his knees.

The pipa played on.

The young man raised two fingers toward the bamboo blind.

The pipa didn't break off at once. It finished the phrase; the last note trembled in the air a moment before it stopped.

Steward Wu took note.

Qingluo brought the tea. The bowl was Yuezhou celadon, its glaze like a pool of spring water. Out of habit, Steward Wu ran a fingertip over the bottom of the bowl. Thin body, the foot neatly trimmed. A fine piece.

The tea was clear. No salt, no ginger, no jujube—nothing added at all.

He took a sip. Bitter. After he swallowed, a faint sweetness slowly rose at the root of his tongue.

"Young Master Shen." He set the bowl down and smiled. "Last night's business—the whole city's talking about it. When my master heard, he said 'Good!' three times over."

"You're too kind, Mr. Wu."

"My master has tasted your Snow Sugar and can't praise it enough," Steward Wu said. "Today he sent me with a cartload of rice—ten shi, just outside your gate. Grain is tight in the city, and my master worried that with so many people in your house, putting food on the table might be hard."

"Thank your master for me."

Steward Wu nodded and went on.

"My master has an estate at Weinan, and there's grain on the estate. From now on, he'll supply your house with two hundred shi a month at a fair price, so you needn't trouble yourself about food again."

He paused, watching Shen You's face.

There was nothing on that face. He was only listening, the tea bowl turning between his fingers—a quarter turn, then back again.

"There's just one matter," Steward Wu said, making his voice gentler still. "The road that carries Snow Sugar into the palace and into the great houses—right now, it's the Dou family's Eleventh who walks it. The Eleventh is a good boy, but he's a concubine's son living under someone else's roof. A business this big, resting on his shoulders alone—my master worries for him. From now on, let the duke's household carry it. As for the Dou side, my master will look after them himself. They won't come out the losers."

Up in the pomegranate tree, a sparrow flapped its wings and flew off.

"And another thing," Steward Wu said. "Having many people in your house is a blessing. Only, in Chang'an, a house with many people gets a second look—from the county, from the Jinwu Guard, from the Ministry of Revenue. A hundred-some people—which register they're on, which prefecture issued their travel passes... In Chang'an, there's no wall the wind can't get through."

He said it very slowly, very softly, every word sounding as though he only had the other man's interests at heart.

"With my master around, no one takes that second look."

The tea bowl in Shen You's hand stopped.

It stopped for only a breath.

Then it went on slowly turning again.

Steward Wu lowered his eyes and took a sip of tea.

"I'll accept the rice," Shen You said. "Zhou One."

Zhou One came over from under the veranda.

"At today's price for rice in the West Market, what do ten shi come to?"

"A hundred and fifty coins a dou. Ten shi, fifteen strings." Zhou One didn't even have to think.

"Make it up in silk and load it onto Mr. Wu's carriage."

The hand holding Steward Wu's tea bowl paused.

A hundred and fifty coins a dou was the price at the West Market grain shops today—the price the Pei family's rice shop had led the way in driving up. And whose word the Pei family's rice shop answered to these days, Steward Wu knew better than anyone.

The young man had paid that price, to the last coin.

"The Snow Sugar road," Shen You went on, "the Dou family walks it just fine. They can keep walking it."

"As for my people—"

He smiled and set the tea bowl on the arm of the chair.

"If the county wants to look, let the county come and look. My gate was open last night, and it's open today."

Qingluo stood up.

Steward Wu knew what that meant: he was being seen out.

He rose and made a folded-hands bow. Shen You didn't get up, only nodded, then picked up a white porcelain dish from the small table at his elbow. On it lay a few snow-white sugar cakes.

"That order you placed at the shop in the East Market last time—they've been holding it for you," he said. "Made fresh this morning. Your master likes them. Take a couple back."

Steward Wu reached for the dish, and the thumb of his left hand came to rest on the jade thumb ring.

The last time he'd gone to the pastry shop in the East Market, he'd been in plain clothes and had said only, "It's for a duke's household." He hadn't said which duke.

"When you get back," Shen You said, "give the Duke of Xue my regards."

Steward Wu looked up.

From the moment he'd come through the gate until now, he had not once said "the Duke of Xue."

He looked at the young man in the chair for the space of a breath. Then he slowly bent at the waist, and this bow went an inch deeper than the one he'd made coming in.

***

Outside the Shen gate, Steward Wu stopped.

Along the ward's east-west dirt street stood a line of ox carts. The carter at the head of it was cracking his whip and calling out to people, like a man who hadn't landed a job in days. The line ran from the ward gate all the way to the gate of the Shen family's east compound. The carts were piled with bulging hemp sacks, millet trickling from the mouths of the sacks and spilling all along the way in a streak of gold.

At the gate of the east compound stood a long table. A branded cypress dou sat on it, and behind it sat a man who looked like a bookkeeper. Next to him, a wooden board had been planted, written in big characters:

*Millet. Fifty coins a dou. One dou per household.*

A long line had formed in front of the table. Women with baskets on their arms, men with sacks on their shoulders. An old man in a greasy apron was pushing his own cloth bag under the dou. A loud-voiced woman, shoving her way forward, shouted back at the people behind her, "Line up! Line up, all of you! The Shen family's millet isn't going anywhere!"

Steward Wu saw a man in a short tunic standing at the mouth of a side lane, outside the line. Steward Wu knew him: an errand boy for the Pei family's grain shop. The man watched for a while, then turned and ran.

Steward Wu climbed into his carriage.

As the carriage went out through the ward gate, he looked back through the window.

The gate of that house still stood open. In the front courtyard, the glass lamps were lighting up one by one, and warm yellow light spilled out through the gateway, lighting a stretch of the dirt street in front of it.

The wheels ground along the yellow-earth road, *clunk, clunk*.

Steward Wu sat in the carriage, the jade thumb ring pinched between his fingers, turning it round and round.

When his master cut off the young man's grain, he'd never meant to starve him. A house worth over a thousand strings, in a city as big as Chang'an, could always pry grain out of some crack or other—just costlier and slower. Cutting off the grain was a knock on the door: to let him know who had the say over Chang'an's grain. Once the door opened, there'd be room to talk. The big estates south of the city, the grain dealers had put deposits on two days ago; as for the odds and ends left over—a few hundred households, three or five shi apiece—the grain dealers' men could run around for a month and never collect them all.

What no one had counted on was the people.

One afternoon. Twenty-some carts. A hundred-some households.

Once that Pei errand boy got back, the Pei rice shop wouldn't be buying a single grain of cheap millet south of the city again, most likely. His master wouldn't lose sleep over the Pei family. But whether this door had been knocked open, or shut from the inside—for the moment, Steward Wu couldn't say.

What would he tell his master when he got back?

Not hurried. Not greedy. Not afraid.

Of all the people he'd met, not many had all three.

He closed his eyes and went over the day again from the beginning. The gatekeeper. The tea. The glass lamps. The house guard who'd put a man's arm out of its socket. The maid who hadn't been sold off. The tune that stopped only once it was finished. Fifteen strings of silk. Two sugar cakes.

And one more thing.

At the word "register," that tea bowl had stopped for a breath.

Steward Wu opened his eyes.

***

Dou the Eleventh rode into Kaiming Ward just as Steward Wu's carriage was coming out.

They passed each other at the Shen front gate. The Eleventh reined in his horse and looked back for a good long while before he swung down and strode inside, so distracted he forgot to hand his riding crop to the gatekeeper.

"Was that Steward Wu?" he burst out the moment he reached the back courtyard. "The Duke of Xue's Steward Wu? What did he come for?"

"To deliver rice."

"Deliver rice?"

The Eleventh sat down on a folding stool, stood up again, sat down again. The falcon on his shoulder was flapping restlessly, and he couldn't even spare a thought for feeding it.

"Brother Shen, do you know who that is? Even my uncle has to call him 'Mr. Wu,' nice and courteous." He lowered his voice. "Last time, the elders at home said some duke was asking around about who owned your sugar works, and he wasn't someone we could afford to cross. I figured it was one of the old dukes. Turns out it's the Zhangsuns."

"What about the Zhangsuns?"

"The Dous are His Majesty's mother's clan. The Zhangsuns are his wife's clan," the Eleventh said. "His mother's clan is the past. His wife's clan is the future."

When he'd said it, he paused, as if he hadn't expected to put it so plainly himself.

Shen You lay in the rocking chair, looked at him, and smiled.

"He wanted your road," Shen You said. "The road that takes Snow Sugar into the palace and the great houses."

The Eleventh's face went white all at once.

"I didn't give it to him," Shen You said.

The Eleventh's mouth hung open. It was a good while before he closed it.

He sat there, fingers drumming on his knee. The falcon flapped its wings on his shoulder, and only then did he remember; he fished a piece of meat out of his sleeve and fed it.

"Brother Shen." He kept his head down as he fed the falcon. "My uncle called me in today. He says in a couple of days he'd like to have you over to the house for a meal."

"Last time I went, I came in through the side gate."

"This time it's the front gate," the Eleventh said. "My uncle said so himself."

"Let's wait till the Turks pull back."

The Eleventh looked up at him and suddenly laughed.

"You sound exactly like the palace," he said. "All the petitions my uncle's sent in these past days come back with the same line: *To be discussed once the Turks withdraw.*"

He finished laughing, but his eyes lit up. "I heard from Senior Colonel Yuwen's people—when last night's urgent dispatch went in, His Majesty read it and said something. Nobody caught exactly what."

He finished feeding the falcon and glanced toward the east compound. On the other side of the wall, someone was digging; iron shovels clinked against stone, *clink, clank*, and someone was calling out a work chant.

"When I came into the ward, I saw millet carts lined up from the ward gate all the way to your east compound," he said. "That's got to be four hundred shi at least?"

"Four hundred and thirty."

The Eleventh's fingers tapped on his knee a few times. He ran the books for that house by the West Market, and he was no slouch with figures.

"Two sheng a person a day, four hundred and thirty shi—that'll feed a hundred people for more than seven months." He looked up. "And you're still digging pits."

"Mm."

"How many people do you actually have?"

He'd been holding that question in for a dozen-odd days.

The first time they met, there had been only two people behind Shen You. Later it was ten, fifteen, dozens. Some at the sugar works, some at the winery, some at the shop in the East Market, some out on the field ridges south of the city, and some up on the rooftops at night. Every time he came, there were new faces—and he had never once seen them all.

"Today, a hundred and thirty-six."

"Today?"

"Tomorrow, a hundred and fifty-three."

The Eleventh opened his mouth, and in the end didn't ask any further.

A dozen-odd days of knowing Shen You, and he'd long since learned that some questions were pointless to ask. He simply filed the two numbers away in his head.

He flopped back on the folding stool, looked up at the pomegranate tree overhead, and let out a long breath.

"I'm not going home tonight," he said. "Got any of that Shaochun left?"

***

At the Hour of the Rat, seventeen more arrived.

A hundred and fifty-three.

Shen You mumbled "Summon" in a half-asleep daze, rolled over, and went back to sleep. Zhou One received the new people outside the sleeping hall, questioned them one by one about what they could do, entered them in the roster, and led them off to the east compound.

In the east compound, Zheng Two and Zhou Five had people out by torchlight, digging the pits.

***

The twenty-fifth day of the eighth month. The seventeenth day.

At first light, Feng Five came back from the ward gate.

"There's a county notice posted at the ward gate," he said. "Yuchi Jingde is going out to meet the Turks at Jingyang. The troops in the city went out the north gate at the fifth watch—the men at the patrol post said so. The supply train can't keep up, so the county is hiring carts, oxen, and laborers to haul grain to Jingyang. Three chi of silk per cart per day."

Shen You sat on the veranda with an outer robe thrown over his shoulders, holding a bowl of freshly brewed tea in both hands. The courtyard was hazy with mist, and dew hung on the leaves of the pomegranate tree.

Zheng Three stood to one side. "Elder Meng asked me to find out about the militia up north. His eldest's garrison, he hears, has been sent to Jingyang."

Shen You didn't answer right away.

He took a sip of tea. The steam rose into his face, and his eyes narrowed.

From over by the stable came a soft snort. Cao One led the dapple-gray out through the side gate and stood in the courtyard. The horse kept its head low, nudging Cao One's shoulder with its nose, again and again.

"There's going to be a battle at Jingyang," Shen You said.

He set down his tea bowl.

"And once the battle's over," he said, "the ground will be covered in horses with no one to claim them."

Around the courtyard, several heads came up.

"We've got the people," Shen You said. "Carts and oxen, go buy them today. The county wants grain hauled—we'll answer the call."

"Take it to Jingyang, have a look around." He glanced at Cao One. "And while you're at it, find the Meng family's eldest."

Zhou One gave his goatee a stroke. "How many horses does the Master want?"

Shen You picked up his tea bowl and took another sip.

"As many," he said, "as there are."
