# Chapter 11: The Investiture of the Empress

The twenty-first day of the eighth month. The thirteenth day.

It wasn't yet light when the drums started up in Chang'an.

Not the calls for the ward gates opening. This was drums and music carried over from the Palace City, wave after wave, rolling across the northern sky, low and heavy, like thunder somewhere far off. The dogs in the ward were startled into barking, and Sister Liu's chickens set to flapping along with them.

Today, the palace was investing an empress.

Kaiming Ward lay a long way from the Palace City, and there was nothing to see. But the moment the ward gate opened, the street held twice as many people as on an ordinary day. The sesame-flatbread sellers' shoulder poles were loaded to the ends. Old Warden Zhao had put on a freshly washed and starched robe and stood at the ward gate talking with people, his voice louder than usual: "The Zhangsun family's daughter! The Prince of Qin's consort! She's empress now!"

"They say the empress is ever so virtuous..."

"Well, naturally. She's from the Zhangsun family..."

"Did you know, the empress's brother—that Zhangsun Wuji—the day at Xuanwu Gate..."

The speaker dropped his voice. A few heads leaned in together, then all pulled back again.

Shen You sat under the eaves of his own gatehouse, listening to the noise from the street, a bowl of sweet rice wine in his hand, sipping at his leisure.

Thirteen had arrived at the Hour of the Rat. Ninety-one now.

Every place in the house where a person could sleep was already full. The run-down compound they'd just bought to the east had been cleared out overnight by Zheng Two and the people he took with him, and another twenty-odd had been squeezed in there. Zhou Five had spent the whole morning squatting in the courtyard drawing plans; he said he wanted to put up two new rows of rooms on the east side.

***

At the Hour of the Dragon, Shen You strolled over to the ward's west gate, just in time to catch the Dou family's carts coming up from the direction of Baoning Ward and heading north along Vermilion Bird Avenue outside the gate.

Two ox carts, stacked with gilt-painted lacquer boxes, covered with oilcloth and lashed down tight with hemp rope, four household slaves walking alongside each cart. Sitting on the lead cart was Fan Da. He spotted Shen You and cupped his hands to him from a distance.

The lacquer boxes held sugar.

On the strength of being the emperor's mother's clan, the Dou family had taken on the white sugar for the palace's investiture banquet. The order had only been settled the night before last. The fires in Chen One's sugar works hadn't gone out for a day and two nights, and they had boiled down a hundred and twenty jin, picked out the whitest and finest of it, packed it into forty gilt-painted lacquer boxes, and loaded two carts.

The carts went north, along Vermilion Bird Avenue, all the way toward the Palace City.

Shen You watched them go and yawned.

"Aren't you going to go see, Master?" A'xing was just back from outside with a vegetable basket on her arm, her eyes shining. "Both sides of Vermilion Bird Avenue are packed with people! They say the palace is handing out wine and meat, and every ward gets a share!"

"No," Shen You said. "Everybody crammed in shoulder to shoulder. What's there to see?"

He finished the last mouthful of sweet rice wine in his bowl.

"As long as my sugar gets there, that's good enough."

***

At the Hour of the Snake, Zhou One came to report.

"The sugar works. Chen One says that at this pace, it can turn out a hundred jin a day. The Dou and Kang orders are already booked into next month."

"The shop in the East Market had twice as many people lined up today as in the past few days. Chen Two says one customer wanted twenty boxes at once—said his family was holding a banquet to celebrate the palace's happy occasion."

"Feng Two's strong spirit—he's given it a name. Shaochun. Kang Satuo had one taste and said it's stronger than the grape wine of the Western Regions. He wants to haul fifty jars out west to start."

"Feng Four is telling stories in the West Market." Zhou One paused. "Yesterday he told a new one, called *The Haunted House of Kaiming Ward*. It's about a monk who's gone back to lay life, catching ghosts at night in a haunted house, and the ghosts he catches turn out to be three ruffians dressed up as ghosts. The crowd laughed till they were rocking in their seats."

Shen You grinned. "He's good at making things up."

"He didn't make it up." Zhou One's face was blank.

"And," Zhou One went on, his tone growing heavier, "from Feng Five. There are more and more people at the Cui house."

"How many?"

"Between last night and this morning, eleven went in through the Cui family's back gate, all in twos and threes. Some dressed as porters, some as distant relatives come to offer congratulations. Counting the ones from before, a little over thirty all told," Zhou One said. "And there are some who didn't go into the Cui house. They went into an abandoned temple in Daye Ward, in the south of the city. Feng Five counted a dozen or so."

"Forty or so."

"Forty or so."

Shen You nodded and said nothing.

Forty-odd veterans of the Eastern Palace Guard. Soldiers who had cut men down outside Xuanwu Gate and hidden from the blades in the Zhongnan Mountains.

His household had ninety-one people. The day after tomorrow, a hundred and twenty. But of the ninety-one, the ones who could hold a blade numbered no more than thirty. The rest could boil sugar, make pastries, weave brocade, paint pictures.

"How's Zhou Four doing?"

"Feng Seven has people out walking formations every day before dawn. Zhou Four has picked thirty and split them into five squads. Zheng Three bought twenty *hengdao* from the ironmongers' row in the West Market, and Feng One forged another ten. Feng Six and Zhou Five have made eight crossbows." As he said it, Zhou One glanced at Shen You. "Master, *hengdao* don't break the law. Keeping crossbows in private does."

"I know," Shen You said. "Just don't let anyone see them."

***

Early in the afternoon, someone came in through the back gate, in plain clothes, a bamboo hat pulled low on his head.

He took the hat off. It was Qian Biao.

Zhou Four's hand settled on the hilt of his blade.

Qian Biao glanced at him and paid him no mind. He walked straight to the veranda and cupped his hands to Shen You. His long face had grown a good deal thinner since the last time, the skin under his eyes had gone a bruised blue, and the scar dragging down the corner of his mouth looked deeper than ever.

"Greetings, young master."

"Chief Qian." Shen You didn't get up. He pointed to the folding stool beside him. "Sit."

Qian Biao didn't sit. He stood there and was silent for a while, as though weighing something.

"Colonel Cui came to see me," he said at last.

"What does he want you to do?"

"The twenty-third of the eighth month. At night," Qian Biao said. "He wants me to pull the constables out of Kaiming Ward and Baoning Ward, both of them. Not one left behind."

"And you agreed?"

"I agreed," Qian Biao said. "If I hadn't, I'd be dead tonight."

"Then what are you doing here?"

Qian Biao raised his eyes and looked at Shen You.

"You've got my confession, young master, and my stolen goods," he said. "Colonel Cui has got my life."

"I've been a chief constable in Chang'an for more than ten years," he said slowly. "I've seen a lot of men. Colonel Cui is a hard one. But what he means to do is take the Eastern Palace's former men and draw blades right here inside the capital. If he wins, I'm his accomplice. If he loses, I'm still his accomplice."

"As for your side, I've had three looks." He paused. "The first time, my men came back and said ten. The second time, I saw fifteen with my own eyes. This time, coming in, there were at least thirty walking formations on that open ground in the back courtyard."

"I don't care to be the man who goes down with the loser."

Shen You looked at him for a while. Then he smiled.

"You're a sensible man, Chief Qian."

"Sensible men live longer," Qian Biao said. "There's one more thing you need to know."

"Go on."

"On the night of the twenty-third, Colonel Cui is on duty." Qian Biao lowered his voice. "If the capital goes under martial law, all the Guards have to take turns sending men out to patrol the streets, and that night it's the Left Encampment Guard's turn. At night, the only men allowed on the streets will be the patrols."

"He won't come over the wall," Qian Biao said. "He'll bring soldiers, dressed in Encampment Guard armor, under the banner of the city patrol, and walk in through the gate of Kaiming Ward, open and aboveboard."

"To do what?"

"Search for Turkic spies."

The veranda went quiet for a moment.

Zhou One's expression changed.

A night under martial law. Soldiers on patrol. A search for Turkic spies. Breaking down doors, seizing people, ransacking the place—every bit of it legitimate. Anyone in the house who dared stand in the way would be resisting arrest. Would be colluding with the enemy.

And once the people were dead and the box was taken, come daylight the report sent up would be a single line: *Shen You of Kaiming Ward: concealed weapons in private, suspicious conduct; resisted arrest and was put to death.*

"Nicely played," Shen You said.

There wasn't a trace of alarm on his face as he said it. If anything, he sounded a little admiring.

"Chief Qian, on the night of the twenty-third, do just what he tells you," he said. "Don't leave a single constable behind."

Qian Biao stared at him.

"Young master..."

"He wants to walk in open and aboveboard," Shen You said with a grin. "So let him walk in."

***

After Qian Biao left, Zhou One stayed where he was on the veranda and didn't move.

"Master," he said at last, "if he comes in wearing army armor and we fight back, that's resisting arrest. If we don't fight back, we're waiting to die."

"I know."

"Then..."

Shen You didn't answer. He turned to him instead. "Under Tang law, someone who barges into a house at night without cause—how's that handled?"

"*Whoever enters another's home at night without cause: forty strokes of the light cane. If the master of the house kills him on the spot, there is no charge,*" Zhou One said. "But he won't be without cause. He'll be on patrol, searching for spies."

"Does he have a writ to search?"

Zhou One stopped short.

"Patrolling the city means patrolling the streets, not raiding people's homes," Shen You said. "To go into a private house and search it, you need a writ—from the county, from the prefecture, or from the Guard. Cui Shouli is one colonel. He's bringing forty former Eastern Palace men on his own say-so, passing them off as Encampment Guard soldiers, with no writ, to break into a private house in the middle of the night."

"So what does that make him?"

Zhou One slowly drew a breath. "...*Whoever enters another's home at night without cause.*"

"*If the master of the house kills him on the spot, there is no charge,*" Shen You said. "But I don't want to kill anyone. Kill them, and there's no one left to testify."

He lay back in the rocking chair and set it swaying.

"Go invite Dou the Eleventh over. And tell Feng Five to stick to the Cui house like a shadow. Which squad he calls up, whose armor he borrows, which streets he takes—I want to know all of it."

***

Dou the Eleventh came toward evening.

He was dressed especially well today: a newly tailored round-collared robe of deep crimson-purple, a jade pendant hanging from his *diexie* belt, and an expression of triumph he couldn't hide. He started shouting the moment he was through the door. "Brother Shen! The palace took it! A hundred and twenty jin, not one jin short! The men from the Palace Food Service opened two boxes right there on the spot, and their eyes about popped out of their heads!"

He dropped onto the folding stool, took the sweet rice wine A'xing handed him, and drank half the bowl in one swallow. Only then did something seem to occur to him, and he lowered his voice.

"There's one more thing."

"Go on."

"Last night, the elders of the family called me in." The Eleventh's smile went away. "They told me to stay out of your affairs from now on."

"Why?"

"A certain duke has been asking around about who owns your sugar works," the Eleventh said. "Which house, the elders said, I don't need to know—and if I did know, I couldn't afford to cross them anyway. The Dou family may be the emperor's mother's clan these days, but a mother's clan still eats by watching other people's faces. No point offending anyone for the sake of some monk gone back to lay life."

Shen You said nothing. He looked at him.

"And you?"

The Eleventh held the bowl of sweet rice wine and studied its bottom for a while.

"I told you before," he said. "I'm a concubine's son, eleventh in line. The family's connections never come down as far as me. I can't squeeze in through the new doors, so I have to find my own road."

He raised his head and looked at Shen You.

"You're the road I found."

"From now on, if the elders ask, I'll tell them the sugar business is mine alone and has nothing to do with the Dou family." He grinned, back to his usual easy slouch. "Nobody ever bothered keeping an eye on me anyway."

Shen You looked at him and suddenly felt there was something more to this young man than there had been the first time they met.

"On the night of the twenty-third," Shen You said, "could you invite a few people over to my place for a drink?"

"Who?"

"Commandant Lu of Wannian County." Shen You thought for a moment. "And one more—someone in the Left Encampment Guard who outranks Cui Shouli."

The Eleventh's eyes narrowed.

He didn't ask why. He only looked at Shen You for a long time.

"The Senior Colonel of the Left Encampment Guard is an old acquaintance of my uncle's," he said slowly. "Getting him to come, that I can do. But I'd need a pretext."

"I've got one," Shen You said. "Tell them I've brewed a new Shaochun and I'd like them to try it. And while they're here, they can watch a show."

***

That night, the Shen household held a family feast.

The city was celebrating the investiture of the empress, so the household celebrated too. The feast was laid out under the pomegranate tree in the back courtyard: several low tables pushed together into one long row, spread with the brocade cushions Zheng Four had just finished sewing, and twenty-odd glass lamps lit, their light a warm yellow, throwing the pomegranate tree's shadow across the gray-brick paving, where it swayed back and forth.

Zhou Three hadn't set foot outside the kitchen since early afternoon.

On the tables: a big basin of chicken stewed with *zhuyu* and Sichuan pepper, swimming in glossy red oil, its numbing fragrance hitting you straight in the nose; a whole fish braised with fermented black beans, ginger, Sichuan pepper, and the sugar Chen Two had just boiled down, sour and sweet and salty and hot all tangled together—Zhou Three said this was called "fish-fragrant, no chilies"; a platter of steamed lamb so tender it fell apart at the touch of a chopstick; and sesame flatbreads, soup noodles, steamed sugared milk curd, jujube-paste mooncakes, dish after dish crowding the tables.

Feng Two carried over two jars of Shaochun and slapped off the clay seals. A sharp, nose-stinging smell of liquor instantly drowned out every food smell in the courtyard.

Shen You poured himself a small cup and took a sip.

A line of fire burned from the tip of his tongue all the way down into his stomach.

He narrowed his eyes and smacked his lips.

Somewhere around fifty percent alcohol. Fourteen hundred years back, this was the kind of stuff that could put a man flat on the ground.

"Good liquor."

Feng Two broke into a grin and scratched his head bashfully.

A'na had her pipa in her arms and was playing a lively Hu tune. Chen Four sat beside her, humming softly along with the beat. Two cups in, Feng Seven insisted on dragging A'xing out onto the open ground to dance—Feng Seven's own routine, three steps left, three steps right—and before long Zhou Five, Zheng Two, and a few of the other old men had been hauled in as well, a whole crowd turning circles under the lamps, a total mess, everyone doubled over laughing.

Qingluo sat beside Shen You, quietly pouring for him. A cup of wine, and then a bowl of plain tea in its place. One cup of wine, one bowl of tea. Never one more of either, never one less.

Shen You looked at the courtyard full of people, and kept looking for a long time.

Twelve days ago, he'd been lying in a ruined temple with a headless Buddha, with no one beside him but Zhou One.

Now there were ninety-one people. He had a house, a shop, a sugar works, a winery. Someone to brew his tea, someone to play the pipa for him, someone willing to fall out with the elders of his own family for his sake.

The night after tomorrow, forty old Eastern Palace soldiers were coming for his life.

He raised his cup and drained it in one go.

"Not so much, Master," Qingluo said softly.

"Just this one."

***

At that same moment: the Eastern Palace, Xiande Hall.

The hall blazed with light, and the music of strings and pipes drifted out in waves.

The grand rite of investiture had been completed during the day; the evening was a family banquet. Only their own people had come: the empress's elder brother, old hands from the Prince of Qin's household, several dukes, and members of the imperial clan. The tables were crowded with delicacies prepared by the Palace Food Service, and the wine was grape wine sent as tribute from the Western Regions, poured into glass cups, red as gemstones.

Two people sat in the seats of honor.

The man was twenty-eight or twenty-nine, tall and straight-backed, with clean, open features, dressed in everyday robes of ocher yellow. He sat at his ease, but his eyes were very bright. Whatever anyone in the hall said, whoever glanced at whom, nothing seemed to get past them.

The woman beside him was twenty-five or twenty-six, with gentle features, dressed in brand-new ceremonial robes, the flowered hairpins in her hair glinting in the lamplight. She didn't say much. Only when someone raised a cup to her did she give a slight nod and a smile.

The Palace Food Service brought out a new sweet: a plain white porcelain dish holding a small heap of sugar.

Not stone honey sent as tribute from the Western Regions. Not sand sugar, yellow shading into brown. It was white—white as snow—every grain of it glittering under the lamps.

The empress glanced at it, a little surprised, scooped up a bit with a silver spoon, and put it in her mouth.

Her brows lifted, just slightly.

"Erlang, try this."

Li Shimin scooped up a little too.

He tasted it slowly. When he was done, he set down the silver spoon and looked at the dish of sugar.

"Whiter than stone honey," the empress said softly. "White as snow."

"Then call it Snow Sugar," Li Shimin said offhandedly, and asked the man from the Palace Food Service, "Where did it come from?"

"If it please His Majesty, the Dou family presented it. They say it's made at a sugar works in the south of the city. The owner is a monk who went back to lay life."

"A monk who went back to lay life?"

Li Shimin raised an eyebrow but asked nothing more, as if he found it a little amusing—or as if he'd only half been listening.

Zhangsun Wuji, seated in a lower place, rose to his feet just then, smiling, drew something out of his sleeve, and offered it up with both hands.

"Your Majesty, your subject also has a rare thing to present today, in congratulation to the empress."

It was a little thing, smaller than a palm, a glowing green—not jade, not stone, not glass—and slick and smooth to the touch.

"What is this?"

"A Hu merchant in the West Market presented it. He said it's a treasure from the kingdom of Fulin, and there are only three in all the world." Zhangsun Wuji smiled. "Your Majesty, press here."

Li Shimin pressed it.

*Click.*

A little flame leaped up.

For an instant, the hall went quiet.

Li Shimin looked at that tiny flame, and something flashed in his eyes. He pressed it a few more times, and it lit every single time. He turned the thing over and held it up to the lamp. Through the green shell, a little bit of something like clear water sloshed around inside.

"The Fulin people have fine craftsmen," he said.

The empress leaned in to look too. Watching the little flame, she smiled. "Snow Sugar, and Fulin Fire. Today brings a double blessing."

Everyone in the hall laughed along.

Only one man, seated toward the back among the lower places, didn't laugh.

He was about fifty, in a purple robe and a jade belt, with a perfectly proper smile on his face. But his eyes stayed fixed on the porcelain dish of Snow Sugar, thoughtful.

***

Halfway through the banquet, hurried footsteps sounded outside the hall.

A palace attendant came in at a rapid walk, knelt in the middle of the hall, and held a military dispatch high in both hands.

"Urgent dispatch for His Majesty—from Jingzhou!"

The music of strings and pipes stopped.

Li Shimin took the dispatch and unfolded it. He had read only a few lines when his face darkened.

When he had finished, he handed the dispatch to Zhangsun Wuji beside him and stood up.

"The two khans, Jieli and Tuli, have joined forces—more than a hundred thousand horsemen. They are raiding Jingzhou, and their vanguard is already past Wugong."

The hall fell dead silent.

Wugong was less than two hundred li from Chang'an.

"Issue the order." Li Shimin's voice was very even—so even it made a man's chest tighten. "The capital is under martial law. All troops of the Guards are to muster for inspection at once."

He looked back at the empress. She looked back at him and said nothing at all, only gave a small nod.

Li Shimin strode out through the doors of the hall.

On the table, most of the dish of Snow Sugar was still left, glittering quietly under the lamps. The little green thing, he had slipped into his sleeve along the way.

***

Kaiming Ward. The Shen house.

The family feast had broken up. The courtyard was all lingering liquor smell and guttering lamps.

Shen You didn't go back inside. He had Zhou Four set up a ladder, climbed up onto the ridge of the main hall's roof, and sat on the tiles, looking north.

Qingluo came up after him, carrying a pot of tea. She sat down beside him and draped a cloak over his shoulders.

The night was very late now. The ward gates had closed long ago, and Chang'an lay sunk in pitch darkness: more than a hundred square blocks, one against the next, with only a few scattered points of light.

To the north, over by the Palace City, everything blazed. Patch after patch of light joined into a single bright band lying across the edge of the sky, like the Milky Way fallen to earth.

"What are you looking at, Master?"

"Taking in the show."

Qingluo followed his gaze and said nothing.

Suddenly, hoofbeats sounded in the distance.

Fast and loud, on the empty avenue, pounding one after another into yellow earth rammed rock-hard, coming closer and closer.

On a curfew night, there was only one kind of horse that dared run like that.

Shen You stood up and looked toward Vermilion Bird Avenue.

He couldn't see anyone. Only a blur of torchlight skimming along the top of the ward wall to the west, dragging from north to south. The hoofbeats swept past outside Kaiming Ward's wall and raced on toward Mingde Gate.

Close behind came another rider. Then another. On other streets farther off, faint hoofbeats rose as well, some heading east, some heading west, racing for every gate in Chang'an.

To the north, inside the Palace City, a quick, urgent drumming broke out.

*Boom. Boom. Boom. Boom.*

One beat after another, rolling heavily out from the Palace City and bearing down on the head of all Chang'an.

Every dog in every ward started barking at once.

In Kaiming Ward, lamps came on in a few houses.

Qingluo was standing beside him now, her face a little pale. "Master, what is this..."

"Martial law," Shen You said.

He stood on the ridge of the roof, the night wind snapping at his robe.

"The Turks are coming."

He looked down at his own courtyard. In the back courtyard, thirty of his people were already up, and Zhou Four stood beneath the moon gate, looking up at him.

The day after tomorrow. The twenty-third day of the eighth month.

The ten-day deadline.

Shen You smiled and pulled tighter the cloak Qingluo had draped over his shoulders.

"Perfect timing."
