# Chapter 2: One Lighter

The moment the Hour of the Rat arrived, the line of gold text lit up in front of his eyes, right on schedule.

[Deathsworn Summoning]

[Day 2]

[Summons Available Today: 2]

Shen You opened his eyes and muttered, "More punctual than the punch clock at work."

Daily quota, use it or lose it. He had no intention of picking a fight with the system.

"Summon."

In the darkness, the air twisted twice.

He fished out the lighter and flicked it. *Click.* The flame jumped, and two figures took shape in its light, one on the left and one on the right, each down on one knee.

"Greetings, Master."

"GREETINGS, MASTER!"

The first voice was lazy. The second was so loud that dust came sifting down from the rafters.

The one on the left was a young man of twenty or so, tall and thin, his eyelids drooping like he hadn't quite woken up. But a small pebble was tumbling across his fingers, on his index finger one moment and his pinky the next, so fast it was nothing but a blur.

The one on the right was a woman of about thirty, broad in the shoulder and thick in the waist, her sleeves already rolled up to the elbow. Her hands were thick and steady, hands that had been born to hold a wok spatula.

[Deathsworn · Two]

[Skill: Gambling (Grandmaster)]

[Deathsworn · Three]

[Skill: Sichuan Cooking (Grandmaster)]

Gambling. The ledger in Shen You's head turned a page: the question of a stake now had an answer.

Sichuan cooking.

His eyes lit up. Seven years as a corporate drone, and his stomach had been raised, one bite at a time, by the *maocai* joint downstairs and the boiled-fish place across from the office.

"Sichuan cooking, now that's what I'm talking about," he said with a grin. "Spicy boiled fish, *maoxuewang*, chili—"

"Master." The woman cut him off. Her voice was as loud as ever, but her tone was flat. "There's no chilies in this day and age."

The words stuck in Shen You's throat.

...Right. Chili peppers wouldn't reach China until the Ming dynasty.

"You know that?"

"Knew it the moment I was called up." The woman pushed her sleeves up a little higher. "No chilies, no bean paste, no iron wok. Tang folk mostly boil, steam, or roast over the coals. Hardly any of 'em even know how to stir-fry."

She ticked the items off one by one, like she was taking inventory of a kitchen.

"So what are you going to do?"

"Sichuan pepper they've got, and it's more fragrant than what comes later. *Zhuyu* can stand in for three parts of the heat, ginger for two. No bean paste, but there's fermented black beans. Give me a crock and a month, and I'll ferment a whole crock of paste myself." She paused. "The wok, we'll need a smith to forge. Without a good wok and a good stove, the Master can forget about getting good food out of me."

Shen You looked at her for a long while.

He'd thought he was about to watch a Sichuan master chef fall apart in an era without chili peppers. Instead, she'd already done all the math, right down to a list of what she needed.

"Whatever you say. Get the best wok there is."

"Then we're settled." The woman nodded, as if they'd just closed a deal.

Shen You turned to the young man, who hadn't made a sound the whole time. "And you?"

"*Chupu*, *shuanglu*, *woshuo*, dice. Anything you put money down on, I can play," the young man said lazily.

"And win at all of them?"

"Luck's for people who can't count." He tossed the pebble into the air, caught it, and opened his hand.

His palm was empty.

In his other hand, the pebble lay quietly.

***

"You're Zhou Two." Shen You pointed at the young man, then at the woman. "You're Zhou Three."

"Mm."

"All right."

Zhou One, Zhou Two, Zhou Three. To anyone else, that was just the eldest, second, and third of the Zhou family. Once they were out in the world, Zhou Three would be Zhou Sanniang, and nobody could find a thing wrong with it.

Only Shen You knew the lineup was really a week in disguise: Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday.

He lay back down on the heap of dry straw. Sleep had fled completely, and he found himself a little curious.

"Let me ask you something. Do you know how you came to be?"

"Yes," Zhou Two said. "The Master summoned us."

"Doesn't that seem strange? One moment there's nothing, and the next you're standing in this broken-down temple."

Zhou Two thought it over, the pebble making two circuits between his fingers.

"Nobody in this world remembers how they were born." He yawned. "At least I know."

Zhou Three chimed in, her voice a low rumble: "Don't matter how we come. Long as there's a stove."

Shen You cracked up.

Healthy outlook. Healthier than his. Back when he'd first started his job, he'd spent every day wondering what the point of being alive even was.

***

At first light, as soon as the ward gates opened, Zhou One was ready to head out.

Shen You sat up in a daze, dug the lighter out of his back pocket, and tossed it over. "Take this. You don't have a single coin on you. If you don't make it back before the ward gates close and you get stuck overnight in some other ward, at least you'll have a fire."

"I'll make it back." Even as he said it, Zhou One caught the lighter and tucked it into his sleeve.

Zhou Two trailed out after him, saying he was going to wander around the city.

Getting from Kaiming Ward to the West Market meant crossing the better part of Chang'an.

Out the ward's west gate was Vermilion Bird Avenue. A thin morning mist hung over the street. The yellow dirt roadbed was half soaked with night dew, rut pressed over rut. The street was absurdly wide; standing on one side, you couldn't make out the faces of the people under the ward wall on the other. Rows of pagoda trees lined the drainage ditches on both sides, their branches hung thick with seedpods.

There wasn't a single shop on the street. There were only ward walls on either side, and in the walls, only ward gates. If you wanted to buy something, you went to a market.

Zhou One headed north along Vermilion Bird Avenue. He had only just passed Lanling Ward when the morning bell of Daxingshan Temple carried over the ward wall to his right; to his left, across the avenue, the roofs of the Xuandu Daoist temple's halls showed above the ward wall. One to the east, one to the west, facing each other squarely across the street.

Four more wards to the north, and the south wall of the Imperial City lay across his path. The gate tower of Vermilion Bird Gate soared high, and the people streaming in and out through its passage were all officials and clerks on their way to their bureaus, some on horseback, some on foot, with servants trotting ahead to clear the way.

At Vermilion Bird Gate he turned west and followed the avenue running outside the Imperial City's south wall. To the east it led to Chunming Gate, to the west to Jinguang Gate, the gate through which the camel caravans from the Western Regions entered the city. He hadn't gone far when a string of camels came toward him, bells clanking, the Hu drover leading them in a peaked felt hat and calling out in a language Zhou One couldn't follow.

The West Market lay on the south side of this avenue.

The West Market didn't open until the Hour of the Horse. Zhou One had come early. On the open ground outside the market gate rested a caravan that had come in through Jinguang Gate just that morning, camels lying all over the ground and drovers gathered around a copper pot, waiting for the water to boil.

One drover was crouched under the pot, trying to strike a fire. He scraped the fire steel against the flint, stroke after stroke. Sparks fell into the tinder, sent up a thread of blue smoke, and died. The night dew had soaked the tinder. He swore in his own tongue, dug a wad of dry tinder out of his robe, and scraped another seven or eight times before he finally cupped a speck of red in his hands and brought it to his mouth to blow on. His cheeks puffed and hollowed, puffed and hollowed, until at last the dried camel dung under the pot caught, grudgingly.

All told, the better part of half a stick of incense.

Zhou One watched the whole thing from the side. In his sleeve, his fingers brushed the little green object.

Roll, press, and there was fire.

He didn't take it out.

Sold to a drover, it was a thing for making fire. For making fire there was the fire steel, one coin a set, and no matter how much less trouble a firestarter was, it couldn't be worth much more. But no one in Chang'an had this thing. No one in the Western Regions had it either. Nobody had ever seen one. And a thing nobody had ever seen shouldn't be judged on whether it was useful. It should be judged on how rare it was.

The Master had said that once the fuel inside burned out, it was just an empty shell. Trading something that would be an empty shell sooner or later for a roomful of things they actually needed right now—the Master wouldn't begrudge that.

Rare meant it needed a provenance. The real provenance of anything that came from the Master could never be told, so he'd fit it with a different one.

Zhou One squatted down beside the caravan and worked at it, inventing and then picking at the details. Persia was too close; plenty of the Sogdians in the West Market had been there. As for anything from India, the monks at Daxingshan Temple would recognize it. It had to be somewhere everyone had heard of and no one had been.

Fulin.

The people of Fulin wrote sideways, from left to right, with an alphabet of twenty-four letters. That much he knew. The craftsman had to be old, the pieces few, and best of all, this would be "the last one." With the last one, no one could beat the price down.

Only once the story was settled did he prick up his ears and listen to the drovers chatting with the brokers: which Hu merchant had the sharpest eye, which one regularly delivered goods to the households of great men.

The thing had to be sold to someone who knew what it was. Better yet, to someone who could sell it on for even more.

The drum sounded, the market gates swung wide, and the crowd waiting outside surged in all at once.

Inside, four main streets crossed like a tic-tac-toe board, and shops in the same trade sat side by side: a row of silk dealers here, a row of medicine sellers there, and beyond them the saddle-and-bridle sellers and the sesame flatbread stalls. The air was a mix of spices, livestock, and the toasty smell of baking flatbread. The jewelry shops were mostly in the northwest corner, and most of their owners were Hu.

Zhou One walked straight into one of them.

The shopfront was small, with a string of glass beads hanging at the door. The owner was a Sogdian Hu with a curly, bristling beard and a pair of green eyes that glittered with shrewdness. He was flicking counting rods with one hand while scolding a skinny young Hu servant boy in his own tongue. The boy kept his head down, his ears bright red.

Only when he saw a customer come in did the merchant switch to Chinese. He looked up and took in Zhou One's half-worn cloth robe with a single sweep of his eyes, and didn't get up.

"Do you buy rare things?"

"Depends on how rare."

Zhou One set the lighter on the counter and pressed down.

*Click.*

A flame shot up.

The merchant's fingers paused on the counting rods for an instant. Only an instant. He picked the thing up and pressed it a dozen-odd times, and it lit every single time. Then he turned it over and held it to the light. Through the green shell, a little bit of something like clear water sloshed around inside.

"There's oil in it?"

"Fire oil. When it's used up, it's gone. Good for three to five thousand lights."

The merchant set the lighter down and looked at Zhou One. "Where's it from?"

"The kingdom of Fulin." Zhou One was unhurried. "In his younger years my master traveled west with a merchant caravan. He got this from an old craftsman in the capital of Fulin. In his whole life, the craftsman only ever succeeded in making three. One went into the royal palace of Fulin. One went into the ground with him. This is the last one."

The merchant heard him out and smiled.

"When the people of Fulin write, do they write sideways or up and down?"

"Sideways. From left to right, with an alphabet of twenty-four letters." Zhou One didn't so much as lift his eyelids. "If the proprietor would like to hear it, I can recite a couple of lines in the Fulin tongue."

"No need." The merchant waved a hand. "I've been in the West Market twenty years. I've heard more provenances than I've sold goods. Real or fake, I don't care."

He pushed the lighter back to the middle of the counter.

"I care about two things only. Whether you have more of these. And who your master is."

"The first: perhaps. The second: my master doesn't care to receive visitors."

"Then we'll talk about the first." The merchant didn't press. "Sell this to a West Market trader and it won't fetch much. Traders only care what a thing is good for, and for making fire there's the fire steel, one coin a set. But send it through the right door, and it stops being a thing for making fire."

He held up five fingers. "Five strings."

"The proprietor just said that sent through the right door, it stops being a thing for making fire," Zhou One said. "Then what it's worth depends on how big that door is."

The merchant blinked, then burst out laughing. When he was done laughing, there was a good deal more seriousness in his eyes.

"Ten strings."

Zhou One reached for the lighter. "The proprietor isn't the only one in Chang'an who knows good goods."

"One gold ingot, plus two strings." The merchant laid his hand over Zhou One's. "And one more condition. In future, whenever your master has something like this, bring it to me for a look first. I don't ask that you sell only to me. Only that I get the first look."

Zhou One thought it over.

Not exclusive, just the first look. The price and the condition were both fair.

"Done."

"Kang's shop, West Market. The name's Kang Satuo." He put the lighter away and cupped his hands in salute. "There's no one in this West Market who doesn't know me."

***

Zhou One was less than half a li from Kang's shop when he knew he'd picked up a tail.

A skinny Hu boy, keeping twenty-odd paces back, never closer, never farther.

Zhou One didn't look back. He went to a clothing shop first and bought two round-collared robes, then went into a *futou* shop, picked out two, and took his time trying them on in the shop. When he was done, he went out the back door, turned into a narrow alley, changed into a new robe, put on a *futou*, rolled his old cloth robe up in a bundle, and came out the other end of the alley into the crowds of the market.

The boy waited at the door of the *futou* shop for as long as it takes a stick of incense to burn. When he finally went in, there was no one inside but the owner, dozing.

***

The sun was sinking westward when Zhou One got back to the ruined temple. Zhou Two had beaten him back and was squatting on the steps at the temple door, drawing something on the ground with his pebble.

Slung over Zhou One's shoulder was the string of coins left over from his shopping, heavy enough to pull his robe crooked. Tucked in his robe was the small gold ingot. In his hands he carried clothes, *futou*, boots, paper, ink, and brushes, a set of carving knives, a block of pearwood, a clay pot, a fire steel and flint, a stack of sesame flatbreads still steaming hot, plus a big slab of mutton, a packet of Sichuan pepper, and a few knobs of old ginger.

"You even bought a fire steel?"

"The Master's lighter. I took it upon myself to sell it," Zhou One said. "From now on, making fire takes one of these."

"Fair enough."

Zhou One told him everything that had happened at the West Market, from start to finish, and added at the end, "Kang Satuo sent someone to follow me. He followed me for half a li. I shook him."

"He wants to know where the thing came from."

"It isn't the thing he wants to know about. It's the person," Zhou One said. "A master who can produce a treasure from Fulin. He wants to see whether that master is worth befriending."

Shen You wasn't angry. If anything, he laughed.

An opening price with some sense of proportion, concessions with conditions attached, and after the deal was done, he still knew to send someone to sniff out the seller.

"This Kang Satuo is a smart man," he said. "Smart men are easy to deal with. From now on, when we have something, show it to him first."

He changed into the round-collared robe, put on the *futou* so it covered his buzz cut completely, and went to the puddle behind the temple to take a look at himself.

The man in the water had clean, fine features, wore the new undyed hemp robe, and had the two tails of his *futou* hanging down behind his head. He actually looked the part.

"Mr. Shen," he said, cupping his hands at his reflection, "welcome to the Great Tang."

***

Zhou Three had already struck a fire with the fire steel. The clay pot went on, the mutton went in, and handful after handful of Sichuan pepper and old ginger went in after it.

Half a double-hour later, the smell had filled the whole ruined temple.

Shen You held a bowl of Sichuan pepper mutton soup and ate it with sesame flatbread, never once lifting his head. The mutton had been stewed till it fell apart, the numbing tingle of the pepper crept up into his nose layer after layer, and the broth was so savory he nearly bit his own tongue.

One bowl down, his forehead broke into a sweat, and his whole body felt cleared out and light.

He set down the bowl. "Sanniang, this cooking..."

"Three parts in ten." Zhou Three was collecting the bowls and didn't look up. "Clay pot, firewood, no paste. Wait till the wok's done, then the Master can praise me."

Shen You said nothing more and ladled himself another bowl.

It was the most satisfying meal he'd had in seven years.

***

Fed and content, Zhou Two still hadn't said a word. He held the gold ingot, turning it slowly between his fingers.

As he turned it, those eyes of his, which never seemed to be awake, came all the way open for the first time.

"Master, we've got our stake."

"Thinking of hitting the gambling dens?"

"Walked around all day. Scouted five." Zhou Two jerked his chin at the ground by the door. In the firelight it was easy to see: what he'd drawn with his pebble during the day, square after neat square in straight lines, a few of them marked with dots. "Gambling's against the law, so the dens are all tucked out of sight, and every one of them has somebody behind it. When there's trouble, they don't go to the authorities. They settle it themselves."

He paused. "So win too little and it's not enough. Win too much and you don't walk out the door."

"So how are you going to win?"

"Three days, five houses. At each one, win up to the number that hurts them, but not enough to make them go all in."

Shen You's interest was piqued. "And how much does that come to?"

Zhou Two clapped the gold ingot into his palm and grinned.

"How much does the Master want?"

***

At that same moment, the West Market had already closed for the day.

In the back room of Kang's shop, the skinny Hu boy stood with his head bowed. "I lost him."

Kang Satuo didn't scold him.

"Anyone who can shake you inside a *futou* shop is no ordinary servant." He lit an oil lamp and brought out the little green object, cradling it in both hands. "If the servant is like that, the master is something else again."

*Click.*

The flame danced in his green eyes.

The new emperor had just ascended the throne, and the great houses of Chang'an were about to be ranked all over again. Who would rise and who would fall, whose threshold would grow higher and whose lower—it would all come out in the next few months. For a Hu merchant to stand firm in Chang'an, it had never been about the goods. It was about the doors.

At a time like this, to send a lavish gift no one had ever seen through a door that had only just grown larger...

What he didn't know was that this lighter of unknown origin would change hands several more times.

And no one could have guessed whose hands it would finally land in.
