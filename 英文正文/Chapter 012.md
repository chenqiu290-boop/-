# Chapter 12: The Ten-Day Deadline

The twenty-third day of the eighth month. The fifteenth day.

Fifteen had arrived at the Hour of the Rat. A hundred and twenty.

On the second day of martial law, Chang'an felt like a city with a hand clamped around its throat.

By day, every ward opened only half its gate, and anyone going in or out was questioned. Cavalry ran north and west along Vermilion Bird Avenue, troop after troop, and the yellow dust their hooves kicked up hung in the air half the day before it settled. Half the shops in the West Market had shut, and the price of grain rose three times in a single day.

The moment the sun went down, the ward gates were locked tight.

The whole city went dark. Across more than a hundred square blocks, people hardly dared light a lamp. Only the patrols moved through the empty avenues, holding up torches, their armor plates clattering as they went.

***

The second watch.

Cui Shouli rode at the very head of the column.

He wore the *mingguang* armor of the Left Encampment Guard, the two mirror plates on his chest polished to a gleam, a *hengdao* hanging at his waist. Behind him came forty-one men, every one in Encampment Guard leather armor and a red-tasseled helmet, spears on their shoulders and *hengdao* at their hips, marching in two files, their steps perfectly even.

Anyone who saw them would have said this was an Encampment Guard patrol.

Yet not one of them was a soldier of the Left Encampment Guard.

They were old hands from the Eastern Palace Guard's Left Command. On the day of Xuanwu Gate, they had followed the Crown Prince's troops in the assault on the Xuanwu Gate. Afterward, some had surrendered along with Cui Shouli, some had hidden in the Zhongnan Mountains for two months, and some had changed their names and worked as porters in Chang'an, hauling sacks for other people.

Tonight, they had put on armor again.

Cui Shouli had drawn the armor from the Left Encampment Guard's own armory. He was the colonel on duty; yesterday he had drawn forty-one sets of leather armor and forty-one helmets, "to make up the patrols' shortfall." On the first day of martial law, men from every unit in the Guard had stood in a long line outside the armory door to draw armor, and the armory clerk hadn't asked so much as a second question.

"Colonel."

A hoarse voice came from beside his horse, like the caw of an old crow.

Sun Laoya walked at his horse's side—scrawny, dressed all in black, his helmet pulled down so low that only one narrowed eye showed. He was past fifty. Back when Emperor Yang of Sui made his three campaigns against Liaodong, he had crawled out of the heaps of the dead. He was the oldest of the forty-one, and the one Cui Shouli trusted most.

"Too smooth," Sun Laoya said.

"What is?"

"Ward gates open when we call. Not a constable on the streets. Qian Biao said he'd move them off, and he did." Sun Laoya spat. "Old soldier like me, fought my whole life—the smoother the road, the more times you look it over."

Cui Shouli didn't answer.

He was thinking of that jar of white sugar. Of Wei San on his knees before him, telling him about the three breaths, about his own voice coming from behind the screen, about the fake box that matched the real one in every detail.

It wasn't that he didn't know this was a trap.

But the roster was in that man's hands. The names on it were his old brothers-in-arms—men who now wore the official robes of the new court and couldn't sleep at night. If that box ever made its way into Xiande Hall of the Eastern Palace, there would be a bloodletting greater than Xuanwu Gate.

A night of martial law was his only chance.

"We do it as agreed," he said.

***

The gate of Kaiming Ward was opened by Warden Zhao, his hands shaking.

On a night of martial law, when patrol soldiers called at the gate, he didn't dare refuse.

Cui Shouli led his men into the ward. Inside it was even darker than out on the avenue: a single dirt street running east to west, with the ward wall and low courtyard walls on either side. At the house in the northeast corner, two lanterns hung by the gate, glaring in the darkness.

Fifty paces short of the house, Cui Shouli reined in and raised his hand.

Behind him, the torches were snuffed one after another, and the column spread out without a sound.

Liu Jiu took eight crossbowmen and slipped along the foot of the walls on both sides of the street, melting into the side lanes to the left and right of the house. Liu Jiu had one eye. The left was covered with a black cloth; the right was narrowed to a slit, and at night it could put a bolt through a man's throat at fifty paces. He licked his thumb and spanned the foot-drawn crossbow in his hands.

Twenty men split into three groups and circled around to the east and north sides of the house to seal off the back wall and the side gate.

The remaining twelve followed Cui Shouli up to the front gate.

Zhang Heng walked on Cui Shouli's left. He stood eight chi tall, with shoulders as broad as a door panel and a scar slanting from his brow bone down to his jaw. He carried a weighted long blade whose back was twice as thick as an ordinary *hengdao*'s.

Wei San walked on his right. He wore no helmet. His lean face was white in the lantern light, and his eyes were locked on that gate.

Last time, he had gone in over the wall and been pinned to the ground within three breaths.

This time, he was going in through the front gate.

***

Cui Shouli dismounted, raised his hand, and knocked.

"Left Encampment Guard patrol! Searching for Turkic spies! Open up!"

Inside, a single breath of silence.

Then the gate opened.

Not a crack. Both leaves swung inward with a creak, perfectly together, all the way back.

Inside, it was dark. The gatehouse was dark, and the front courtyard was dark too. There was only a single lamp, lit on the platform of the main hall.

Beneath the lamp stood a tall chair. A man sat in it, a moon-white outer robe draped over his shoulders and a wine cup in his hand.

"Colonel Cui," the man said. "Out patrolling the city this late. You work hard."

Cui Shouli looked at him for a while.

So this was Shen You.

Younger than he'd expected. He sat there lazily, like a man taking the cool night air in his own courtyard.

"We are under orders to search." Cui Shouli drew a writ from the breast of his armor and held it out. "Your household is hiding weapons. Your conduct is suspicious. You are suspected of being Turkic spies."

"Who issued the writ?"

"The Left Encampment Guard."

Shen You didn't take it. He only smiled, turned his head, and spoke toward the main hall behind him.

"Senior Colonel Yuwen—a writ from your Guard."

The door curtain of the main hall lifted, and a man stepped out.

He was in his forties, not tall, solidly built, in a deep crimson robe of the sort worn at home, and he was still holding half a cup of Shaochun he hadn't finished. He walked to the edge of the platform and, by the light of that lamp, looked out through the gate.

All at once, Cui Shouli's face went white.

Senior Colonel of the Left Encampment Guard, Yuwen Yan.

His superior.

"Colonel Cui." Yuwen Yan didn't raise his voice, but it landed like a stone dropped on the ground. "This Guard issued no writ tonight to search any private home."

His gaze swept over the twelve men behind Cui Shouli, going from face to face.

"These men behind you," he said. "Not one of them is a soldier of my Left Encampment Guard."

Two more people stepped out to stand at the door of the main hall. One was Commandant Lu in a blue robe, his face pale, a brush clutched in his hand. The other was Dou the Eleventh in a purple-crimson robe, leaning against the door frame, his eyes alarmingly bright.

Inside the gate and out, dead silence.

***

Cui Shouli stood in the gateway and didn't move.

He was no fool.

Forging a writ. Misusing Guard armor. Bringing in former Eastern Palace men on the sly. Forcing his way into a private home on a night of martial law. Any one of them was a capital crime.

His superior was standing on the platform. The County Commandant was standing in the doorway, brush in hand.

Retreat, and he was a dead man.

Hold his ground, and he was a dead man too.

But if he held his ground, he could at least kill everyone in this courtyard, burn this house down, and report at daybreak: *Turkic spies ran amok; the Encampment Guard fought hard; all perished together.* A senior colonel, a county commandant, a concubine's son of the Dou family—all dead at the hands of Turkic spies.

And the roster would burn right along with them.

Slowly, Cui Shouli raised his head and looked at Yuwen Yan on the platform.

"Senior Colonel Yuwen," he said, "you should not have come."

Yuwen Yan's brow twitched.

Cui Shouli's thumb brushed once across the guard of his blade.

Then he drew.

"Crow!"

***

The word "Crow" had barely left his mouth when Shen You, up on the platform, tapped one finger lightly on the arm of his chair.

Under the eaves on all four sides of the front courtyard, twenty-four black cloths dropped at once.

Beneath the black cloths were twenty-four glass lamps.

Light splashed across the whole front courtyard like a basin of water.

The twelve men behind Cui Shouli had snuffed their torches and then stood facing that pitch-black gateway for a good while; their eyes had long since adjusted to the dark. When the lamps blazed up, ten of the twelve threw up a hand to shield their eyes without thinking, their vision swimming with white spots.

It lasted only a breath.

In that breath, the gate slammed shut behind them with a *boom*.

Two men slipped out of the shadows of the gatehouse, one to each leaf, and shoved the gate closed. A bar as thick as a bowl's rim crashed down into its brackets with a *clang*.

The men outside couldn't get in.

The men inside couldn't get out.

The doors of the east and west wing rooms opened at the same moment.

People streamed out of them without a sound, squad after squad, and formed two arcs.

***

Zhang Heng was the first to charge.

The spots in his eyes hadn't cleared, but he didn't need to see. He had been a famous brute in the Eastern Palace Guard; outside the Xuanwu Gate, he had held off three horsemen of the Prince of Qin's household all by himself. He fixed on the lamp on the platform, on the man sitting in that chair, and charged with his long blade in hand.

A man eight chi tall, in armor, charging like a bull.

From the shadows beside the steps, a man even taller and broader came forward to meet him.

Wei One.

Zhang Heng swung his blade from high overhead, straight down. He had made this cut hundreds of times. It had split leather armor, split shields, split men.

Wei One didn't back away.

He stepped forward.

That one step carried him under the edge of the blade and inside Zhang Heng's reach, into the one place that long blade couldn't touch. The blade cut empty air behind his back and struck the gray bricks of the steps in a spray of sparks.

Wei One's shoulder drove into Zhang Heng's chest, and his two arms, like a pair of iron hoops, scooped Zhang Heng around the waist from beneath his armor skirt.

"Pardon me," he said quietly.

Then he pulled Zhang Heng clean off the ground.

Eight chi of man, armor and all, two hundred jin at the very least, feet leaving the earth—Wei One hoisted him onto his shoulder, swung him half around, and slammed him down over his back.

*Bam!*

The armor plates hit the bricks like a basket of ironware crashing into a stone mortar. The back of Zhang Heng's head struck the edge of a step; his helmet went flying; he bounced once on the ground, and the long blade fell from his hand.

Teeth gritted, he tried to roll over. Wei One dropped a knee onto his chest, caught his right wrist, and wrenched it outward.

*Crack.*

Zhang Heng let out a muffled roar and lay still.

***

Wei San didn't rush the platform.

He went around.

The instant the lamps blazed, he had shut his eyes; he made his living by night and knew how to dodge the light. He slid along the foot of the west wing's wall like a cat, a short knife held in a reverse grip, his target the County Commandant standing at the door of the main hall with a brush in his hand.

Take the Commandant, and he'd have a hostage.

He was still five paces from the steps of the main hall when a man standing on them blocked his way.

The man was a little past thirty, thin and pale, dressed in a plain gray cloth robe. He looked like a shop's accountant. A *hengdao* hung at his waist, still in its scabbard.

Wei San didn't so much as slow down. His knife stabbed straight for the man's belly.

The man's left hand moved.

The sound of the blade leaving its scabbard was very soft, like a sheet of paper tearing.

All Wei San saw was a flash of white. His wrist went numb, and his knife was already flying; it stuck fast in a pillar with a *thock*. What had struck his wrist was the back of the blade.

He was quick. His right hand dove into his boot and came out with a second knife.

It was only half drawn when the man's hilt drove in under his ribs.

Not hard. Right into the gap between two plates of armor.

Wei San's mouth fell open. He couldn't get a breath. He doubled over and went down on his knees.

The man drew back his blade and wiped it twice on his sleeve.

There was nothing on it.

He still wiped it twice before he slid it back into the scabbard.

Chen Five.

***

Sun Laoya didn't charge either.

The moment the lamps lit, he knew it was over.

The front courtyard was a sack. Once the gate shut, its mouth was tied off. The people who'd come out of the wing rooms—their positions, their footwork, their spacing—not one was out of place. These weren't house guards. These were soldiers.

When an old soldier fights, the most important thing isn't winning. It's living.

Without a word, he backed away to the east. Between the east wing and the main hall stood a moon gate that led to the back courtyard. The back courtyard was the family's inner quarters, and in the inner quarters there would be women—there would be the things the master couldn't afford to lose.

Take them from behind.

Hugging the eaves of the east wing, he reached the moon gate in a dozen-odd steps.

Inside the moon gate, a man was standing.

Not tall, very broad in the shoulders, both hands hanging at his sides, the backs of them thick with calluses. He stood there like a post.

"Old soldiers love going around the back," the man said. "Been waiting on you a while."

Sun Laoya didn't waste words. His left hand flicked up, and a fistful of white powder flew straight at the man's face. It was something he'd learned in Liaodong: lime. Blind a man, and he's half finished.

Zhou Four closed his eyes.

He didn't back away. He took a step forward, and in the same instant the lime hit his face, his right hand closed precisely on the wrist that had thrown it.

No looking. By feel.

Grip. Twist. Drive upward.

*Crack.*

Sun Laoya's left shoulder came out of its socket.

The pain turned the old soldier's vision black, and a hoarse shriek like an old crow's squeezed out of his throat—yet in the same moment his right hand flipped a short dagger out of his sleeve and slashed it hard across Zhou Four's forearm.

Blood welled up at once.

Zhou Four didn't so much as frown. He drove his left knee into Sun Laoya's belly, and as the man doubled over, his right hand wound along Sun Laoya's arm, wrapped it, bore down, and pinned him bodily across the threshold of the moon gate, a knee on the back of his neck.

With his face pressed to the cold stone, Sun Laoya dragged in a few ragged breaths—and actually let out a laugh.

"...You're good."

Zhou Four opened his eyes. Lime sifted down from his lashes. There was a gash two inches long on his forearm, and blood ran down to drip from his fingers.

He didn't spare it a glance.

***

The remaining nine didn't break.

They were veterans of the Eastern Palace Guard; they had seen blood outside the Xuanwu Gate. Zhang Heng was down, Wei San was down, Sun Laoya was pinned, and not one of them turned to run.

Back to back, blades pointed outward, the nine formed a small circle in the blink of an eye.

"Net."

Off to one side of the platform, a square-jawed man said one word.

His name was Chu One, a grandmaster of battle formations. The house guards were organized in five squads; in ordinary times they answered to Zhou Four. When it came to a fight, they took their orders from him.

Up in the rafters of the gatehouse, someone yanked a rope.

A huge net dropped from above, its cords weighted with lead, and came down over the little circle.

The nine hacked at it. The cords were hemp soaked in tung oil; one cut didn't sever them, and two cuts parted only a single strand.

In the few breaths they spent hacking at the net, a dark, burly man came in from the side, planting a staff as he walked.

Chu Four.

Ordinarily he was always laughing. Right now there wasn't a trace of a smile on his face.

The staff flicked.

*Whack!* One man took a blow to the wrist, and his blade hit the ground.

*Whack!* Another took one behind the knee and dropped to one knee.

*Whack! Whack! Whack!*

One blow per wrist, one per knee, one per ankle. He picked only the places armor couldn't cover; every blow landed on bone, and not one landed anywhere that could kill.

Two men hacked through the net, squeezed out through the hole, and lunged at Chu Four's back with their blades raised.

On a roof, a bowstring sang twice.

Two arrows: one buried itself in one man's thigh, the second in the other man's thigh, both a palm's width above the knee, just below the armor skirt. The two went down at the same time and rolled on the ground clutching their legs.

On the ridge of the gatehouse roof, Zheng Six lowered his bow, blew out a soft breath, and felt in his quiver with his fingers, counting how many he had left.

Legs, not bodies.

The Master had said he wanted them alive.

Under the net, two men were still struggling.

Chu Four turned around. One blow. Another.

They stopped struggling.

***

In the courtyard, only one man was still on his feet.

Cui Shouli.

He hadn't moved from start to finish. He stood three paces inside the gate, *hengdao* in hand, and watched his men go down one after another.

Zhang Heng. Wei San. Sun Laoya. Nine old brothers.

In under thirty breaths.

He had fought for half his life. He had fought under Dou Jiande, fought under the Crown Prince, and outside the Xuanwu Gate, not even the Prince of Qin's men had beaten him this fast.

He drew a slow breath, lifted his blade, and walked, step by step, toward the steps of the main hall.

To catch the bandits, catch their king first.

On the platform, Shen You was still sitting in that chair, the wine cup still in his hand.

Chen Five came around from the side of the steps to meet him, putting himself between Cui Shouli and Shen You.

Cui Shouli didn't stop. His blade was already coming down.

It was a battlefield cut, wide and sweeping; one stroke like that could cleave a man and his horse together. Chen Five turned aside and let it pass, and the back of his blade knocked against Cui Shouli's wrist.

Cui Shouli's blade was knocked off line, but he rode the force of the knock into a turn, passed the blade to his left hand, and swept a backhand cut at Chen Five's waist.

He could fight with a blade in either hand. It was a skill learned among the heaps of the dead.

Chen Five gave half a step. The edge brushed past the front of his robe and slit it open.

Then Chen Five stepped in.

A very small step.

The tip of his blade stopped in front of Cui Shouli's throat.

Less than a hair's breadth from the skin.

Cui Shouli froze.

He could feel the chill of that point resting against his Adam's apple. One push forward—or one lean forward on his own part—and there would be a hole.

He didn't move.

Neither did Chen Five.

Only then, up on the platform, did Shen You set down his wine cup.

"Colonel Cui," he said, "put the blade down. Things outside are about wrapped up too."

***

Things outside were, in fact, about wrapped up.

The moment the gate shut, the men outside knew something had gone wrong within.

Liu Jiu, in the side lane, was the first to react. He and his eight crossbowmen leaned out from the mouths of the lanes on either side, crossbows trained on the top of the house's wall.

Sure enough, there were figures on the wall.

Liu Jiu pulled the trigger.

The bolt struck a sheet of wet felt propped along the top of the wall—*thup*—and sank only halfway in.

They weren't people. It was a row of wooden boards and wet felt set up along the top of the wall, and behind the boards, shadowy figures stood.

"They're ready for us on the wall!" Liu Jiu growled under his breath, planted a foot on the stock, and hauled the string back again. "Go around to the side gate—"

Before he could finish, a muffled grunt came from the lane behind him.

He whipped around.

Deep in the lane, the side gate of the newly bought compound on the east side had opened at some point. Fifteen people filed out of it, hugged the walls, and crept up behind the crossbowmen without a sound. The one leading them was a lean, long-faced man: Feng Five.

One of Liu Jiu's crossbowmen had a hand clamped over his mouth from behind, took a knife-hand chop to the back of the neck, and went limp.

Liu Jiu's one eye narrowed. He spun and loosed.

The bolt punched through a man's shoulder. The man grunted and swayed, but somehow stayed on his feet, gritted his teeth, and kept coming.

Liu Jiu planted a foot on the stock, hauled back the string, licked his thumb, and reached for a third bolt.

His fingers had just touched the shaft when, from the roof of the gatehouse, came a *thrum*.

A short little bolt shot down at a slant from up high and pierced clean through his right forearm, the arm that held the crossbow.

Liu Jiu's crossbow fell to the ground.

He lifted his one eye toward the roof.

Crouched on the ridge was a small, wiry man, holding a little crossbow of a kind Liu Jiu had never seen before, with a row of bronze teeth set into its stock.

Feng Six.

Crossbowman against crossbowman.

Liu Jiu stared at that crossbow for a long moment. Then, all at once, he laughed and sat down with his back against the wall.

***

The twenty who had circled around to the east and north were, just then, ramming the side gate with a threshold beam they'd pried loose somewhere in the ward.

*Boom!*

*Boom!*

The side gate shuddered twice.

Just then, a voice rang out from the top of the wall.

Slow and heavy, with a touch of a Hebei accent, every word sounding as if it had been pressed flat in the mouth before it came out.

"Fall back!"

"Fall back to the ward gate! It is an ambush in there!"

The twenty men at the gate stopped dead.

That was the Colonel's voice.

They had followed Cui Shouli for years. They could have picked that voice out with their eyes shut.

For one breath the twenty hesitated. Some dropped the beam; some looked back. A few remembered Wei San saying someone in that house could do the Colonel's voice. But inside the gate everything had gone quiet, and the crossbows in the side lanes had fallen silent too. Real or fake, falling back couldn't be wrong.

From the top of the wall, again: "Fall back!"

They fell back.

Twenty Eastern Palace veterans ran down the ward's dirt street toward the ward gate.

On top of the wall, Zhou Seven poked his head out from behind the boards, watched their backs, and grinned.

The twenty reached the ward gate and ran straight into a row of torches.

The ward gate had long since been locked. In front of it stood thirty-odd constables holding iron truncheons and coils of rope. The one at the very front was tall and long-faced, with a scar at the left corner of his mouth that dragged it downward.

Qian Biao.

Cui Shouli had told him to move the constables off, and he had—into Warden Zhao's house beside the ward gate. The moment Cui Shouli's men were through the ward gate, he'd brought his own men out and locked it.

Wherever Shen You told him to move them, that was where he moved them.

"Drop the blades," Qian Biao said. "The Commandant's inside, watching."

The twenty looked back.

At some point, the dirt street behind them had filled with people too. Not a squad—a whole mass. On the walls, on the roof ridges, at the mouths of the lanes, shadowy and indistinct: heads everywhere, seventy or eighty at the very least. Feng Seven stood at the very front. She clapped twice, and the few dozen in the middle of the street took one step forward, all together.

The sound of their feet was like a muffled clap of thunder.

The twenty Eastern Palace veterans looked at the torches ahead of them, then at the black wall of people behind them, and the blades in their hands sank, one after another.

If they could have seen clearly, they would have found that those seventy or eighty were really only sixty or so.

The ones who could actually fight were Feng Five's dozen-odd, fresh from dealing with the crossbowmen in the side lanes, plus a few who'd come down off the walls. Most of the other forty-odd had been called back that afternoon from Baoning Ward next door: people who boiled sugar at the sugar works, who tended the fires at the winery, who wove brocade at the weaving workshop—plus the ones who chopped vegetables in the house's own kitchen. What they held were rolling pins, fire tongs, shoulder poles.

Since the night of Mid-Autumn, Feng Seven had been hauling people out every day before dawn to practice their steps. The ones living in the house practiced in the back courtyard; for the ones who'd moved to Baoning Ward, she went over every other day and kept them at it in the yard of the sugar works. She hadn't taught them anything else. Just one thing: stepping out together.

But the Eastern Palace veterans understood that sound. A few dozen people making a single footstep—only drilled soldiers could march like that.

They understood it. So they put down their blades.

Feng Seven brushed her hands together and turned to whisper to the crowd clutching rolling pins behind her, "See that? Stand in step, and you're soldiers."

***

In the front courtyard, Cui Shouli heard it.

He heard his own voice from the top of the wall, heard the commotion over by the ward gate, heard that one footstep like muffled thunder.

He closed his eyes for a moment.

Then he opened his hand.

The *hengdao* fell to the gray bricks with a *clang*.

Chen Five lowered his blade, stepped back a pace, wiped it twice on his sleeve, and slid it back into its scabbard.

***

Yuwen Yan had stood at the edge of the platform the whole time. The half cup of Shaochun in his hand—he hadn't taken a single sip of it, start to finish.

When the Dou family sent word inviting him, the message had carried only one line: someone in the Encampment Guard meant to use martial law as cover to settle some private business in the south of the city. He had come for that line, not for the liquor.

He looked over the courtyard full of people: Sun Laoya with his shoulder out of its socket; Zhang Heng, slammed down too hard to get up; the two rolling on the ground clutching their legs; the seven sprawled every which way under the net, wrists and knees swollen up like steamed buns. And that ring of people standing in perfect order, not even their breathing ragged.

From the moment the lamps lit to the moment Cui Shouli dropped his blade, he had counted it out in his head: less than the time it takes to drink a cup of tea.

Yuwen Yan had followed the Retired Emperor since the year the army rose at Taiyuan. He had fought at Huoyi, fought at Chang'an, fought Xue Ju, Liu Wuzhou, and Wang Shichong. He had seen more soldiers than there were bricks in this courtyard.

He slowly turned his head and looked at Shen You.

"These men," he said. "They're your house guards?"

"They are."

Yuwen Yan was silent for a long time.

"I have led troops for ten years," he said. "Put any man in this courtyard at the front of a battle line, and he could serve as a squad leader."

"The one who threw that man—in my Guard, he could command a company. The one with the staff, the one with the blade..."

He didn't go on. He just looked at the thin man wiping his blade on his sleeve, and shook his head.

"The most frightening one was the man standing at the moon gate," he said. "Eyes shut, and he put an old soldier's arm out of its socket—an old soldier throwing lime at him."

Dou the Eleventh leaned against the door frame, his mouth hanging open for a long while. His gaze darted all over the courtyard, his lips moving soundlessly, as if he were counting.

After a while, he stopped counting.

Commandant Lu's face was still white. When Dou the Eleventh invited him, he had made himself perfectly clear: someone meant to use the Guard's name to break into a private home at night, and he was asking the County Commandant to come as a witness. It was happening on Wannian County ground; if the Commandant didn't come, he'd never be clear of it afterward. But Commandant Lu had never imagined this was what he would be witnessing.

The brush in his hand hadn't stopped moving. On a sheet of paper, item by item, he wrote: *misusing Guard armor; forging a writ; a night of martial law; entering another's home at night without cause; bearing blades; intent to kill...*

When he had finished, he raised his head and looked at Shen You, his expression complicated.

"Young Master Shen," he said, "that day at the county office, you told me you copied sutras at a hermitage."

"I did."

"At this hermitage," Commandant Lu said, "what sutras were you copying?"

Shen You laughed.

"Books on war," he said.

***

Cui Shouli knelt before the steps, his hands bound behind his back.

His helmet had been taken off, and the white at his temples stood out sharply under the lamps. He raised his head and looked at Shen You in the chair for a long time.

"What are you, really?"

"A man who grows millet south of the city."

Cui Shouli was taken aback for a moment. Then he gave a bitter laugh and asked nothing more.

Shen You rose from his chair, came down the steps, and crouched in front of him. He kept his voice so low that only the two of them could hear.

"The box—I won't hand it over, and I won't burn it," he said. "I'm keeping it. Nobody gets to use it to kill."

Something moved in Cui Shouli's eyes.

"Including me?"

"Including you."

The two of them looked at each other for a long time.

Slowly, Cui Shouli lowered his head.

Yuwen Yan came over and looked down at him. "Cui Shouli, why did you come here tonight?"

Cui Shouli raised his head. There was no expression on his face at all.

"The Shen house has a sugar works and a winery. Gold pours in by the dou every day." He spaced out every word. "I am greedy for money."

Yuwen Yan stared at him for a long time, as if trying to read something in that face.

But there was nothing in that face at all.

***

Yuwen Yan asked nothing more.

He called over the two guardsmen he had brought with him. One he sent to the ward gate to have Warden Zhao unlock it, then ride back to the Imperial City for troops to escort the prisoners; the other he kept here to guard Cui Shouli.

Commandant Lu borrowed the west wing, lit lamps, and had the prisoners brought in one at a time to take their statements. When he got to Liu Jiu, he glanced at the short bronze-tailed bolt in Liu Jiu's forearm; the tip of his brush paused, and he didn't write it down. Qian Biao's constables searched the men in the courtyard, and what they turned up—short knives, daggers, packets of lime, crossbow bolts—lay in one long row across the gray bricks.

On the Shen side, three were hurt: Zhou Four, with a two-inch gash on his forearm; one of Feng Five's men, with a bolt from Liu Jiu through the shoulder; and one more whose scalp had been grazed by a bolt up on the wall.

Zhou Six lit a row of lamps in the main hall and stitched up the three of them one by one. Zhou Four sat there with his arm held out, watching Zhou Six put in stitch after stitch, without a flicker of expression.

"Does it hurt?" Zhou Six asked.

"No."

Zhou Six pushed the next stitch in a little harder, on purpose.

Zhou Four's eyebrow twitched.

Zhou Six kept her head down, the corners of her mouth curving up just slightly.

***

When the Hour of the Rat was nearly upon them, Shen You told Dou the Eleventh he was going to change his clothes, went back alone to the sleeping hall in the back courtyard, and shut the door.

The moment the Hour of the Rat arrived, there were sixteen more people in the room.

A hundred and thirty-six.

The sixteen stood beneath the lamp, men and women both, quietly watching him.

Zhou One was already waiting outside the door.

"We've got guests tonight," Shen You said. "Zhou One will take you to the east compound. Eat, sleep. Until daybreak, stay out of the front courtyard."

He went back to the front courtyard and sat down in that chair again.

Dou the Eleventh shot him a sidelong look. "That's a long time to change your clothes."

"Counted heads while I was at it," Shen You said.

Dou the Eleventh didn't get it, and didn't ask.

***

At the fourth watch, a hundred soldiers of the Left Encampment Guard arrived.

Of the forty-two former Eastern Palace soldiers, two were dead. One had tried to climb over the wall at the ward gate, fallen, and broken his neck on a stone; the other, in the side lane, had been hit by one of his own side's bolts, loosed in the confusion. The other forty were all taken alive, a dozen-odd of them wounded.

Yuwen Yan wrote out an urgent dispatch on the spot and had a guardsman carry it by fast horse to the Eastern Palace. He himself escorted the forty out through the ward gate just before day broke.

Before he left, he paused at the front gate and looked back at the twenty-four glass lamps, still burning.

"Young Master Shen," he said, "that jar of Shaochun—I'll be back another day to finish it."

Once everyone had gone, Shen You went back to the sleeping hall, collapsed onto the bed, sank into the silk-floss mattress, and let out a long breath.

Outside the window, the edge of the sky was turning pale.

***

At that same moment: the Eastern Palace, Xiande Hall.

The lamps in the hall had burned all night. The desk was piled with nothing but military dispatches—from Jingzhou, from Wugong, from Gaoling.

Li Shimin hadn't slept all night. His eyes were shot through with red, yet still alarmingly bright.

A palace attendant came in holding an urgent dispatch. "Urgent dispatch for His Majesty—from Senior Colonel Yuwen Yan of the Left Encampment Guard."

Li Shimin took it and unfolded it.

A few lines in, his brows slowly rose.

*Colonel Cui Shouli of the Left Encampment Guard forged a writ and misused Guard armor, and led forty-one former Eastern Palace men, on a night of martial law, to break into a private home in Kaiming Ward, Wannian County, with intent to rob and kill. The master of the house, Shen You, is a monk returned to lay life, with a sugar works and a winery south of the city. His house guards captured forty on the spot; two dead. Cui Shouli claims he acted out of greed.*

He read the dispatch twice.

"Kaiming Ward. Shen You."

He said the name aloud once and tapped a finger lightly on the desk.

"The monk who makes Snow Sugar?"

The palace attendant was caught off guard. "...If it please His Majesty, the dispatch says the owner of a sugar works south of the city."

Li Shimin said nothing.

From his sleeve he took out that little green thing, turned it over in his hand, and pressed it.

*Click.*

A flame leaped up, reflected in his bloodshot eyes.

"A man of the old Eastern Palace," he said slowly, "risks having his whole clan wiped out to lead soldiers in the dead of night and rob a monk."

"All for greed?"

He blew out the little flame and set the dispatch aside.

"Once the Turks have pulled back," he said, "I want to meet this monk."
