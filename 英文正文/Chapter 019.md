# Chapter 19: Willow Gully

Early in the Hour of the Rooster, with the sun sinking toward the tableland in the west, smoke rose beside the road.

Not black smoke. It was a grayish white, wisp after wisp, drifting up without any strength to it.

"Burned out," Feng Five said.

It was a village of thirty-some households, ringed by an earthen wall, with a big willow at the village entrance, scorched black down one side. The yard walls were all still standing, but the roofs inside them were gone, the rafters burned to charcoal and jutting slantwise out of the ash. At the east end of the village was the threshing floor. It should have held stack after stack of millet; instead there were rings of black scars, one after another, and the biggest was still smoking.

There were people in the village.

An old man was digging a pit at the edge of the threshing floor, digging very slowly, resting after every spadeful. On the ground beside him lay someone rolled in a straw mat. The mat was too short, and a pair of feet stuck out in old cloth shoes, the soles stitched dense and fine—an old woman. A man squatted at the gate of his own yard clutching a little red cloth shoe, a child's, and didn't move. A half-grown boy sat on the well platform, his arm blistered all over from a burn, scooping water from a cracked earthenware jar and pouring it over the blisters, scoop after scoop. The jar was nearly empty. A half-burned roof beam was jammed slantwise down the mouth of the well, and no water could be drawn.

When they saw this column, they shrank back first. Once they'd made out the Tang dress, they stopped shrinking back—but didn't come over either, just watched.

Shen You dismounted. Zhou Four dismounted with him and stood three paces behind him.

"What's this village called?" Shen You asked the old man digging the pit.

The old man leaned on his spade and looked at him a long time.

"Willow Gully."

"When did they come?"

"Yesterday afternoon," the old man said. "Twenty, thirty riders."

"Yesterday..." Behind them, Zheng One's voice changed all at once.

Yesterday morning, in the middle of the Bian Bridge over the Wei, a white horse had been killed.

"We hid in the gullies up north for ten-odd days," the old man said. "Yesterday around noon, somebody came running over from the bridge shouting, treaty's made, treaty's made, the Turks are leaving. So we came out. Millet in the fields not all cut yet, what's on the threshing floor not threshed. Don't come out, what do we eat all winter?"

He drove the spade into the ground.

"We came out, and they came."

He said it flatly, the way you'd say how much rain there'd been this year. When he'd finished, he added: "Our twenty mu, we got seventeen shi off it. After rent, enough to eat till the wheat harvest."

He glanced at the biggest of the black scars on the threshing floor.

"Enough to eat till the wheat harvest," he said again, pulled out the spade, and went on digging.

On the far side of a yard wall, someone suddenly let out a wail.

It was a woman. She'd dug an iron pot out of a heap of ash, with a crack through the bottom. She clutched the pot and sat down hard in the ash, wailing until her voice split. "The Wangs' pot! I borrowed it—I've got to give it back! The Wangs' eldest girl and second girl, they tied them up and took them! Who am I supposed to give it back to!"

She wailed and pounded the ground with her fist. The ash flew up and hit her full in the face, and tears and snot ran down mixed with ash, streaking her black.

No one went to pull her up.

The man squatting at his yard gate with the red cloth shoe, at the words *tied them up and took them*, buried his head in his knees. His shoulders began to shake—silently at first, then with sound, like an ox that had taken a blade.

Shen You stood there, his fingers pinching a copper coin inside his sleeve.

Pinching it. Not moving.

Zhou Four, three paces behind him, saw that hand go still.

"Zhou Six," Shen You said.

Zhou Six was already there, squatting by the well platform. She took the half-grown boy's arm, looked at it once, took a small porcelain jar from her cloth bag, scooped out a fingerful of salve, and spread it over the blisters layer by layer. The boy sucked in his breath with the pain and gritted his teeth and didn't cry out.

"Ge Four."

"Here."

"Dig a stove. Cook a pot. Cook it for them."

Ge Four said not a word, took two men, and went to dig.

The pot went on, the parched-grain meal went in and was stirred loose, and as the smell rose, a few children hiding behind yard walls poked their heads out.

The old man who'd been digging the pit came over. He looked at the pot and didn't reach for it.

"We don't eat for free."

"Then what'll you trade for it?" Shen You asked.

The old man thought, squatted down, picked up a charred stick, and drew on the ground. "North of here, three gullies. The west one, no way through—dead-ends at a cliff. The middle one, wide—the Turks' horses go along the tableland on top of it. The east one, narrow, lots of bends, and at the bottom there's a spring that doesn't freeze even in winter. Forty years I've grazed sheep. The Turks don't know about it."

Feng Five squatted down beside him and looked at the three lines on the ground. He looked them over once and gave a single nod. He had it.

The old man tossed the stick away, dusted the ash off his hands, and only then went over to the pot and took the bowl Ge Four handed him.

North of the village, Feng Five squatted in the road.

Once he was down, he didn't get up. He pinched up a bit of soil with his fingertips and sniffed it, moved two steps on, pinched up another. The prints in the road were a jumble, hoofprints over hoofprints, and he read down through them layer by layer, like turning the pages of a book.

Shen You came over, Zhou Four with him.

"Thirty-one riders," Feng Five said. "Two horses a man. One ridden, one packed. The packed one's hooves sink deeper than the ridden one's."

He moved forward a step and pointed to a string of prints wedged in among the hoofprints.

"These are people. More barefoot than shod. These little ones"—he traced a circle with his finger around a footprint no longer than half a palm—"are children. Steps all just as short, just as wide. Tied on one rope. When the ones in front walk faster, the ones behind have to run."

"How many?"

"Nineteen," Feng Five said. "Two of them got this far and fell. Got hauled up and kept walking."

He pointed them out to Shen You. In the dirt by the road were two scuffed-open marks, and in one of them, the print of a palm pressed down.

Nobody said anything. Wei One was standing by his horse, a man eight chi tall, and the tears came before anything else. He cried and wiped his nose on his sleeve, wiped and it ran again, until he gave up wiping. He walked to the well platform, bent down, locked both hands around the beam, and with a "*Hah!*" pulled it out of the well and flung it aside. The ground shook.

The half-grown boy stared at him, dumbstruck.

"What're you crying for?" the boy asked. "It's not your family."

Wei One scrubbed his face with his sleeve and said thickly, "It hurts to look at."

The boy had nothing to say to that. He looked down at the blisters all over his arm, looked a while—and then his mouth crumpled, and he was crying too, louder than Wei One, hiccuping as he cried. Wei One squatted down and reached out one hand as big as a palm-leaf fan and patted him on the back. Each pat, the boy hiccuped once.

Chu Four stood at the village entrance leaning on his staff, without a trace of a smile left on his face. Zheng Six leaned against his horse, reaching back into his quiver and touching his arrows one by one; when he'd been through them all, he started over from the first. Zhou Seven squatted under the willow. Ordinarily, at the sight of anyone new, his mouth would be silently trying out a line or two of their voice. Today he kept it shut tight.

Ashina stood with his horse beside the nineteen sets of footprints Feng Five had read, looking at that string heading north. The bone thumb ring was turning on his thumb, faster and faster.

***

When it was nearly dark, they left Willow Gully and went on another ten li north.

As they left, the old man who'd been digging didn't look up. The pit was nearly done. He stuck the spade in the heap of earth, squatted, and began easing the straw mat toward the edge of the pit, an inch at a time, resting after each one. Wei One looked back once, pushed his reins into Chu Four's hand, and ran back. He and the old man each took one end, lifted the mat, and lowered it gently into the pit. The old man opened his mouth to say something. Wei One didn't wait—he turned and ran back, covered in dust. Chu One saw it and said nothing.

Ge Four left that pot of parched-grain gruel for the villagers—pot and all. The woman hugged the Wangs' cracked pot to her chest and wouldn't let go of it. Ge Four unloaded another, new pot from a pack frame and set it at her feet. She didn't even look at it.

The horses went slowly. Shen You rode in the middle of the column. The insides of his thighs were burning again, and he didn't make a sound.

Ashina urged his horse up alongside and rode with him a while, then spoke.

"In Turkic there is a saying," he said. "I put it in Tang speech, it is 'beating the grass for grain.'"

Shen You turned his head. The gauze curtain swung.

"The Turks come south, they bring no grain. One man, two horses, three horses. The horses eat the grass by the road. The men eat the grain by the road," Ashina said. "Coming, they raid, it is to eat. Going back, they raid, it is to take."

"There's a treaty."

"The treaty, the khan makes it with the emperor," Ashina said. "The Tang silk, it goes into the khan's tent, into the shads' tents, into the tegins' tents. The families below—half a month and more they fight, men die, horses are lost. Back in their tents, how much do they get? The little they get, it is not enough for one family through the winter."

The bone thumb ring stopped.

"What is not enough, on the road they take it themselves. No one stops them. The khan, he also does not stop them. If the khan stops them, next time no one comes with him."

"What about on the way here?"

"On the way here, they fear to alarm what is ahead. They do not dare burn much. Going back, they burn, and no one follows," Ashina said. "So going back, they burn harder than coming."

"And people?"

"A person is worth more than a sheep," Ashina said. "Sheep, someone must drive. People walk by themselves. The men herd horses. The women..." He paused. "Tied and taken, they do not come back."

Hooves clopped. Someone up front lit a torch, and soon snuffed it again.

"Thirty-three thousand strings," Shen You said. "A thousand taels of gold. Five thousand taels of silver. What did that buy?"

Ashina thought a while.

"It buys south of the Wei."

Shen You asked nothing more.

There was a sum in his head, the one Zheng Three had laid out in beans: thirty-three thousand strings—if not one coin more came into the household, living on that alone would carry them to the two hundred and fourteenth day. On that day, the household would number twenty-three thousand and five.

What it had bought was south of the Wei. North of the Wei was Willow Gully.

He rode on with the reins clenched in his hand, clenched for a long time. Two li farther on, he let go with one hand, took off the curtained hat, and tucked it under his arm. The wind came from the north and blew on his face—cool, carrying the scorched smell of burned millet stalks—and blew all the way down into his collar.

Zhou Four, three paces behind him, glanced at the color of the sky, then glanced all around, and didn't bring up Zhou One's second condition.

The sun had already gone down.

***

The Hour of the Dog. A dry gully.

It was the west one of the three gullies the old man had drawn. It was some three zhang deep and ran north to south, its walls yellow earth, straight up and down, wild jujube bushes growing along the top. The floor was flat, room for over a hundred horses to lie down, and at its north end was a cliff—no way through. A gully with no way through, travelers didn't enter, and neither did anyone out beating the grass for grain. Feng Five had pointed it out before he left, and Chu One had nodded.

Feng Five hadn't picked the east one with the spring. Where there was a spring, people went for water and animals went to drink. The old man knew about it, and so did every shepherd in the villages around.

On the gully floor, Ge Four dug a pit, then dug a trench off it at a slant, roofed the trench with jujube branches, and covered it with earth. The fire caught in the pit, and the smoke crept out along the trench and surfaced in the grass three zhang away, and the moment it surfaced, it was gone. Shen You climbed up to the rim of the gully to look. In the dark, there was nothing to see.

A pot of soup noodles, scallions sizzled in mutton fat, scalding hot. Shen You squatted at the edge of the pit with his bowl, slurping, burning his mouth. Halfway through, he thought of that pot in Willow Gully and set the bowl down. After a while he picked it up again and finished it, one mouthful at a time.

Zhou Six came over and handed him a small porcelain jar.

"For your legs," she said. "The Master walks just like Cao One now."

Shen You looked down at his legs—spread apart, bowed. Then he looked over at Cao One by the horses. Cao One happened to be walking over just then, bandy-legged, rolling with every step.

The two of them looked at each other.

Shen You burst out laughing—laughed so hard he nearly dropped the jar, laughed till his legs hurt, and the hurting made him laugh more. A few people feeding the horses nearby held it in at first, then couldn't hold it anymore either, and the gully floor filled with snorting, smothered laughter—until one glare from Feng Seven shut it down.

It was the first time anyone had laughed since Willow Gully.

After the laughter, everyone on the gully floor went about their own business. Chu One posted four sentries—two at the mouth of the gully, two on the clifftop—changed every double-hour. The horses were unsaddled, their forelegs hobbled, and tied in a line at the foot of the cliff, chewing on millet stalks cut from the field edges at Willow Gully. Feng Seven's people slept in three rows across the gully, each still gripping the hemp rope—her orders: no letting go, not even asleep. Wei One ate three bowls of soup noodles, his eyes still red. He picked up a fourth, looked at it, and passed it to Chu Four beside him.

Cao One didn't sleep. He sat by the gray mare's forelegs. The gray mare wasn't eating. Her head was turned north, her ears pricking up one moment and drooping the next. Cao One reached up and stroked her neck, humming that wordless tune—hum a while, stop a while, hum again.

Shen You lay on a felt with his head on his saddle, wearing the Hu trousers Kang Satuo had given him, Zhou Six's salve cool on his legs. He lay with his eyes open, looking at the sky. After a while he turned over, then turned back.

He thought of the eyes in the Kaiyuan Gate tunnel, lids half lowered, looking at people as if checking a ledger. *Counting houses. To see how many burned.* She'd said it flatly, the way you'd say you were going out to buy vegetables. Only now, lying in a dry gully ten li north of Willow Gully, did he taste the weight underneath those words: she'd known all along what it looked like north of the Wei, and she was still going to count it herself.

Twelfth Daughter. He said it once more in his head, the corner of his mouth twitching, and slid an arm under his head.

***

Early in the Hour of the Pig, Feng Five came back.

After reading the road at Willow Gully, he'd taken two men and ridden north to feel his way forward, and he didn't come back until it was full dark. When he did, his horse was lathered with sweat. He didn't drink first. First he found Shen You, Chu One, and Ashina—four of them, plus Zhou Four—and they squatted beneath the rim of the gully.

"Fifteen li north, there's a ridge," Feng Five said. "Up on the ridge, this afternoon, there were three riders."

"Turks?"

"Came from the north, stood up on the ridge a while. Their horses trampled one spot flat and dropped two piles of dung—dry outside, still soft inside. Gone less than half a day," Feng Five said. "After standing there, they went back north."

"Doing what?"

"Looking back," Feng Five said.

Ashina nodded once. "*Fuli*," he said. "The camels carry the gold and silk, they walk slow. Yesterday at noon, already they start out first. The main host breaks camp only today at dawn. In one day, it goes half again as far as the camels. One day, two days, it passes them by. The camels also wait for it, they walk slower than usual. After it passes, the main host goes in front, the baggage falls behind, the *fuli* follow the baggage. These days, in front is the tail of the main host, all mixed with the slow baggage. Every so often, they send a few riders back, they stand on high ground, they look south once."

"Look for what?"

"To see if someone follows."

It was quiet beneath the rim for a while.

"There's more," Feng Five said. "Camel tracks, north of the ridge, heading north, a little east. Two toes, round, pressed deep. A finger and a half deeper than an unladen camel."

Nobody asked what they were carrying. Yesterday on the north bank by the Bian Bridge, Feng Five had watched those camels being loaded, one after another.

Chu One broke off a jujube twig and drew on the ground: one horizontal line, one vertical, and a dot at the top of the vertical. He looked at the dot for a while, then tossed the twig away.

He'd been working from Ashina's "fifty, sixty li a day." The camels had set out yesterday at noon; by tonight they should have been eighty or ninety li off, and by day, passing themselves off as a horse train, his people wouldn't run into them. But the camel tracks were just north of a ridge fifteen li away, and the men looking back had been standing on that ridge this afternoon. The camels hadn't gotten nearly that far.

"From tonight," he said, "we sleep by day and move by night."

He didn't say it twice.

"And me?" Shen You asked.

Chu One looked at him. "The Master sleeps by day as well."

"That I'm good at," Shen You said.

The corner of Feng Five's mouth twitched. Ashina lowered his head. Behind them, Zhou Four didn't make a sound, but his shoulders shook once.

***

Shen You wanted to go up on the rim and look.

Zhou Four didn't stop him, and went up with him. Feng Five and Ashina went up too. Yun Five followed on his own, carrying a hide robe, which he draped over Shen You's shoulders.

Above the gully lay a stretch of tableland, flat, and the eye could run out over it dozens of li. Dark. The first day of the ninth month—no moon. The stars in the sky crowded one against the next, thick as if someone had knocked over a whole dou of millet, spread all the way down to the edge of the earth.

"The Northern Dipper's there." Yun Five pointed low in the northwest. "Handle pointing west. Autumn."

He raised his hand a little higher. "People say the Northern Pole doesn't move. The spot that doesn't move is empty. That bright one is still nearly a fist away from it, and goes around it in a small circle every night. Moving by night, you go by that empty spot, not by the star."

Shen You followed his finger and looked a long while, but couldn't find the empty spot—only a sky full of stars.

"As long as you can find it," he said.

"The northern sky," Yun Five said, "I don't miss a single one."

Ashina didn't look at the sky. He looked north.

To the north, along the line where sky met earth, lay a band of red.

No flames were visible. What they saw was the light the flames cast on the underside of the sky, very faint, brighter in one stretch, dimmer in the next, drawn out from west to east, as if someone had smeared a streak of not-quite-dead embers along the edge of the earth.

"Takes twenty or thirty fires to light up that wide," Ashina said.

"How far?"

"Beyond the ridge, forty or fifty li," Feng Five said.

Shen You stood at the edge of the tableland wrapped in the hide robe, looking at that band of red.

What lay beneath that band of red, he couldn't see. Maybe the nineteen people, barefoot, tied on a single rope; maybe not—maybe they'd long since been carried off on some family's horses, farther north still. Things roped onto camels' backs, one camel after another, heavy enough to press the tracks a finger and a half deeper. A herd of horses waiting for their lead mare.

And a band of men who had burned their way along the road home, and would sleep just fine tonight.

The wind came from the north and slapped the hem of the hide robe against his legs—against the rubbed-raw places—and it stung. He didn't move.

"Crossing the Wei counts as leaving the capital," he said.

Zhou Four, three paces behind him: "The Master paid the fine."

Shen You smiled, very briefly.

Ahead lay the tail of the Turkic host.

And the tail had eyes.
